# Summary 22 

Technology that precisely controls macrophage polarization to distinct functional states would 23 deepen our understanding of macrophage biology and enable development of new macrophage 24 cell therapies. Here, we use a synthetic cytokine receptor (SCR) platform with programmable 25 signaling domains to control primary human macrophage polarization. SCRs containing signaling 26 motifs from the interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) or Interleukin-10 (IL-10) receptors mimic key features 27 of pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory polarization, respectively. Random recombination of nine 28 distinct signaling motifs to create new SCR signaling domain generates a diverse landscape of 29 synthetic macrophage states with varied expression of inflammatory markers (CD80, CD40) and 30 anti-inflammatory markers (CD163, CD206), and varied phagocytic capacity. SCRs programmed 31 with multiple YLxQ motifs increase macrophage phagocytosis of E. coli and chimeric antigen 32 receptor (CAR)-macrophage phagocytosis of cancer cells in mice, reducing tumor burden by 30-33 fold. The motif-dependent polarization is well-described by a two-state model, enabling 34 quantitative prediction of macrophage polarization state from SCR signaling domain composition. 35 Leveraging this model, we design an SCR that simultaneously enhances phagocytosis and 36 maintains a macrophage pro-inflammatory state. Together, these findings establish a framework 37 to synthetically program macrophage polarization states with potential applications in cancer 38 immunotherapy and other disease contexts. 39 40

# Introduction 43 

Macrophages are highly plastic cells that play critical roles in natural immunity and, increasingly, 44 as engineered cell therapies 1. Macrophages sense infection and tissue damage, phagocytose 45 pathogens and debris, and sculpt the local environment through a broad repertoire of cytokines 46 and chemokines 2. Central to these roles is the ability of macrophages to polarize into varied cell 47 states with unique functional properties in response to external cues. For example, interferon-48 gamma (IFN-γ) can polarize macrophages into a state characterized by display of costimulatory 49 molecules and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines 3. Conversely, interleukin (IL)-10 can 50 polarize macrophages to a state characterized by secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines and 51 promotion of tissue repair 4. Evidence indicates that macrophages can integrate signals from 52 multiple stimuli to adopt a broad spectrum of phenotypic states 5–7 . This plasticity enables 53 macrophages to perform a variety of essential functions, such as secretion of pro-inflammatory 54 or anti-inflammatory cytokines, clearance of apoptotic cells and presentation of antigens. 55 Dysregulation of such functions through aberrant polarization is associated with a range of 56 diseases, including susceptibility to infection, cancer progression, atherosclerosis, and fibrosis 8– 

57 11 . Accordingly, there has been growing interest in engineering primary human macrophages for 58 therapeutic application. Technologies for precisely engineer macrophage polarization states 59 would significantly enhance our understanding of macrophage biology and provide new strategies 60 for controlling the immune landscape in various disease contexts. 61 62 Several strategies have advanced macrophage engineering, including polarization with 63 exogenous cytokines, use of chimeric switch receptors 12 or chimeric antigen receptors with 64 polarizing signaling domains 13 , and gene knockdown or overexpression 14 . These approaches can 65 guide macrophages to altered polarization states and enable course-grained control of 66 macrophage phenotype. A remaining challenge is to reliably program macrophage polarization 67 states for research and therapeutic applications. Ideally, we would be able to co-optimize 68 phenotypes like phagocytic activity and inflammatory state. One potential solution is a69 programmable platform that allows the user to precisely navigate the polarization landscape and 70 predictably guide human primary macrophages to desired states. 71 72 Cytokines are major drivers of macrophage polarization state 15 . In addition to IFN-γ and IL-10, 73 cytokines like IL-4, IL-13, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, 74 macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating 75 factor (GM-CSF), and others can alter macrophage polarization 6. These cytokines are sensed by 76 receptors whose intracellular domains contain short linear signaling motifs 16–18 . These signaling 77 motifs serve as docking sites for downstream effectors that activate signaling cascades and drive 78 phenotype changes 17,18 . Nature has evolved hundreds of cytokine receptors that use varied 79 signaling motifs to steer the phenotypes of innate and adaptive immune cell types 19 . Because 80 natural receptor domains achieve functional diversity through varied combinations of signaling 81 motifs, we hypothesized that synthetic recombination of these motifs could polarize macrophages 82 to diverse functional states. We further reasoned that a quantitative model that relates signaling 83 motif combination to macrophage polarization would enable rational programming of macrophage 84 phenotype. 85 86 We previously developed a synthetic cytokine receptor (SCR), which constitutively activates cell 87 signaling cascades via short modular signaling motifs (~16 amino acids including a 4 to 8 amino 88 acid consensus binding motif and flanking amino acids) in the receptor’s intracellular signaling 89

domain. SCR signaling is cytokine-independent, activating signals in the absence of external 90 input. SCRs with varied signaling motifs produce diverse chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell 91 states with unique survival, proliferation, differentiation, and cytotoxicity 20 .92 

93 Here, we extend the use of SCRs to program user-defined macrophage polarization states. By 94 recombining signaling motifs in the SCR signaling domain, we generate a broad spectrum of 95 synthetic macrophage polarization states, encompassing pro-inflammatory, anti-inflammatory, 96 and highly phagocytic phenotypes. We find that expression of select SCRs can improve the 97 targeted phagocytosis of cancer cells by CAR-macrophages in vitro and in vivo . We find that a 98 two-state model describes the motif-to-phenotype relationship and use this model to rationally 99 design an SCR whose expression drives macrophages to a state that is highly phagocytic, but 100 also maintains a pro-inflammatory profile during a long-term CAR-macrophage killing assay. 101 Together, these results establish a modular and programmable strategy for interrogating 102 macrophage polarization and provide a foundation for the rational engineering of macrophage 103 functions for therapeutic application. 104

Synthetic cytokine receptors drive ligand-independent macrophage polarization 108 

Cytokine binding to natural receptors induces receptor complex assembly and activates signaling 109 cascades. The specific signaling pathways activated are determined by the signaling motifs 110 present on the receptors’ intracellular domain (ICD) 21 . To achieve cytokine-independent activation 111 of user-defined signaling, we created synthetic cytokine receptors (SCRs) (Fig. 1a). A single 112 lentiviral vector encodes both the SCR and green fluorescent protein (GFP) (Fig. 1a). Each SCR 113 contains three domains: an extracellular coiled-coil that facilitates homodimerization, a114 transmembrane domain (TMD) positioned for maximal signaling 20,22 , and the gp130 box domain 115 which recruits Janus kinases (JAKs) 23 . These three domains are followed by signaling motifs in 116 the distal region, which can be varied to alter cell signaling and thereby program cell phenotype. 117 SCR0, which lacks signaling motifs, serves as a control. 118 119 To construct SCRs with varied signaling, we selected nine signaling motifs from natural receptor 120 ICDs (Fig. 1b). Each motif has binding or signaling activity that has previously been documented. 121 Included are signaling motifs from the IL-10 receptor (IL-10R), which drives anti-inflammatory 122 polarization, and the IFN-γ receptor (IFNGR1), which drives pro-inflammatory polarization 6. We 123 hypothesized that SCRs containing these motifs would drive distinct macrophage polarization 124 states. To test this hypothesis, we constructed nine SCRs, each containing one of the motifs (S1 125 through S9) (Fig. 1b). We then co-transduced human primary monocyte-derived macrophages 126 with lentivirus encoding the nine SCRs (Fig. 1c) and virus-like particles containing the protein Vpx, 127 which enables efficient myeloid cell transduction 24–26 (Extended Data Fig. 1b). To assess the 128 effect of each SCR on macrophage polarization, we used flow cytometry to measure expression 129 of select surface proteins that serve as macrophage polarization markers (CD163, CD206, CD80, 130 CD40 and PDL1). E. coli labeled with a pH-sensitive dye (pHrodo) that fluoresces in the 131 phagolysosome was used in live-cell imaging experiments to assess the effects of the SCRs on 132 phagocytosis (Fig. 1c). We benchmarked the SCR-driven effects against 48-hour treatments with 133 exogenous ligands (LPS and IFN-γ, IFN-γ, IL-10, IL-4, and GM-CSF) known to drive macrophage 134 polarization into distinct states 6 (Extended Data Fig. 1e). 135 136 For each of the measured surface markers, at least one motif increased expression to levels 137 comparable to ligand stimulation (Fig. 1d and Fig. 1e). For example, SCR(S1), which contains the 138 core amino acid “YDKPH” sequence from IFNGR1, increased CD40, CD80, and PDL1 expression 139

SCRs with signaling motifs from natural receptors mimic cytokine-induced macrophage 160 

polarization 161 

162 The motifs S1 through S9 are small fragments of natural receptors and our initial characterization 163 of SCR-induced polarization investigated a narrow set of phenotypes. This raises two questions. 164 First, can these signaling motifs recapitulate cytokine-induced macrophage polarization? Second, 165 how do cytokine-induced and SCR-induced changes compare for more global phenotypic 166 readouts like transcriptional state? 167 168 To address these questions, we compared the transcriptional programs induced by SCRs with 169 those induced by cytokines that activate the native receptors from which the motifs were derived 170 (Fig. 2a). We focused our inquiry on IFNγ and IL-10, which polarize macrophages into well-171 defined pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory states, respectively 4,29–31 . As in Fig. 1c, we 172 transduced macrophages to express SCRs containing phosphotyrosine signaling motifs from 173 either IFNGR1 (S1) or IL-10Rα (S3) (Extended Fig. 2b). We then performed bulk RNA sequencing 174 (RNA-seq) on these macrophages and on macrophages treated with IFNγ or IL-10 for 48 hours. 175 Cytokine-stimulated macrophages were also transduced with SCR0 to control for lentiviral 176 transduction effects (Extended Fig. 2b). Monocytes from three different donors were used to 177 assess donor variability (Extended Fig. 2a—b). 178 179 SCR(S1-S1), SCR(S3-S3), IL-10, and IFNγ each changed gene expression relative to SCR0 (Fig. 180 2b). Hierarchical clustering showed that samples clustered by stimulation condition rather than 181 donor (Fig. 2b). Consistent with their motif origins, macrophages expressing SCR(S3-S3) 182 clustered with IL-10-treated macrophages, and macrophages expressing SCR(S1-S1) clustered 183 with IFNγ-treated macrophages (Fig. 2b). The top differentially expressed genes (DEGs) included 184 many DEGs shared between conditions. IL-10-stimulated and SCR(S3-S3) macrophages shared 185 canonical IL-10-responsive genes (e.g. TNIP, CD163, JAK3, SOCS3) (Fig. 2b) 32 . Similarly, 186 several IFNγ-responsive genes (e.g. CXCL9, GBP1, IDO1, GBP4, GBP5, IDO2) were shared 187 between the IFNγ-treated and SCR(S1-S1) macrophages 31,33 (Fig. 2b). These known cytokine-188 regulated genes also appeared in the top 10 DEGs across all samples, indicating strong activation 189 of general macrophage polarization pathways (Extended Data Fig. 3a). 190

Motif S1 induces a myeloid activation state associated with CAR T cell activity in patients 208 

209 The RNAseq data demonstrate that SCRs with signaling motifs from cytokine receptors can 210 induce transcriptional features similar to those induced by cytokine treatment in vitro . We next 211 investigated whether the SCR-driven transcriptional signatures reflect transcriptional signatures 212 observed in clinical settings. A previous study included single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) 213 of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples collected from four patients undergoing CAR T cell therapy 214 for diffuse midline glioma 34 . This study identified an interferon response signature in myeloid cells 215 that emerged in the days immediately following intracerebroventricular (ICV) CAR T cell infusion 216 in a patient (DIPG2) who experienced improved neurological symptoms and a 27% reduction in 217 tumor volume 34,35 . Patient DIPG2 also showed increases in CSF cytokines, including IFNγ and 218 CXCL9, following ICV CAR T cell infusion. To determine if SCR(S1-S1) drives macrophages to a 219 state reflective of this clinical myeloid response to CAR T cell therapy, we calculated an SCR(S1-220 S1) signature score for myeloid cells in the scRNA-seq dataset. The signature score quantifies 221 the transcriptional similarity between patient myeloid cells and SCR(S1-S1) macrophages. In 222 patient DIPG2, the SCR(S1-S1) signature was mostly highly enriched immediately following ICV 223 CAR T cell infusion and subsequently decreased over the following days (Fig 2f, upper panel). 224 This signature score closely paralleled the concentration of IFNγ measured in the patient’s CSF 225 (Fig 2f, lower panel). To generalize this observation, we analyzed all timepoints across patients 226 for which IFNγ measurements and CSF cells were collected on the same day. We observed a 227 strong correlation (ρ = 0.94) between CSF IFNγ measurements and the myeloid SCR(S1-S1) 228 gene signature score (Fig. 2g). These results demonstrate that SCR(S1-S1)-driven transcriptional 229 program observed in vitro is similar to the IFNγ-driven myeloid transcriptional programs observed 230 in CSF following ICV CAR T infusion in a treatment-responsive patient. The alignment of SCR-231 macrophage transcriptomics with patient-identified myeloid signatures suggests programmed 232 cells may serve as a model to understand the mechanistic implications of these cell types in 233 patients. 234 235

Chimeric antigen receptor-expressing macrophages (CAR-Ms) have emerged as 287 promising immunotherapeutics for a range of diseases including cancer, cardiac fibrosis, and 288 bacterial infections 36–39 . The efficacy of CAR-Ms in these therapies will depend on factors like 289 inflammatory state and phagocytic capacity. 290 291

Signaling motifs predictably alter macrophage phenotypes to enable programmable 324 

control of macrophage polarization 325 

326 While SCRs with unique combinations of one to three signaling motifs generated a wide range of 327 macrophage polarization states, these SCRs represents only ~1.78% of the 7,372 possible SCRs 328 containing up to four motifs. Future applications may require expanding the motif selection and 329 increasing motifs per SCR. As this combinatorial space grows, experimentally testing all SCRs 330 becomes impractical, necessitating computational models to predict optimal SCR designs from 331 limited experimental data 40 . Neural networks can predict the effects of signaling motifs on CAR T 332 cell phenotype 20,41 , but these models require more data than is present in our macrophage array 333 dataset. Thus, we sought a data-efficient model that connects the choice of signaling motifs to 334 measurable phenotypes such as cellular activity (e.g., phagocytosis) or expression of a protein. 335 336 Two-state models have relatively few parameters and have been used to describe various 337 biological phenomena such as protein folding reactions or receptor-ligand interactions 42,43 . We 338 employed a two-state model to predict how SCR signaling motifs program macrophage 339 polarization (Fig. 5a, Extended Data Fig. 7). Because motif position does not alter measured 340 phenotypes (Extended Data Fig. 4e), the model assumes motif effects are position-independent. 341 This model also assumes that for each phenotype, cells can exist in an ON state or an OFF state 342

Modeling guides programming of signaling domains that polarize macrophages to a highly 361 

phagocytic and pro-inflammatory cell state 362 

363 We sought to leverage the two-state model to engineer a macrophage with a unique polarization 364 profile advantageous in a disease setting. One desirable macrophage state for anti-cancer 365 therapy is one that both phagocytoses cancer cells efficiently and remodels the tumor 366 microenvironment through pro-inflammatory actions 44 . However, within the experimentally tested 367 SCRs, we noticed a tradeoff between the level of phagocytosis and the expression of pro-368 inflammatory markers (CD40 and CD80) by SCR-expressing macrophages (Fig. 6a). No SCR 369 drove macrophages to a high CD40 and CD80 expression state while also maintaining enhanced 370 phagocytosis (Fig. 6a). We hypothesized that the two-state model could be used to identify motif 371 combinations that overcome this tradeoff—specifically, combinations that would drive 372 macrophages to the CD80 +/CD40 +/phagocytosis high quadrant (Fig. 6a). 373 374 We used the fitted Ks values to predict CD40, CD80, and phagocytosis measurements for all 375 possible SCRs containing up to four motifs. These predictions recapitulated the tradeoff between 376 phagocytosis and CD40/CD80 expression for the SCR designs that were experimentally tested 377 (Fig. 6b). However, we also found that several untested SCRs were predicted to drive 378 macrophages to the CD40 +/CD80 +/Phago high quadrant (Fig. 6b). ## Discussion 416 

417 Macrophages adopt a wide array of polarization states to carry out diverse functions. However, 418 precise control over these states remains a challenge. Here, we leveraged synthetic signaling to 419 systematically program human primary macrophage polarization. Using a two-state model to 420 quantify the motif-to-phenotype relationship, we rationally designed a synthetic receptor that 421 overcomes an observed trade-off between macrophage inflammatory markers and phagocytosis. 422 We additionally showed that this fine-tuned phenotype persists in CAR-macrophages during a 423 long-term co-culture with cancer cell targets. This highlights an important advantage of the motif 424 recombination strategy: each motif has a unique effect on phenotypes like phagocytosis, CD80 425 expression, or CD40 expression. Recombination of motifs therefore allows multi-dimensional 426 programming of phenotypes that are not inherently coupled. 427 428 Building on recent advances in engineered macrophage cell therapies, we used an SCR to 429 enhance killing of solid tumor cells by CAR-Ms. Further optimization of SCRs could increase CAR-430 M survival and persistence of these cells to increase anti-tumor efficacy. The wide range of SCR-431 driven phenotypes observed in our arrayed screen may also be useful in different therapeutic 432 contexts. For example, phagocytic pro-inflammatory CAR-Ms may be useful in treatment of 433 immunosuppressive tumors 44 , while anti-inflammatory macrophages that release Secreted 434 Phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1) may be useful in treating spinal injuries 47 .435 436 The programmability enabled by signaling motif recombination offers a significant advantage over 437 traditional use of exogenous cytokines. By combining signaling motifs from different receptors, 438 we can generate unique signaling 20 —and corresponding macrophage phenotypes—that would 439 be difficult or impossible to control using conventional cytokine stimulation. This programmability 440 creates new possibilities for rational design of SCRs that induce desired macrophage phenotypic 441 profiles for various therapeutic applications, as well as T cells and potentially other immune cells. 442 Though this study focuses on the use of SCRs to polarize macrophages, we have also used SCRs 443

# Study Design 577 

This study was designed to determine if expression of synthetic cytokine receptors (SCRs) could 578 drive polarization of macrophages. SCRs were cloned and expressed in human primary 579 macrophages. Effects of SCR expression on several macrophage phenotypes was assessed 580 using surface marker staining and flow cytometry or by co-culturing the macrophages with 581 biological targets of phagocytosis followed by live cell imaging. Shifts in measured phenotypes 582 were compared to those achieved when untransduced macrophages were stimulated for 48 hours 583 with select exogenous ligands. To determine the transcriptional effects of expressing select SCRs 584 in macrophages, we performed bulk RNA sequencing. To discover if SCRs could generate a wide 585 range of macrophage phenotypes, we recombined 9 signaling motifs on the intracellular domain 586 of the SCR to generate >100 SCRs and tested these using previously described methods. Data 587 from this screen were used to fit a two-state equilibrium model and predict SCR designs to drive 588 both a high inflammatory profile and high phagocytosis. To evaluate if SCRs associated with high 589 phagocytosis could augment the targeted phagocytosis of cancer cells by CAR-Macrophages, we 590 co-cultured macrophages expressing both an SCR and a CAR (SCR-CAR-Ms) with cancer cells 591 expressing the CAR-targeting antigen and a fluorescent marker. # Two-state model 889 

The two-state model was carried out using both R (v.4.3.2) and Mathematica (Wolfram), which 890 produced nearly identical results. Motif combinations and all phenotype measurements (CD163, 891 CD206, CD80, CD40, PDL1, and phagocytosis) for all replicates were used as input to fit models 892 that predict the effect of SCR motif combinations on each phenotype. Equations used in this model 893 can be found in Extended Data Figure 7 and on Github (see Code Availability ). For each SCR, 894 the copy number of each of the 9 motifs (S1–S9) was enumerated from the motif composition 895 metadata. Standard errors across technical replicates were retained as a measure of replicate 896 variability but were not used as weights in fitting. Phenotype-specific fluorescence bounds were 897 defined as the single lowest and single highest mean observed value across all samples for that 898 phenotype, specifying the dynamic range of the two-state model. Model parameters—an intrinsic 899 energy term (ΔEi) and a per-motif energy contribution for each of the nine motifs (ΔEs for S1– 900 S9)—were estimated by minimizing the sum of squared residuals between observed and model-901 predicted fluorescence values, using all individual technical replicates and BFGS optimization 902 (optim() in base R). Model fit statistics (r and R²) were evaluated on per-condition averages of 903 technical replicates. Generalization performance was evaluated via 10-fold cross-validation using 904 the rsample package. Coefficient stability was assessed as the standard deviation of each ΔEs 905 estimate across folds. The difference between in-sample R² and cross-validated R² was used as 906 a diagnostic for overfitting. Data manipulation was performed using dplyr , tidyr , and purrr .907 Per-motif equilibrium constant contributions (Ks) were derived from the fitted ΔEs coefficients, 908 where Ks values greater than 1 indicate that a motif shifts the equilibrium toward the ON state, 909 while values less than 1 indicate a shift toward the OFF state. Ks values are reported on a log ₁₀ 910 scale for visualization. Uncertainty on each log ₁₀ Ks value was propagated from the cross-911 validation fold standard deviation of ΔEs. See Data Availability and Code Availability for details 912 on how this model can be run. 913 914
