# Summary 22 

Technology that precisely controls macrophage polarization to distinct functional states would 23 deepen our understanding of macrophage biology and enable the development of new 24 macrophage cell therapies. Here, we use a synthetic cytokine receptor (SCR) platform with a 25 programmable signaling domain to control the polarization of primary human macrophages. SCRs 26 containing signaling motifs from the interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) or Interleukin-10 (IL-10) receptors 27 mimic key features of pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory polarization, respectively. Random 28 recombination of nine distinct signaling motifs to create new SCR signaling domains generates a 29 diverse landscape of synthetic macrophage states with varied expression of inflammatory 30 markers (CD80, CD40) and anti-inflammatory markers (CD163, CD206), and varied phagocytic 31 capacity. SCRs programmed with multiple YLxQ motifs increase macrophage phagocytosis of E. 32 

coli and chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-macrophage phagocytosis of cancer cells in mice, 33 reducing tumor burden by 30-fold. The motif-dependent polarization is well-described by a two-34 state model, enabling quantitative prediction of macrophage polarization state from SCR signaling 35 domain composition. Leveraging this model, we design an SCR that simultaneously enhances 36 phagocytosis and maintains a macrophage pro-inflammatory state. Together, these findings 37 establish a framework for synthetic programming of macrophage polarization states, with potential 38 applications in cancer immunotherapy and other disease contexts. 39 40

# Introduction 43 

Macrophages are highly plastic cells that play critical roles in natural immunity and, increasingly, 44 as engineered cell therapies 1. Macrophages sense infection and tissue damage, phagocytose 45 pathogens and debris, and sculpt the local environment through a broad repertoire of cytokines 46 and chemokines 2. Central to these roles is macrophages' ability to polarize into distinct cell states 47 with unique functional properties in response to external cues. For example, interferon-gamma 48 (IFN-γ) can polarize macrophages into a state characterized by the display of costimulatory 49 molecules and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines 3. Conversely, interleukin (IL)-10 can 50 polarize macrophages to a state characterized by the secretion of anti-inflammatory cytokines 51 and promotion of tissue repair 4. Evidence indicates that macrophages can integrate signals from 52 multiple stimuli to adopt a broad spectrum of phenotypic states 5–7 . This plasticity enables 53 macrophages to perform a variety of essential functions, such as secretion of pro-inflammatory 54 or anti-inflammatory cytokines, clearance of apoptotic cells, and presentation of antigens. 55 Dysregulation of such functions through aberrant polarization is associated with a range of 56 diseases, including susceptibility to infection, cancer progression, atherosclerosis, and fibrosis 8– 

57 11 . Accordingly, there has been growing interest in engineering primary human macrophages for 58 therapeutic applications. Technologies for precisely engineering macrophage polarization states 59 would significantly enhance our understanding of macrophage biology and provide new strategies 60 for controlling the immune landscape in various disease contexts. 61 62 Several strategies have advanced macrophage engineering, including polarization with 63 exogenous cytokines, use of chimeric switch receptors 12 or chimeric antigen receptors with 64 polarizing signaling domains 13 , and gene knockdown or overexpression 14 . These approaches can 65 guide macrophages to altered polarization states and enable coarse-grained control of 66 macrophage phenotype. A remaining challenge is to reliably program macrophage polarization 67 states for research and therapeutic applications. Ideally, we would be able to co-optimize 68 phenotypes like phagocytic activity and inflammatory state. One potential solution is a69 programmable platform that allows the user to precisely navigate the polarization landscape and 70 predictably guide human primary macrophages to desired states. 71 72 Cytokines are major drivers of macrophage polarization state 15 . In addition to IFN-γ and IL-10, 73 cytokines like IL-4, IL-13, tumor necrosis factor (TNF), transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, 74 macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF), granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating 75 factor (GM-CSF), and others can alter macrophage polarization 6. These cytokines are sensed by 76 receptors whose intracellular domains contain short linear signaling motifs 16–18 . These signaling 77 motifs serve as docking sites for downstream effectors that activate signaling cascades and drive 78 phenotype changes 17,18 . Nature has evolved hundreds of cytokine receptors that use varied 79 signaling motifs to steer the phenotypes of innate and adaptive immune cell types 19 . Because 80 natural receptor domains achieve functional diversity through varied combinations of signaling 81 motifs, we hypothesized that synthetic recombination of these motifs could polarize macrophages 82 to diverse functional states. We further reasoned that a quantitative model that relates signaling 83 motif combination to macrophage polarization would enable rational programming of macrophage 84 phenotype. 85 86 We previously developed a synthetic cytokine receptor (SCR), which constitutively activates cell 87 signaling cascades via short modular signaling motifs (~16 amino acids, including a 4 to 8 amino 88 acid consensus binding motif and flanking amino acids) in the receptor’s intracellular signaling 89

domain. SCR signaling is cytokine-independent, activating signals in the absence of external 90 input. SCRs with varied signaling motifs produce diverse chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cell 91 states with unique survival, proliferation, differentiation, and cytotoxicity 20 .92 

93 Here, we extend the use of SCRs to program user-defined macrophage polarization states. By 94 recombining signaling motifs in the SCR signaling domain, we generate a broad spectrum of 95 synthetic macrophage polarization states, encompassing pro-inflammatory, anti-inflammatory, 96 and highly phagocytic phenotypes. We find that expression of select SCRs can improve the 97 targeted phagocytosis of cancer cells by CAR-macrophages in vitro and in vivo . We find that a 98 two-state model describes the motif-to-phenotype relationship and use this model to rationally 99 design an SCR whose expression drives macrophages to a state that is highly phagocytic, but 100 also maintains a pro-inflammatory profile during a long-term CAR-macrophage killing assay. 101 Together, these results establish a modular and programmable strategy for interrogating 102 macrophage polarization and provide a foundation for the rational engineering of macrophage 103 functions for therapeutic applications. 104

Synthetic cytokine receptors drive ligand-independent macrophage polarization 108 

109 Cytokine binding to natural receptors induces receptor complex assembly and activates signaling 110 cascades. The specific signaling pathways activated are determined by the signaling motifs 111 present on the receptors’ intracellular domain (ICD) 21 . To achieve cytokine-independent activation 112 of user-defined signaling, we created synthetic cytokine receptors (SCRs) (Fig. 1a). A single 113 lentiviral vector encodes both the SCR and green fluorescent protein (GFP) (Fig. 1a). Each SCR 114 contains three domains: an extracellular coiled-coil that facilitates homodimerization, a115 transmembrane domain (TMD) positioned for maximal signaling 20,22 , and the gp130 box domain, 116 which recruits Janus kinases (JAKs) 23 . These three domains are followed by signaling motifs in 117 the distal region, which can be varied to alter cell signaling and thereby program cell phenotype. 118 SCR0, which lacks signaling motifs, serves as a control. 119 120 To construct SCRs with varied signaling, we selected nine signaling motifs from natural receptor 121 ICDs (Fig. 1b). Each motif has binding or signaling activity that has previously been documented. 122 Included are signaling motifs from the IL-10 receptor (IL-10R), which drives anti-inflammatory 123 polarization, and the IFN-γ receptor (IFNGR1), which drives pro-inflammatory polarization 6. We 124 hypothesized that SCRs containing these motifs would drive distinct macrophage polarization 125 states. To test this hypothesis, we constructed nine SCRs, each containing one of the motifs (S1 126 through S9) (Fig. 1b). We then co-transduced human primary monocyte-derived macrophages 127 with lentivirus encoding the nine SCRs (Fig. 1c) and virus-like particles containing the protein Vpx, 128 which enables efficient myeloid cell transduction 24–26 (Extended Data Fig. 1b). To assess the 129 effect of each SCR on macrophage polarization, we used flow cytometry to measure expression 130 of select surface proteins that serve as macrophage polarization markers (CD40, CD80, PDL1, 131 CD206, and CD163). E. coli labeled with a pH-sensitive dye (pHrodo) that fluoresces in the 132 phagolysosome was used in live-cell imaging experiments to assess the effects of the SCRs on 133 phagocytosis (Fig. 1c). We benchmarked the SCR-driven effects against 48-hour treatments with 134 exogenous ligands (LPS and IFN-γ, IFN-γ, IL-10, IL-4, and GM-CSF) known to drive macrophage 135 polarization into distinct states 6 (Extended Data Fig. 1e). 136 137 For each of the measured surface markers, at least one motif increased expression to levels 138 comparable to ligand stimulation (Fig. 1d and Fig. 1e). For example, SCR(S1), which contains the 139

In contrast, SCRs containing motifs S2 or S3, each with a core “YxxQ” sequence, 141 increased both CD163 expression and E. coli phagocytosis (Fig. 1e). 142 143 As expected, IL-4 treatment resulted in expression of CD206. SCRs with signaling motifs from natural receptors mimic cytokine-induced macrophage 161 

polarization 162 

163 The motifs S1 through S9 are small fragments of natural receptors, and our initial characterization 164 of SCR-induced polarization investigated a narrow set of phenotypes. This raises two questions. 165 First, can these signaling motifs recapitulate cytokine-induced macrophage polarization? Second, 166 how do cytokine-induced and SCR-induced changes compare for more global phenotypic 167 readouts like transcriptional state? 168 169 To address these questions, we compared the transcriptional programs induced by SCRs with 170 those induced by cytokines that activate the native receptors from which the motifs were derived 171 (Fig. 2a). We focused our inquiry on IFNγ and IL-10, which polarize macrophages into well-172 defined pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory states, respectively 4,29–31 . As in Fig. 1c, we 173 transduced macrophages to express SCRs containing phosphotyrosine signaling motifs from 174 either IFNGR1 (S1) or IL-10Rα (S3) (Extended Fig. 2b). We then performed bulk RNA sequencing 175 (RNA-seq) on these macrophages and on macrophages treated with IFNγ or IL-10 for 48 hours. 176 Cytokine-stimulated macrophages were also transduced with SCR0 to control for lentiviral 177 transduction effects (Extended Fig. 2b). Monocytes from three different donors were used to 178 assess donor variability (Extended Fig. 2a—b). 179 180 SCR(S1-S1), SCR(S3-S3), IL-10, and IFNγ each changed gene expression relative to SCR0 (Fig. 181 2b). Hierarchical clustering showed that samples clustered by stimulation condition rather than 182 donor (Fig. 2b). Consistent with their motif origins, macrophages expressing SCR(S3-S3) 183 clustered with IL-10-treated macrophages, and macrophages expressing SCR(S1-S1) clustered 184 with IFNγ-treated macrophages (Fig. 2b). The top differentially expressed genes (DEGs) included 185 many DEGs shared between conditions. IL-10-stimulated and SCR(S3-S3) macrophages shared 186 canonical IL-10-responsive genes (e.g., TNIP, CD163, JAK3, SOCS3) (Fig. 2b) 32 . Similarly, 187 several IFNγ-responsive genes (e.g., CXCL9, GBP1, IDO1, GBP4, GBP5, IDO2) were shared 188 between the IFNγ-treated and SCR(S1-S1) macrophages 31,33 (Fig. 2b). Motif S1 induces a myeloid activation state associated with CAR T cell activity in patients 209 

210 The RNAseq data demonstrate that SCRs with signaling motifs from cytokine receptors can 211 induce transcriptional features similar to those induced by cytokine treatment in vitro . We next 212 investigated whether the SCR-driven transcriptional signatures reflect transcriptional signatures 213 observed in clinical settings. A previous study included single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) 214 of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples collected from four patients undergoing CAR T cell therapy 215 for diffuse midline glioma 34 . This study identified an interferon response signature in myeloid cells 216 that emerged in the days immediately following intracerebroventricular (ICV) CAR T cell infusion 217 in a patient (DIPG2) who experienced improved neurological symptoms and a 27% reduction in 218 tumor volume 34,35 . Patient DIPG2 also showed increases in CSF cytokines, including IFNγ and 219 CXCL9, following ICV CAR T cell infusion. To determine if SCR(S1-S1) drives macrophages to a 220 state reflective of this clinical myeloid response to CAR T cell therapy, we calculated an SCR(S1-221 S1) signature score for myeloid cells in the scRNA-seq dataset. The signature score quantifies 222 the transcriptional similarity between patient myeloid cells and SCR(S1-S1) macrophages. In 223 patient DIPG2, the SCR(S1-S1) signature was mostly highly enriched immediately following ICV 224 CAR T cell infusion and subsequently decreased over the following days (Fig 2f, upper panel). 225 This signature score closely paralleled the concentration of IFNγ measured in the patient’s CSF 226 (Fig 2f, lower panel). To generalize this observation, we analyzed all time points across patients 227 for which IFNγ measurements and CSF cells were collected on the same day. We observed a 228 strong correlation (ρ = 0.94) between CSF IFNγ measurements and the myeloid SCR(S1-S1) 229 gene signature score (Fig. 2g). These results demonstrate that the SCR(S1-S1)-driven 230 transcriptional program observed in vitro is similar to the IFNγ-driven myeloid transcriptional 231 programs observed in CSF following ICV CAR T infusion in a treatment-responsive patient. The 232 alignment of SCR-macrophage transcriptomics with patient-identified myeloid signatures 233 suggests programmed cells may serve as a model to understand the mechanistic implications of 234 these cell types in patients. 235 236

We next asked if combining multiple motifs in one SCR can achieve unique polarization states 239 not achieved using single cytokine stimulations. In contrast to the classical “M1/M2” paradigm, 240 transcriptomic analyses have shown that polarized macrophages exist along a polarization 241 spectrum, shaped by diverse stimuli 5. Thus, we sought to explore whether expression of SCRs 242 with several motifs would access a spectrum of polarization states (Fig. 3a). We generated a 243 library of 131 unique SCRs (Fig. 3b) containing zero (n=1), one (n=9), two (n=81), or three (n=40) 244 of the nine signaling motifs (Fig. 1b). With these 131 SCRs, we performed an arrayed experiment 245 to assess each SCR’s effect on macrophage polarization (Fig. 3c). To contextualize these effects, 246 we compared SCR-induced phenotypes to untransduced macrophages, SCR0-expressing 247 macrophages, and macrophages stimulated with exogenous factors (IL-10, IFNγ, LPS + IFNγ, 248 IL4, or GM-CSF). 249 250 The SCR library induced a broad spectrum of macrophage polarization states with distinct 251 phagocytosis and expression of CD163, CD206, CD40, CD80, and PDL1 (Fig. 3d). This spectrum 252 included both states that mimicked single cytokine treatments and states not achieved with 253 cytokine stimulation. We used hierarchical clustering (Fig. 3d) and dimensionality reduction (Fig. 254 3e—g) to summarize SCR-induced polarization. UMAP embedding of all normalized surface 255 marker and phagocytosis measurements showed that exogenous ligand treatments occupy 256 distinct regions of the map, while SCR-driven phenotypes occupy the intervening phenotypic 257 space (Fig. 3e). Five distinct functional clusters emerged. First, a CD40 high PDL1 high region 258 occupied by LPS + IFNγ-stimulated macrophages (Fig. 3f). Second, a CD206 high area populated 259 by GM-CSF-stimulated macrophages (Fig. 3f). Third, a region containing SCR-expressing 260 samples with elevated phagocytic activity (Phago high ) (Fig. 3f). Fourth, a baseline cluster 261 containing SCR0 controls and SCRs with motif combinations that produced no significant 262 phenotypic shifts (Fig. 3e). Lastly, a cluster of untransduced or IL-4-treated macrophages (Fig. 263 3e). The measured phenotypes were variably correlated with each other, with CD40 and PDL1 264 showing the strongest correlation (r = 0.86) (Extended Data Fig. 4b). Unexpectedly, CD80, a 265 canonical pro-inflammatory marker, was expressed in multiple clusters, demonstrating that CD80 266 expression can occur outside strictly pro-inflammatory contexts (Extended Data Fig. 4c). 267 268 We analyzed how motifs were distributed across UMAP space to determine each motif’s 269 contribution to macrophage polarization. SCRs containing solely the S1 motif (derived from 270 IFNGR1) populated the CD40 high PDL1 high region (Fig. 3g). This S1-driven effect dominated 271 regardless of the presence or number of non-S1 motifs within the SCR (Fig. 3g). In contrast, motif 272 S4 (derived from IL2Rβ) drove macrophages to the CD206 high region, while S2 (derived from IL9R) 273 and S3 (derived from IL10Rα) drove macrophages to the Phago high region. Unlike S1, the effects 274 of S4, S2, and S3 were not dominant and could be modulated by the presence of other motifs 275 within the SCR (Fig. 3g, Extended Data Fig. 4d). Motif position within the ICD did not affect 276 phenotype. SCRs with reversed motif orders (e.g. ‘S1-S2’ vs. ‘S2-S1’) produced similar 277 phenotypes (Extended Data Fig. 4e). Additionally, motif copy number impacted phenotype 278 differently for each motif and phenotype (Extended Data Fig. 4f). For example, one copy of S1 279 drove maximal PDL1, CD40 and CD80 expression, while S2 increased phagocytosis 280 proportionally with copy number (Extended Data Fig. 4f). Collectively, these findings are 281 consistent with the macrophage polarization spectrum model and demonstrate that the complex 282 phenotypic landscape can be navigated by varying SCR motif combinations. 283

Signaling motifs predictably alter macrophage phenotypes to enable programmable 325 

control of macrophage polarization 326 

327 While SCRs with unique combinations of one to three signaling motifs generated a wide range of 328 macrophage polarization states, these SCRs represent only ~1.78% of the 7,372 possible SCRs 329 containing up to four motifs. Future applications may require expanding the motif selection and 330 increasing the number of motifs per SCR. As this combinatorial space grows, experimentally 331 testing all SCRs becomes impractical, necessitating computational models to predict optimal SCR 332 designs from limited experimental data 40 . Neural networks can predict the effects of signaling 333 motifs on CAR T cell phenotype 20,41 , but these models require more data than is present in our 334 macrophage array dataset. Thus, we sought a data-efficient model that connects the choice of 335 signaling motifs to measurable phenotypes such as cellular activity (e.g., phagocytosis) or 336 expression of a protein. 337 338 Two-state models have relatively few parameters and have been used to describe various 339 biological phenomena, such as protein folding reactions or receptor-ligand interactions 42,43 . We 340

employed a two-state model to predict how SCR signaling motifs program macrophage 341 polarization (Fig. 5a, Extended Data Fig. 7). Because motif position does not alter measured 342 phenotypes (Extended Data Fig. 4e), the model assumes motif effects are position-independent. 343 This model also assumes that for each phenotype, cells can exist in an ON state or an OFF state 344 whose relative populations are defined by the polarization constant K (Fig 5a). Without SCR 345 signaling, the relative populations of the ON and OFF states are defined by the intrinsic 346 polarization constant, Ki (Fig. 5b). Each motif added to the SCR shifts these populations of the 347 ON and OFF states for a given phenotype. The effect of each motif on each phenotype is 348 described by motif-dependent polarization constant s, Ks (s, signaling motif) (Fig. 5b). 349 

350 We fit this two-state model to the array data (Fig. 3d) to estimate the effects of each motif ( Ks ) on 351 each phenotype (Fig. 5b). The data were well-described by the model. We observed high 352 correlation between predicted and observed values across phenotypes, including surface marker 353 expression (CD163 (r = 0.85), PDL1 (r = 0.95), CD40 (r = 0.94), CD80 (r = 0.80), CD206 (r = 354 0.92)), and phagocytic activity (r = 0.79) (Fig. 5b—c). Estimated Ks values reflect the impact of 355 each motif on phenotype. These values were consistent with the prior observation that motif S1 356 drove macrophages to a CD40 high state (log 10 Ks = 1.01) (Fig. 5d), motifs S2 and S3 drove 357 macrophages to the Phago high state (log 10 Ks (S2) = 0.32; log 10 Ks (S3) = 0.70), and motif S4 drove 358 macrophages to a CD206 high state (log 10 Ks (S3) = 1.17) (Fig. 5d). These modeling results suggest 359 that signaling motifs, ubiquitous elements in cytokine receptors that control macrophage 360 phenotype, shift phenotype in a predictable manner. 361 362 

Modeling guides programming of signaling domains that polarize macrophages to a highly 363 

phagocytic and pro-inflammatory cell state 364 

365 We sought to leverage the two-state model to engineer a macrophage with a unique polarization 366 profile advantageous in a disease setting. One desirable macrophage state for anti-cancer 367 therapy is one that both phagocytoses cancer cells efficiently and remodels the tumor 368 microenvironment through pro-inflammatory actions 44 . However, within the experimentally tested 369 SCRs, we noticed a tradeoff between the level of phagocytosis and the expression of pro-370 inflammatory markers (CD40 and CD80) by SCR-expressing macrophages (Fig. 6a). No SCR 371 drove macrophages to a high CD40 and CD80 expression state while also maintaining enhanced 372 phagocytosis (Fig. 6a). We hypothesized that the two-state model could be used to identify motif 373 combinations that overcome this tradeoff—specifically, combinations that would drive 374 macrophages to the CD80 +/CD40 +/phagocytosis high quadrant (Fig. 6a). 375 376 We used the fitted Ks values to predict CD40, CD80, and phagocytosis measurements for all 377 possible SCRs containing up to four motifs. These predictions recapitulated the tradeoff between 378 phagocytosis and CD40/CD80 expression for the SCR designs that were experimentally tested 379 (Fig. 6b). However, we also found that several untested SCRs were predicted to drive 380 macrophages to the CD40 +/CD80 +/Phago high quadrant (Fig. 6b). ## Discussion 417 

418 Macrophages adopt a wide array of polarization states to carry out diverse functions. However, 419 precise control over these states remains a challenge. Here, we leveraged synthetic signaling to 420 systematically program human primary macrophage polarization. Using a two-state model to 421 quantify the motif-to-phenotype relationship, we rationally designed a synthetic receptor that 422 overcomes an observed trade-off between macrophage inflammatory markers and phagocytosis. 423 We additionally showed that this fine-tuned phenotype persists in CAR-macrophages during a 424 long-term co-culture with cancer cell targets. This highlights an important advantage of the motif 425 recombination strategy: each motif has a unique effect on phenotypes like phagocytosis, CD80 426 expression, or CD40 expression. Recombination of motifs therefore allows multi-dimensional 427 programming of phenotypes that are not inherently coupled. 428 429 Building on recent advances in engineered macrophage cell therapies, we used an SCR to 430 enhance the killing of solid tumor cells by CAR-Ms. Further optimization of SCRs could increase 431 CAR-M survival and persistence of these cells to increase anti-tumor efficacy. The wide range of 432 SCR-driven phenotypes observed in our arrayed screen may also be useful in different 433 therapeutic contexts. For example, phagocytic pro-inflammatory CAR-Ms may be useful in the 434 treatment of immunosuppressive tumors 44 , while anti-inflammatory macrophages that release 435 Secreted Phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1) may be useful in treating spinal injuries 47 .436 437 The programmability enabled by signaling motif recombination offers a significant advantage over 438 traditional use of exogenous cytokines. By combining signaling motifs from different receptors, 439 we can generate unique signaling 20 —and corresponding macrophage phenotypes—that would 440 be difficult or impossible to control using conventional cytokine stimulation. # Study Design 578 

This study was designed to determine if expression of synthetic cytokine receptors (SCRs) could 579 drive polarization of macrophages. SCRs were cloned and expressed in human primary 580 macrophages. Effects of SCR expression on several macrophage phenotypes was assessed 581 using surface marker staining and flow cytometry or by co-culturing the macrophages with 582 biological targets of phagocytosis followed by live cell imaging. Shifts in measured phenotypes 583 were compared to those achieved when untransduced macrophages were stimulated for 48 hours 584 with select exogenous ligands. To determine the transcriptional effects of expressing select SCRs 585 in macrophages, we performed bulk RNA sequencing. To discover if SCRs could generate a wide 586 range of macrophage phenotypes, we recombined 9 signaling motifs on the intracellular domain 587 of the SCR to generate >100 SCRs and tested these using previously described methods. Data 588 from this screen were used to fit a two-state equilibrium model and predict SCR designs to drive 589 both a high inflammatory profile and high phagocytosis. To evaluate if SCRs associated with high 590 phagocytosis could augment the targeted phagocytosis of cancer cells by CAR-Macrophages, we 591

# Two-state model 890 

The two-state model was carried out using both R (v.4.3.2) and Mathematica (Wolfram), which 891 produced nearly identical results. Motif combinations and all phenotype measurements (CD163, 892 CD206, CD80, CD40, PDL1, and phagocytosis) for all replicates were used as input to fit models 893 that predict the effect of SCR motif combinations on each phenotype. Equations used in this model 894 can be found in Extended Data Figure 7 and on Github (see Code Availability ). For each SCR, 895 the copy number of each of the 9 motifs (S1–S9) was enumerated from the motif composition 896 metadata. Standard errors across technical replicates were retained as a measure of replicate 897 variability but were not used as weights in fitting. Phenotype-specific fluorescence bounds were 898 defined as the single lowest and single highest mean observed value across all samples for that 899 phenotype, specifying the dynamic range of the two-state model. Model parameters—an intrinsic 900 energy term (ΔEi) and a per-motif energy contribution for each of the nine motifs (ΔEs for S1– 901 S9)—were estimated by minimizing the sum of squared residuals between observed and model-902 predicted fluorescence values, using all individual technical replicates and BFGS optimization 903 (optim() in base R). Model fit statistics (r and R²) were evaluated on per-condition averages of 904 technical replicates. Generalization performance was evaluated via 10-fold cross-validation using 905 the rsample package. Coefficient stability was assessed as the standard deviation of each ΔEs 906 estimate across folds. The difference between in-sample R² and cross-validated R² was used as 907 a diagnostic for overfitting. Data manipulation was performed using dplyr , tidyr , and purrr .908 Per-motif equilibrium constant contributions (Ks) were derived from the fitted ΔEs coefficients, 909 where Ks values greater than 1 indicate that a motif shifts the equilibrium toward the ON state, 910 while values less than 1 indicate a shift toward the OFF state. Ks values are reported on a log ₁₀ 911 scale for visualization. Uncertainty on each log ₁₀ Ks value was propagated from the cross-912 validation fold standard deviation of ΔEs. See Data Availability and Code Availability for details 913 on how this model can be run. 914 915
