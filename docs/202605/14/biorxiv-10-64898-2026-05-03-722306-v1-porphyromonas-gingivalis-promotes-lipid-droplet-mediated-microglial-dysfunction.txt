Abstract 26 

Growing evidence supports a strong association between periodontitis and Alzheimer’s disease 27 

(AD), yet the mechanisms linking these conditions remain poorly defined. In neurodegenerative 28 

disorders, including AD, microglia are often characterized by increased lipid droplet (LD) 29 

accumulation, heightened activation, and impaired function. In this study, we examined whether 30 

Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg ), a keystone periodontal pathogen, promotes LD accumulation in 31 

microglia and disrupts their function. We found that Pg infection induces robust LD 32 

accumulation in BV2 microglial cells and in microglia from Pg -infected App KI mice. This Pg -33 

driven LD buildup was closely associated with elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS) 34 

production, impaired phagocytic ability, and altered activation. Notably, pharmacological 35 

inhibition of LD with a triglyceride synthesis inhibitor effectively reversed Pg -induced LD 36 

accumulation, mitigated ROS production, and restored phagocytic function, thus underscoring 37 

the critical role of lipid metabolism in regulating microglial function. These findings support a 38 

model in which, in the context of periodontitis, systemic dissemination of periodontal pathogens 39 

promotes LD accumulation in microglia, and this metabolic alteration exacerbates microglia 40 

dysfunction via a self-reinforcing cycle of excessive oxidative stress and impaired phagocytosis, 41 

potentially accelerating AD progression. 42

Introduction 46 

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common cause of dementia, characterized by an age-47 

related, progressive, and irreversible decline in cognitive and behavioral functions within the 48 

central nervous system (CNS) (Klyucherev et al. 2022). AD and its related dementias not only 49 

severely reduce patients quality of life but also bring a heavy economic burden to their families 50 

and society (Cavaco et al. 2025). The pathological hallmarks of AD include extracellular 51 

amyloid-β (A β) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles composed of 52 

hyperphosphorylated tau (Knopman et al. 2021). A β accumulation can be detected long before 53 

clinical symptoms appear, with its aggregation triggering a cascade of pathogenic events 54 

contributing to disease progression (Uhlmann et al. 2020). Despite extensive research, the 55 

mechanisms underlying AD remain incompletely understood, and no curative therapy is 56 

available (Passeri et al. 2022). Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors is crucial to 57 

developing strategies to delay, mitigate, or prevent disease onset and progression. 58 

Epidemiological studies have indicated multiple AD risk factors, including aging, systemic 59 

inflammation, traumatic CNS injury, and infection (Heneka et al. 2015). Periodontitis is a 60 

chronic inflammatory disease of the periodontium driven by dysbiosis of subgingival biofilm and 61 

is the leading cause of tooth loss in adults (Lamont and Hajishengallis 2015). Notably, the 62 

keystone periodontal pathogen Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg ) and its virulence factors have 63 

been detected in AD brains and linked to elevated A β and tau pathology (Dominy et al. 2019). 64 

Furthermore, patients with elevated serum antibody titers against Pg are associated with elevated 65 

cerebrospinal fluid levels of both A β and tau (Laugisch et al. 2018). Animal studies further 66 

demonstrate that Pg and its LPS promote Aβ deposition, tau hyperphosphorylation, 67 

neuroinflammation, and cognitive decline (Aravindraja et al. 2022). Large-scale longitudinal data 68 

reveal that older adults with periodontal disease and oral infections are more likely to develop 69 

AD, with Pg -specific antibodies associated with both AD diagnosis and AD-related mortality 70 

(Merchant et al. 2024). Collectively, these findings support a strong association between 71 

periodontitis and AD, yet the mechanisms by which periodontitis contributes to AD development 72 

remain poorly understood. 73 

Microglia, the resident immune cells of the CNS, play key roles in maintaining brain 74 

homeostasis by responding to injury or infection and by removing harmful proteins such as A β75 

through phagocytosis and enzymatic degradation (Heneka et al. 2015). Under chronic stress, 76

aging, or persistent inflammation, microglia lose their homeostatic state and become 77 

dysfunctional. This shift is marked by impaired phagocytosis, excessive production of 78 

inflammatory mediators, and increased oxidative stress (Mirarchi et al. 2024). Microglia 79 

dysfunction is a major contributor to AD pathology. We have previously shown that Pg can 80 

activate microglia and trigger robust inflammatory responses (Hao et al. 2022). However, the 81 

specific mechanisms through which Pg disrupts microglial function remain largely unknown. 82 

Lipid droplets (LD) are dynamic, lipid-rich organelles consisting of a hydrophobic core of 83 

neutral lipids surrounded by a phospholipid monolayer and regulatory proteins (Jin et al. 2023). 84 

Once viewed as inert lipid storage sites, LD are now recognized as key regulators of cellular 85 

stress, metabolism, and inflammation (Verma et al. 2025). Under physiological conditions, LD 86 

formation is thought to exert protective effects by sequestering excess or toxic lipids and 87 

buffering cells against metabolic and oxidative stress (Cortini et al. 2025). However, when LD 88 

biogenesis becomes dysregulated, their accumulation can shift from adaptive to pathological (Yu 89 

et al. 2025). Aberrant or ectopic LD accumulation in non-adipose cells and tissues has been 90 

documented in a range of chronic inflammatory and metabolic disorders, including 91 

atherosclerosis, diabetes, obesity, rheumatoid arthritis, and AD (Chait and den Hartigh 2020), all 92 

of which have been linked with periodontitis (Hajishengallis 2022). In myeloid cells, abnormal 93 

LD accumulation has been linked with inflammation and cellular dysfunction (Zhao et al. 2024). 94 

This connection has gained particular attention in the CNS, where recent work has identified a 95 

distinct population of LD-accumulating microglia that emerges with aging (Marschallinger et al. 96 

2020b). These LD-accumulating microglia exhibit a dysfunctional and proinflammatory state in 97 

the aging brain and contribute to neurodegenerative processes. Pg is known to disseminate 98 

systemically to influence neuroinflammatory pathways (Hao et al. 2022), yet it is not known if 99 

Pg affects LD accumulation in microglia and the functional consequences. 100 

In the present study, we demonstrate that the Pg infection increases LD accumulation in 101 

microglia both in vitro and in vivo in an AD mouse model. These LD-accumulated microglia 102 

exhibit increased ROS production and impaired phagocytic capacity toward Ab. Inhibiting Pg -103 

induced LD accumulation decreased ROS production and restored phagocytosis deficits of 104 

microglia. Our findings suggest that, in the context of periodontitis, systemic dissemination of 105 

periodontal pathogens may induce LD accumulation in microglia, which could establish a self-106 

reinforcing cycle of microglia dysfunction that accelerates AD progression. 107

Results 117 

Pg invades microglial cells and induces LD accumulation 118 

Invasion to host cells and tissues is a critical virulence factor for Pg , enabling deeper 119 

tissue destruction, systemic spread, and immune evasion (Olsen and Progulske-Fox 2015). Pg 120 

has been shown to invade multiple cells, including epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and 121 

macrophages (Li et al. 2008). To evaluate Pg microglial invasion, CFSE-labeled Pg uptake by 122 

BV2 cells was analyzed by confocal microscopy. Distinct uptake of Pg in the cytoplasm of BV2 123 

cells were observed 3 h following Pg treatment (Figure 1A) . The presence of Pg in BV2 cells 124 

was further validated by the presence of a significantly higher level of 16S rRNA expression in 125 

Pg -infected BV2 cells as compared with control cells (Figure 1B) .126 

To investigate time-dependent effect of Pg -induced LD formation, BV2 cells were 127 

infected with Pg (MOI = 50) for 0-24 h. Significantly increased LD accumulation was seen as 128 

early as 3 h post-infection (Appendix figure 1) . No further increase in LD number and intensity 129 

was noted at later time points. To investigate whether there is a dose-dependent effect, BV2 cells 130 

were infected with Pg at different MOIs for 3 h, and no significant differences in LD 131 

accumulation were seen at different doses of Pg tested (Appendix figure 2) .132 

To further validate LD accumulation, BV2 cells were pre-treated with TrC, an enzyme 133 

that is essential for the synthesis of triglycerides and cholesterol esters required for LD formation 134 

(Namatame et al. 1999). Results from both confocal microscopy and flow cytometry analysis 135 

showed that Pg -infection significantly increased LD accumulation in BV2 cells, while pre-136 

treating cells with TrC significantly inhibited Pg -induced LD accumulation (Figure 1C-F) .137 

These results demonstrate that Pg can invade microglial cells and induce LD accumulation. 138

Emerging evidence indicates that brain tissue in patients with AD is subjected to oxidative 140 

stress, characterized by an imbalance between ROS production and impaired antioxidant defense 141 

mechanisms (Alkhalifa et al. 2025). Increased ROS production in microglia has been linked to 142 

microglial dysfunction and AD progression (Li et al. 2022). Here, we investigated the effect of 143 

Pg infection on ROS production and the role of LD in regulating the effect. Confocal microscopy 144 

revealed that Pg stimulation markedly increased ROS production compared to the controls 145 

(Figure 2A-B) . However, pre-treatment with TrC significantly reduced ROS levels, which is 146 

comparable to that observed with the ROS inhibitor AD4. Consistently, flow cytometry analysis 147 

showed that Pg stimulation significantly elevated ROS production, while TrC pre-treatment 148 

significantly attenuated this response (Figure 2C-D) .149 

We next examined the expression of iNOS and NOX-2 genes, which are crucial producers of 150 

ROS (Nocella et al. 2023). Pg -infection significantly increased iNOS and NOX-2 mRNA 151 

expression in BV2 cells (Figure 2E-F) . Furthermore, pre-treatment with TrC markedly reduced 152 

iNOS and NOX-2 gene expression, while no significant inhibition of iNOS and NOX-2 gene 153 

expression was observed in BV2 cells pre-treated with AD4. It has been shown that AD4 154 

removes ROS that are already produced via boosting intracellular glutathione level, but it does 155 

not necessarily suppress the signaling pathways that drive iNOS/NOX-2 gene expression (Amer 156 

et al. 2008). Taken together, our results indicate that Pg -induced LD accumulation contributes to 157 

oxidative stress in microglial cells via regulation of iNOS and NOX-2 genes. 158 

Pg -induced oxidative stress regulates LD accumulation in microglial cells 159 

Recent studies have shown that oxidative stress promotes the biogenesis of LD, which 160 

protects vulnerable lipids such as unsaturated fatty acids by rerouting them into the triglyceride 161 

core (Cortini et al. 2025). This sequestration prevents ROS-induced peroxidation, thereby 162 

preserving lipid homeostasis and underscoring the regulatory role of oxidative stress on LD-163 

production (Lange et al. 2025). To investigate whether Pg -induced oxidative stress reciprocally 164 

regulates LD, the effect of AD4 on Pg -induced LD accumulation was examined. AD4 pre-165 

treatment markedly reduced LD accumulation in BV2 cells compared to the Pg -infected group 166 

(Figure 2G-H) . This effect was comparable to that observed with the LD inhibitor TrC. Flow 167 

cytometry analysis confirmed that pre-treatment with TrC and AD4 significantly reduced LD 168 

accumulation in BV2 cells (Figure 2I-J) . These results indicate that Pg -infection induces both 169

Pg -induced LD accumulation in microglial cells impairs their ability to phagocytose A β172 

peptides 173 

One of the primary functions of microglia is to clear cellular debris, including A β peptides, 174 

whose buildup contributes to cognitive decline and AD (Brown et al. 2026). To investigate the 175 

effect of Pg on microglial phagocytosis of Ab peptides and the role of LD in regulating the 176 

effect, BV2 cells were pre-treated with vehicle or TrC, followed by Pg-infection, and their ability 177 

to phagocytose Hilyte Fluor 488-labeled Aβ was analyzed. CytoD, an inhibitor of actin 178 

polymerization, was used as a positive control for phagocytosis inhibition (Xie et al. 2025). 179 

Confocal microscopy showed that vehicle-treated BV2 cells exhibited the highest level of A β180 

internalization, whereas CytoD significantly reduced A β uptake as compared to the other groups 181 

(Figure 3A-B) . Pg infection significantly reduced A β uptake in BV2 cells, while TrC restored 182 

the uptake. Flow cytometry further confirmed that the vehicle-treated cells had the maximal rate 183 

of A β internalization, while Pg infection significantly reduced the A β uptake, and TrC restored 184 

the A β uptake (Figure 3C-D) . Taken together, these results demonstrate that Pg infection impairs 185 

microglia phagocytosis of A β, and that inhibition of LD can restore the ability of microglial cells 186 

to phagocytose A β.187 

Since microglial phagocytosis ability is directly linked to their activation state (Valiukas et al. 188 

2025), we next evaluated the activation state of BV2 cells following Pg -infection. Pg infection 189 

significantly downregulated the gene expression of CD68, a key marker for activated and 190 

phagocytic microglia, as compared to control cells (Figure 3E ). Interestingly, the expression of 191 

MHC-II, another microglial activation marker, was significantly up-regulated following Pg 192 

stimulation, while the expression of TMEM119, a microglial resting marker, was significantly 193 

down-regulated (Figure 3F-G) . No difference in CD68 gene expression was seen following TrC 194 

treatment compared to Pg -treated cells (Figure 3E) . These results suggest that Pg -infection 195 

causes microglia dysfunction, characterized by increased activation and reduced phagocytosis, at 196 

least partially due to Pg -induced LD accumulation. 197

We next assessed if Pg -infection could induce LD accumulation in microglia in vivo, and if 200 

these affect oxidative stress and microglial phagocytosis of Ab. App KI mice (Pang et al. 2022) 201 

were infected with Pg or vehicle via retro-orbital injection, and brain tissues were collected 6 h 202 

post-infection for further analysis (Appendix figure 3) . For confocal microscopy analysis, brain 203 

sections were co-stained with IBA1 (microglia), LipidSpot (LD), and Methoxy-X04 (A β) to 204 

examine the spatial distribution of microglia, LD, and Ab plaques. Since the hippocampus is 205 

significantly affected by Ab pathology in AD patients and mouse models (Vyas et al. 2020), we 206 

focused our analysis on this region. An age-dependent increase in the number of hypertrophic 207 

microglia with enlarged cell bodies, along with an increased number and size of LD, were 208 

observed in controls. Pg infection further increased the number of LD and the LD-laden 209 

microglia at 3 months of age. Importantly, A β plaques were predominantly adjacent to LD-laden 210 

microglia, and Pg infection significantly increased the number of LD-loaded microglia adjacent 211 

to A β plaques (Appendix figure 4A-D) . Similar findings were seen in mice at 6 and 12 months 212 

of age (Appendix figure 4E-L) . The number of hypertrophic microglia with enlarged cell bodies 213 

was also observed in Pg -infected App KI mice compared with control App KI mice (Figure 4A) .214 

These results indicate that Pg infection increases LD in microglia in vivo .215 

To investigate how LD accumulation regulates microglial activation and phagocytosis, App 216 

KI mice were pre-treated with TrC before Pg infection. Confocal microscopy showed that TrC 217 

significantly reduced the number of LD-loaded microglia and their proximity to A β plaques 218 

(Figure 4A-D) . Similarly, flow cytometry analysis showed that Pg infection increased the 219 

percentage of LD-loaded microglia and activated microglia without affecting the total number of 220 

microglia (Figure 4E-G) . TrC treatment significantly decreased the percentage of LD-loaded 221 

microglia and activated microglia. Taken together, these results indicate that Pg infection 222 

increases LD accumulation in microglia in AD mice and that Pg -induced LD accumulation 223 

impairs microglial phagocytosis of Ab in vivo .224

Discussions 233 

Microglia are the primary innate immune cells in the brain (El Mesaoudi et al. 2026), and 234 

growing evidence indicates that LD accumulation in microglia represents a key pathological 235 

feature of AD, linking metabolic dysfunction, impaired immune responses, and 236 

neurodegeneration (Wu et al. 2025). For many years, the precise role of microglia in brain 237 

pathology has been subject to debate (Chakrabarty et al. 2015). Currently, microglia are 238 

recognized as immune sentinels and active sculptors of the brain microenvironment, influencing 239 

synaptic remodeling, neuronal survival, and tissue homeostasis (Ye et al. 2025). In AD, microglia 240 

undergo profound metabolic and functional changes, and the emergence of LD-rich microglia 241 

has recently been identified as a hallmark of aging and neurodegeneration (Xing et al. 2025). 242 

While LD formation may initially serve adaptive roles, excessive LD accumulation has been 243 

shown to impair microglial functions (Li et al. 2025). 244 

Increasing evidence suggests that periodontitis is associated with a range of systemic 245 

disorders, including AD (Chen et al. 2026). Although the mechanisms responsible for this 246 

association remain largely unknown, the key periodontal pathogen Pg has been detected in AD 247 

brains and linked to elevated A β and tau pathology (Dominy et al. 2019). However, there is no 248 

information if Pg exerts any influence on LD accumulation in microglia and the metabolic 249 

consequences of this effect. Our study addresses this gap by demonstrating that Pg infection is a 250 

potent inducer of LD accumulation in microglia, both in vitro in BV2 cells and in vivo in App KI 251 

mice. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that Pg infection enhances LD 252 

accumulation in microglia within an AD-relevant animal model. These findings align with 253 

literature showing that Pg infection can induce LD formation in macrophages (Rho et al. 2021) 254 

and that chronic periodontitis contributes to foam cell formation in peripheral immune cells 255 

(Afzoon et al. 2023). 256 

One potential mechanism linking LD accumulation to neurodegeneration is lipid 257 

peroxidation (Sung et al. 2025). When exposed to ROS, LD can undergo peroxidation, 258 

generating cytotoxic lipid species that exacerbate cellular stress and contribute to neuronal injury 259 

(Liu et al. 2015). Enhanced ROS generation is a hallmark of LD-rich microglia and has also been 260 

observed in LD-loaded peripheral immune cells (Marschallinger et al. 2020b), suggesting a 261 

conserved stress-associated metabolic phenotype across immune compartments. However, 262 

whether ROS drives LD accumulation or vice versa remains debated. Some studies propose that 263

oxidative stress promotes LD biogenesis as a protective hub for damaged lipids (Jarc and Petan 264 

2019), whereas others argue that excessive LD buildup can itself enhance ROS generation 265 

through impaired mitochondrial function or increased substrate availability for lipid peroxidation 266 

(Sung et al. 2025). Our results suggest a bidirectional relationship between LD accumulation and 267 

ROS generation in the presence of Pg infection: inhibition of LD formation with TrC markedly 268 

suppressed ROS generation in BV2 cells and in microglia in AD mice, indicating that LD 269 

contributes to Pg -induced oxidative stress; conversely, pharmacologic ROS inhibition reduced 270 

LD accumulation, suggesting that early ROS production may initiate LD formation. This 271 

bidirectional relationship may potentially create a feed-forward loop that accelerates 272 

neurodegenerative processes. 273 

Microglial phagocytosis is essential for clearing cellular debris, protein aggregates, and 274 

dysfunctional synapses that accumulate with aging (Brown et al. 2026). When this clearance 275 

function declines, toxic species, such as A β build up, promoting neuronal damage in AD (Dias 276 

and Socodato 2025). Impaired microglial phagocytosis is therefore recognized as a key 277 

contributor to AD progression. Recent studies have identified phagosome maturation as the top-278 

regulated pathway in LD-accumulating microglia, and these cells exhibit marked deficits in 279 

zymosan particle uptake (Marschallinger et al. 2020a). In our work, we found that Pg infection 280 

similarly disrupts microglial function. We also observed an age-dependent increase in LD-281 

loaded, plaque-proximal microglia in App KI mice. This aligns with recent reports showing that 282 

LD-laden plaque-proximal microglia are present in both human AD brain and the Ab-rich 5xFAD 283 

mouse model (Prakash et al. 2025). Notably, Pg infection further amplified the number of LD-284 

rich microglia in proximity to A β plaques. Together, these findings support a model in which Pg 285 

infection exacerbates microglial LD accumulation, thereby impairing phagocytosis and 286 

potentially accelerating AD-related pathology. 287 

Microglial activation is another key factor linking LD-accumulation with impaired microglial 288 

function (Konishi et al. 2020). We have shown previously that Pg infection induces microglia 289 

activation in vitro and exacerbates microglia activation in vivo in AD mice (Hao et al. 2022); 290 

however, the functionality of these activated microglia needs further study. Data from our present 291 

study demonstrate that Pg infection upregulates microglial activation markers in parallel with LD 292 

accumulation and phagocytic impairment, suggesting that metabolic and functional activation are 293 

intertwined. Importantly, TrC treatment not only reduced LD-accumulation but also restored 294

microglia homeostasis, highlighting the potential of targeting lipid metabolism to improve 295 

microglial function. Indeed, recent studies have suggested that restoring microglial homeostasis 296 

may be a promising therapeutic strategy for slowing AD progression (Liu et al. 2025). Our 297 

ongoing studies are exploring the signaling mechanisms involved in Pg -induced LD 298 

accumulation in microglia. 299 

Taken together, our data support a model in which periodontal infection drives LD 300 

accumulation in microglia, and this metabolic alteration exacerbates microglia dysfunction via a 301 

self-reinforcing cycle of excessive oxidative stress and impaired phagocytic function that may 302 

accelerate AD progression (Figure 5) . While epidemiological studies have long suggested an 303 

association between periodontitis and cognitive decline, our findings offer a mechanistic 304 

explanation rooted in microglial metabolism. Our work strengthens the emerging oral-brain axis 305 

and highlights the importance of maintaining oral health in improving the progression of 306 

neurodegenerative diseases, including AD. 307
