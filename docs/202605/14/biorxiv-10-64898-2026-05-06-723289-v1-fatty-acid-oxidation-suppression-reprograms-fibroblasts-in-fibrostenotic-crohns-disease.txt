Abstract 69 

> 70

Fibrostenotic complications represent a major cause of morbidity in Crohn’s disease (CD), yet the 71 

cellular mechanisms that drive intestinal fibrosis independent of active inflammation remain 72 

poorly understood. Here, we identify impaired fatty acid oxidation (FAO) as a defining metabolic 73 

feature of fibroblasts in fibrostenotic CD. Untargeted lipidomics of non-inflamed colonic tissue 74 

from CD patients demonstrated enrichment of triacylglycerols and long-chain acylcarnitines, 75 

suggesting altered lipid utilization. Across three independent RNA-sequencing cohorts, including 76 

treatment-naïve pediatric ileal biopsies, FAO genes ( CPT1A, CPT2, SLC25A20 ) were selectively 77 

downregulated in patients with or destined to develop fibrostenotic disease. Single-cell RNA-78 

sequencing localized these transcriptional alterations specifically to fibroblasts within strictured 79 

ileum. Primary fibroblasts derived from fibrostenotic CD exhibited increased neutral lipid 80 

accumulation, impaired mitochondrial fatty acid trafficking, and diminished responsiveness to 81 

PPARγ-mediated suppression of TGFβ-induced myofibroblast activation. Together, these findings 82 

demonstrate that FAO impairment is a conserved, fibroblast-specific metabolic program 83 

associated with intestinal fibrosis in CD and suggest that metabolic modulation of stromal cells 84 

represents a potential therapeutic strategy for fibrostenotic disease. 85 

Keywords: Crohn’s disease; fibrostenotic disease; fatty acid oxidation; myofibroblasts; fibrosis 86

Introduction 89 

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory disorder marked by transmural 90 

inflammation affecting any portion of the alimentary tract. Clinically, CD is heterogeneous, with 91 

distinct phenotypes including inflammatory, penetrating, and stricturing presentations. Among 92 

these, stricturing CD poses a particularly challenging clinical course, affecting up to 70% of 93 

patients within a decade of diagnosis and often leading to recurrent bowel obstructions and 94

surgical resections(1, 2). Despite its prevalence, the pathogenesis of intestinal fibrosis and 95 

stricture formation remains incompletely understood and likely reflects contributions from 96 

multiple tissue compartments(3). Moreover, no approved anti-fibrotic therapies currently exist. 97 

Fibrosis, a hallmark of chronic tissue injury and dysregulated repair, involves the excessive 98 

accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components, principally mediated by activated 99 

mesenchymal cells such as myofibroblasts (4, 5). These cells arise from diverse sources and are 100 

metabolically reprogrammed to support ECM production, contractility, and survival (6). In well-101 

established models of pulmonary and dermal fibrosis, including the bleomycin-induced lung 102 

injury model and stiff skin syndromes, myofibroblast activation is tightly linked to shifts in 103 

cellular metabolism, notably enhanced glycolysis and suppressed fatty acid oxidation (FAO)(7– 104 

11). For instance, in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, inhibition of the key FAO rate limiting 105 

enzyme carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A) exacerbates fibrosis, whereas enhancement 106 

of FAO ameliorates disease severity (12, 13). These metabolic adaptations are not merely 107 

consequences but drivers of fibrogenesis, offering compelling targets for therapeutic modulation. 108 

While the fibrotic phenotype of CD shares histological and cellular features of fibrosis in other 109 

organ systems, the metabolic regulation of myofibroblast activation and fibrosis in CD remains 110 

poorly understood. In this study, we employed a multi-omic approach to uncover evidence of 111 

dysregulated fatty acid oxidation (FAO) in fibrotic CD. These findings support a model in which 112 

altered cellular metabolism contributes to myofibroblast activation. Targeting these metabolic 113 

vulnerabilities may offer new therapeutic strategies to mitigate fibrotic complications in CD . 114

> 119

The metabolomic study cohort included 59 patients, comprising 38 individuals with Crohn’s 120 

disease (CD) and 21 non–inflammatory bowel disease (NIBD) controls (table 1). CD patients were 121 

younger than NIBD controls (43 ± 15 vs 59 ± 16 years, P = 0.0002), while sex and smoking status 122 

were similar between groups. Nearly half of CD patients exhibited stricturing disease behavior. 123 

Detailed clinical characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table 1. 124

Adult tissues represented patients with an already manifest clinical phenotype. To understand 196 

potential prognostic value of altered FAO programs in FCD we then asked whether a similar 197 

transcriptional program is already evident at diagnosis in children who later develop stricturing 198 

disease. In this cohort, all ileal biopsies were obtained at treatment naïve diagnosis before the 199 

patient had developed a stricture, and individuals were prospectively classified as non-FCD or 200 

FCD based on longitudinal follow up (Figure 3C)(14). Despite being sampled prior to stricture 201 

formation, children who later progressed to FCD already showed marked down regulation of 202 

FAO genes CPT1A , CPT2 and SLC25A20 relative to non IBD, with non FCD patients again 203

regulatory node tracks strictly with fibrosis risk. 209

activation of glycolytic and fibrogenic pathways, with early emergence in patients destined for 212

Impaired Mitochondrial Fatty Acid Trafficking in Fibrotic Crohn’s Disease 275 

We sought to spatiotemporally resolve neutral lipid accumulation by examining fatty acid 276 

trafficking into mitochondria of intestinal myofibroblasts. Myofibroblasts were pulsed with 277 

BODIPY-C16, then transferred to fresh, unlabeled media to allow for intracellular redistribution 278 

(Figure 6A). Mitochondria and nuclei were respectively counterstained, and cells were visualized 279 

by confocal imaging (representative staining in Figure 6B). 280 

The degree of colocalization (fraction of mitochondria label overlapping with BODIPY-C16 FA 281 

signal) was calculated using Manders’ M1 coefficient and Pearson’s correlation coefficient, 282 

which reflects the extent to which mitochondria and BODIPY-C16 FA intensities align on a 283 

pixel-by-pixel basis. Both measures demonstrated progressive reductions in colocalization from 284 

NIBD to non-FCD to FCD myofibroblasts. Manders’ coefficient was significantly lower in both 285 

non-FCD and FCD compared to NIBD myofibroblasts (Figure 6C), suggesting impaired FFA 286 

utilization in mitochondria. Similarly, Pearson’s correlation coefficient was significantly reduced 287 

in FCD relative to both NIBD and non-FCD (Figure 6D), indicating decreased spatial correlation 288 

of mitochondrial and FA signals. 289 

These findings suggest that mitochondrial fatty acid trafficking is impaired in fibrotic 290 

myofibroblasts, aligning with prior observations of altered lipid accumulation and reduced FAO 291 

gene expression. 292

Discussion 316 

> 317

Crohn’s disease (CD) is a chronic relapsing inflammatory disorder of the gastrointestinal tract with 318 

rising global incidence and disease burden (16, 17). Despite the advent of biologic therapies, 319 

including anti-TNF agents, anti-integrins, IL-12/23 inhibitors, and more recently JAK inhibitors, 320 

response rates remain variable, and a substantial proportion of patients experience treatment failure 321 

and ultimately require surgical intervention (18, 19). Failure may result from refractory 322 

inflammation, but increasingly recognized is the progression of complications independent of 323 

active inflammation, including fistulas and fibrostenotic strictures(20–23). Notably, even patients 324 

who achieve clinical or endoscopic remission may continue to develop stricturing disease, 325 

highlighting a critical limitation of current therapies(20, 21). Most available treatments target the 326 

immune component of CD, yet little is known about the non-immune mechanisms that drive tissue 327 

remodeling and fibrosis(24). Importantly, no approved therapies currently exist to prevent or 328 

reverse intestinal fibrosis(19, 22). This unmet clinical need underscores the importance of 329 

understanding the non-immune drivers of fibrotic complications. In this multi-omic study, we 330 

identify alterations in cellular metabolism as a potential mechanistic contributor to fibrostenotic 331 

disease. 332 

> 333

To explore novel disease pathways, we employed an untargeted metabolomic approach to identify 334 

metabolic perturbations in CD. Using non-inflamed colon tissue from CD and non-IBD (NIBD) 335 

controls, we observed striking enrichment of triacylglycerols (TAGs) and acylcarnitines in CD 336 

samples. These metabolite profiles suggest disrupted lipid handling, raising the possibility of 337 

impaired fatty acid oxidation (FAO), as both TAGs and acylcarnitines serve as key precursors 338 

(25). Although dietary intake and gut microbial composition may influence tissue lipid 339 

composition, demographic and clinical features did not differ significantly between CD and NIBD 340 

controls. Dysbiosis, commonly observed in CD and known to alter microbial-derived lipid 341

metabolites, may partially account for these differences(26). Additionally, dietary factors are also 342 

recognized modulators of host lipid profiles and influence gut microbiota (27, 28). FAO plays a 343 

central role in cellular homeostasis and is increasingly implicated in inflammation and tissue 344 

remodeling in multiple organ systems (29, 30). However, the importance of FAO in cellular 345 

phenotype prompted further transcriptomic investigation to determine whether intrinsic defects in 346 

lipid metabolism underlie CD and disease progression. 347 

> 348

To further investigate the metabolic alterations suggested by our lipidomic data, we examined the 349 

expression of genes critical for fatty acid oxidation (FAO), including CPT1A , CPT2 , and CACT ,350 

which coordinate long-chain fatty acid transport and mitochondrial oxidation(25). Bulk RNA 351 

sequencing of a matched adult colonic cohort revealed reduced expression of CPT2 in CD samples 352 

compared to non-IBD (NIBD) controls. Given the ileum’s predilection for fibrostenotic 353 

complications, we next profiled FAO gene expression in adult ileal samples and observed a broader 354 

pattern of downregulation. Notably, CACT expression was reduced in both non-FCD and FCD 355 

relative to NIBD, with no significant difference between the two CD phenotypes. A similar trend 356 

was observed for CPT2 . These findings suggest that FAO suppression may be a general feature of 357 

Crohn’s disease, but this later-stage cohort may not effectively distinguish fibrotic versus non-358 

fibrotic phenotypes. To assess whether FAO suppression is more specific to fibrostenotic disease, 359 

we analyzed treatment-naive pediatric ileal samples stratified by phenotype. In this earlier-stage 360 

cohort, we observed selective downregulation of CACT and CPT2 in FCD samples compared to 361 

both non-FCD and NIBD controls, while CPT1A was more strongly reduced in FCD than in non-362 

FCD relative to NIBD. These results suggest that early FAO dysregulation may contribute to 363 

fibrostenotic pathogenesis. In parallel, we observed increased expression of glycolytic regulators 364 

(HK1, HK2, PFKFB3, PDK1 ) (6, 10), reduced expression of PPARγ and its downstream targets 365 

(PLIN2, FABP4 )(31, 32), and skewing of the TGF-β pathway with decreased SMAD7 , ID1 , and 366 

ID3 alongside increased THBS1 , SNAI1 , NOX4 , and MMP2 (33–38) . These results suggest that 367 

early metabolic reprogramming involving both suppression of FAO and compensatory induction 368 

of glycolysis, coupled with loss of PPARγ restraint and enhanced TGF-β signaling may contribute 369 

to fibrostenotic pathogenesis. 370 

> 371

To identify the cellular origin of these metabolic changes, we focused on the stromal compartment 372 

the primary site of fibrotic remodeling and leveraged single-cell RNA sequencing data from ileal 373 

resections of CD patients stratified by disease involvement.(15) As expected, COL1A1 expression, 374 

a key driver of extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, was elevated in fibroblasts and smooth 375 

muscle cells from strictured regions, consistent with their roles in fibrosis. FAP, another marker 376 

of activated intestinal fibroblasts, was similarly enriched in strictured fibroblasts(39). We also 377 

found that fibroblasts exhibited reduced expression of SMAD7 , ID1 , and ID3 , along with increased 378 

SNAI1 , further highlighting their bias toward TGF-β–driven activation. Notably, we observed 379 

decreased expression of CPT1A specifically in fibroblasts from strictured tissue compared to those 380 

from uninflamed and inflamed regions, along with a similar trend for CACT . These findings 381 

highlighted fibroblasts as candidate effectors of FAO dysfunction in FCD and motivated direct 382 

functional interrogation. 383 

> 384

To functionally validate our transcriptomic observations, we isolated and cultured primary 385 

intestinal fibroblasts from the terminal ileum of NIBD, non-FCD, and FCD patients. Under 386 

standard culture conditions, these cells spontaneously differentiated into myofibroblasts.(40) 387

using a fluorescent pulse-chase assay(41). Cultured myofibroblasts were first incubated with 395 

BODIPY-C16, a fluorescent fatty acid analog, followed by mitochondrial labeling with PKmito 396 

Deep Red. Colocalization analysis revealed reduced overlap between fatty acid and mitochondrial 397 

signals in FCD-derived cells compared to non-FCD and NIBD controls. This decrease in 398 

colocalization suggests impaired trafficking of long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria for 399 

oxidation. Importantly, these defects were observed under standardized culture conditions, 400 

implying a persistent metabolic phenotype independent of exogenous inflammatory cues. 401 

> 402

Consistent with these metabolic defects, we next evaluated whether impaired FAO in Crohn’s 403 

disease fibroblasts alters their responsiveness to PPARγ agonism during TGFβ-induced activation. 404 

Across all CD-derived fibroblasts (isolated from fibrotic or non-fibrotic lesions) examined, 405 

rosiglitazone showed markedly reduced ability to suppress TGFβ-induced αSMA expression 406 

compared to NIBD controls, indicating diminished PPARγ sensitivity. While our results focused 407 

on the overall CD versus NIBD distinction, internal heterogeneity was observed across CD 408 

samples with fibroblasts derived from fibrotic strictures displaying the least responsiveness to 409 

PPARγ agonism, suggesting that disease stage or prior in vivo conditioning may imprint a more 410 

refractory phenotype. These observations align with the concept of a stably reprogrammed, FAO-411 

deficient myofibroblast state in CD and further support a model in which metabolic dysfunction 412 

contributes functionally to impaired resolution of fibroblast activation. 413 

> 414

Reduced FAO has emerged as a unifying metabolic signature across fibrotic diseases involving 415 

the lung(10), liver(42), kidney(43), heart(44), and skin(45). In these contexts, the profibrotic 416 

cytokine transforming growth factor beta (TGFβ) plays a central role by driving fibroblast-to-417 

myofibroblast differentiation and promoting myofibroblast activity, including extracellular matrix 418 

(ECM) deposition, contractility, and tissue remodeling.(46, 47) Mechanistically, TGFβ suppresses 419 

FAO in part through downregulation of PPARγ, a nuclear transcription factor essential for lipid 420 

metabolism and mitochondrial function.(35, 48) Loss of PPARγ activity impairs mitochondrial 421 

oxidative capacity, promotes intracellular lipid accumulation, and promotes differentiation to 422 

myofibroblasts and activation of the myofibroblast phenotype.(49, 50) In both in vitro and in vivo 423 

models of pulmonary(49), cardiac(51, 52), and dermal fibrosis(50, 53), PPARγ agonists have been 424 

shown to inhibit myofibroblast differentiation and ameliorate fibrotic remodeling.(29, 54) While 425 

this TGFβ–PPARγ–FAO regulatory axis is well-established in other organ systems, its relevance 426 

to intestinal fibrosis and CD, has remained largely unexplored. Our findings provide new evidence 427 

that this metabolic-fibrotic circuitry may also contribute to fibrostenotic progression in the context 428 

of CD. 429 

> 430

Our data suggest that impaired FAO is not merely a consequence of fibrosis but may precede and 431 

contribute to its development. Early suppression of FAO-related genes in treatment-naive pediatric 432 

FCD samples points to metabolic reprogramming as a potential driver of fibrotic remodeling. The 433

metabolism, while loss of PPARγ activity and enhanced TGF-β signaling provide parallel routes 435 

to promote fibroblast activation. These findings suggest that once fibroblasts acquire a fully 436 

differentiated myofibroblast phenotype, they may become refractory to PPARγ-mediated 437

> 439

This observation aligns with the budding concept of “fibrotic memory,” wherein activated 440 

myofibroblasts maintain a stable transcriptional and mechanical phenotype even after the removal 441 

of profibrotic cues. Fibroblasts have been shown to retain “mechanical memory” that persists 442 

despite changes to exogenous cues, contributing to sustained activation.(40) Increasing evidence 443 

suggests that this memory is not solely mechanical but epigenetically reinforced. For example, 444 

studies in cardiac(55), kidney(56), and dermal fibroblasts(57) have demonstrated that persistent 445 

DNA methylation and altered chromatin accessibility maintain the expression of fibrotic genes 446 

even after the withdrawal of fibrotic stimuli such as TGFβ. Additionally, mechanical cues have 447 

also been shown to induce durable memory via histone modifications and noncoding RNAs, which 448 

may be reversible through targeted epigenetic therapies, such as histone deacetylase inhibitors 449 

(HDACi)(58). Consistent with this, preclinical studies have demonstrated encouraging antifibrotic 450 

potential of HDACi, although use has not extended to human trials yet.(59, 60) The persistent 451 

suppression of FAO in ex vivo FCD-derived myofibroblasts supports the existence of a stably 452 

reprogrammed fibrotic state in CD—potentially maintained through similar epigenetic 453 

mechanisms. 454 

> 455

Together, these findings support the positioning of FAO impairment as a central metabolic 456 

signature of fibrotic myofibroblasts in CD. Clinically, this raises the possibility that FAO gene 457 

expression profiling in tissue or blood could serve as a biomarker for fibrotic risk. Notably, 458 

emerging epidemiologic evidence suggests that therapies influencing lipid metabolism may 459 

already modify disease course in IBD, as statin use has been associated with reduced progression 460 

to more severe disease phenotypes in a large nationwide cohort study(61). Moreover, early 461 

intervention to preserve FAO may prevent fibroblast activation and fibrosis progression. Although 462 

PPARγ agonists have demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models(49, 62–64), their translational 463 

success in human disease has been limited, highlighting the importance of therapeutic timing and 464 

disease stage.(60) Our findings also resonate with recent reports linking creeping fat and 465 

fibrostenotic progression in CD, which suggest that mesenteric fat-derived lipid flux may further 466 

interact with stromal metabolic reprogramming to promote stricturing disease(65). Strategies 467 

aimed at reversing fibrotic memory or restoring FAO in established myofibroblasts may offer new 468 

avenues for antifibrotic therapy in CD. 469 

> 470

Our study has several limitations. While the use of primary human samples and integrative multi-471 

omic analysis strengthens the validity of our conclusions, in vitro studies do not fully recapitulate 472 

the complex in vivo microenvironment. Fibroblast behavior is influenced by interactions with 473 

immune, epithelial, and endothelial cells, which were not captured in our study. Although our data 474 

suggests a role for FAO dysfunction in myofibroblast activation, the causal contribution of altered 475 

FAO to intestinal fibrosis requires validation in preclinical animal models. Future studies 476 

incorporating co-culture systems and in vivo perturbation of FAO pathways will be essential to 477 

determine the functional consequences of metabolic reprogramming in tissue remodeling and 478 

fibrosis progression. 479

In summary, our study identifies FAO dysfunction as a defining feature of fibrotic intestinal 481 

myofibroblasts in CD. Through a multi-omic approach integrating metabolomics, transcriptomics, 482 

and functional imaging, we uncover potentially targetable metabolic vulnerability in the fibrotic 483 

stroma. These findings lay the foundation for future translational studies aimed at intercepting 484 

intestinal fibrosis in CD via metabolic reprogramming. 485
