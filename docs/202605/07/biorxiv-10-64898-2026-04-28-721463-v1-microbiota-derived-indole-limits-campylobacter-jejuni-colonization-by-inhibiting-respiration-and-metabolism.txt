Abstract 38 

How the microaerophilic Campylobacter jejuni grows in the inflamed gut is poorly understood, 39 

hampering our ability to identify novel targets for controlling infections by this pathogen. Our 40 

prior ferret study indicated that C. jejuni growth during infection is driven by intestinal 41 

inflammation. Without manipulating microbiota or introducing pro-inflammatory genetic lesions 42 

(or both), conventional mice are naturally resistant to C. jejuni colonization and infection. To test 43 

the impact of inflammation per se on C. jejuni infection, we induced transient intestinal 44 

inflammation in mice using short-term dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) treatment. The resulting 45 

colitis disrupted colonization resistance, enabling rapid C. jejuni growth in the murine colon within 46 

three days of infection, accompanied by exacerbated intestinal inflammation. DSS-induced colitis 47 

led to enrichment of mucin-degrading bacteria and depletion of taxa producing short-chain fatty 48 

acids and indole; metabolomic profiling confirmed a marked reduction in colonic indole levels in 49 

both DSS-treated and infected mice. At physiological concentrations, indole inhibited C. 50 

jejuni growth in vitro and resulted in reduced transcript levels from key energy-generating 51 

pathways, including nitrate respiration ( napA ), aerobic respiration ( ccoN ), lactate utilization ( lctP ), 52 

and the acetate switch ( ackA/ptaA ); consistent with this, mutations in these pathways led to fitness 53 

defects in DSS-treated mice, highlighting their importance for C. jejuni colonization in the 54 

inflamed gut. Moreover, treatment with indole or the indole-producing probiotic Escherichia coli 55 

Nissle 1917 significantly reduced C. jejuni colonization in vivo . Our findings show how C. jejuni 56 

establishes and grows in the inflamed gut and demonstrate that microbiota-derived metabolites 57 

play a key role in regulating C. jejuni pathogenicity. 58 

Significance 59 

The mechanisms by which the microaerophilic pathogen C. jejuni proliferates in the inflamed 60 

intestine remain poorly understood. Using a DSS-induced colitis mouse model, we identify host 61 

physiological changes, microbiota alterations, and metabolic factors that promote C. jejuni 62 

colonization during intestinal inflammation. These findings provide mechanistic insight into 63 

why Campylobacter species are frequently detected in patients with Inflammatory Bowel Disease 64 

(IBD) and how infection can exacerbate intestinal inflammation and worsen disease symptoms. 65 

We further identify the microbiota-derived metabolite indole as a potent inhibitor of C. jejuni 66 

colonization, highlighting a potential metabolite-based therapeutic strategy to combat emerging 67 

multidrug-resistant C. jejuni infections. 68 

Introduction 69 

Campylobacter jejuni , a Gram-negative foodborne pathogen, is a leading cause of gastroenteritis 70 

in humans, primarily transmitted via contaminated food products (1). The emergence of multidrug-71 

resistant strains highlights the urgent need to understand the molecular mechanisms underlying C. 72 

jejuni pathogenesis (2). A central question in this field is how C. jejuni adapts and proliferates in 73 

the face of host inflammation. 74

We demonstrated that the ferret is a physiologically relevant model of C. jejuni disease and 75 

determined that the pathogen exploits intestinal inflammation to grow in the gut (3). During acute 76 

infection in this model, C. jejuni triggered pronounced crypt hyperplasia in the colon, consistent 77 

with activation of epithelial repair programs. This response was accompanied by goblet cell 78 

depletion and a marked increase in undifferentiated, proliferating (Ki67+) epithelial cells. We also 79 

observed increased epithelial oxygenation and metabolic remodeling, including elevated colonic 80 

luminal L-lactate, which supported bacterial growth (3). Although highly informative, the ferret 81 

model is constrained by high cost, limited tractability, and the lack of robust genetic tools to dissect 82 

host determinants of C. jejuni proliferation. 83 

Mice, in contrast, offer a well-characterized immune system, genetic tractability, and affordability. 84 

However, to support C. jejuni colonization and growth, conventional mouse models typically 85 

require genetic modifications, such as IL-10 deficiency, or manipulation of the microbiota, 86 

complicating studies of host-pathogen interactions (4–6). Based on our observations using ferrets 87 

to study C. jejuni infection, we hypothesized that inducing an intestinal inflammatory environment 88 

in mice would permit C. jejuni colonization and growth without genetic or antibiotic interventions. 89 

In this study, we applied short-term treatment with dextran sodium sulfate (DSS), a widely used 90 

inflammatory agent in murine models of colitis and irritable bowel syndrome, leading to increases 91 

in epithelial oxygenation and luminal lactate levels (7–9). Following DSS treatment, mice were 92 

orally infected with C. jejuni , resulting in successful colonization and growth, demonstrating that 93 

inflammation alone is sufficient to facilitate this outcome. We further characterized the DSS-94 

treated mouse model by profiling gut microbiota and metabolites. Notably, DSS treatment reduced 95 

levels of indole-producing commensals, leading to a significant decrease in luminal indole. Further 96 

analysis revealed that indole directly inhibits C. jejuni growth and colonization by disrupting 97 

bacterial respiration and metabolic pathways, thereby highlighting a previously unappreciated 98 

microbiota-mediated mechanism of colonization resistance. Together, these findings establish a 99 

physiologically relevant and genetically tractable mouse model of C. jejuni colonization driven by 100 

inflammation. This model provides a robust platform to dissect host, microbial, and metabolic 101 

factors that regulate pathogen growth and offers insights into how C. jejuni exploits inflammatory 102 

niches to overcome microbiota-mediated resistance. 103 

Results: 104 

Intestinal inflammation promotes robust C. jejuni growth in the murine colon. 105 

Intestinal inflammation promotes the outgrowth of several facultative enteric bacteria, 106 

including Salmonella Typhimurium, E. coli , and Citrobacter rodentium , in the murine gut (10– 107 

12). However, whether inflammation similarly enhances colonization by the microaerophilic 108 

pathogen C. jejuni remains poorly understood and our work with a ferret model led us to consider 109 

this possibility. 110

We performed an infection experiment using four groups of female mice (n = 5 per group). Two 111 

groups were treated with 3% DSS in drinking water for three days to induce intestinal 112 

inflammation, while the remaining two groups received normal drinking water as controls. On the 113 

following day, one DSS-treated group and one control group were orally infected with C. 114 

jejuni WT 11168 strain (10⁸ CFU/mL). After infection, DSS was withdrawn and all mice were 115 

returned to normal drinking water (Fig. 1A) .116 

At day one post-infection, fecal loads of C. jejuni were approximately 10⁸ CFU/g in both DSS-117 

treated and untreated infected groups. However, by day three, fecal C. jejuni levels gradually 118 

declined in DSS-untreated infected mice, whereas DSS-treated infected mice showed an increase 119 

to approximately 10⁹-10 10 CFU/g (Fig. 1B) . Because peak colonization in human infection is 120 

typically observed two-to-three days post-infection in the large intestine, we next quantified C. 121 

jejuni loads in the colon at day three. We detected approximately 10⁹–10¹⁰ CFU C. jejuni /g of colon 122 

tissue in DSS-treated infected mice (Fig. 1C). In contrast, C. jejuni was undetectable or below the 123 

limit of detection in DSS-untreated infected mice, as well as in both uninfected control groups. 124 

These results indicate that DSS-induced inflammation promotes C. jejuni growth in the mouse 125 

colon by day three post-infection. The highest colonization was observed in the colon and cecum 126 

(~10⁹ CFU/g tissue), whereas the small intestine showed lower levels (~10⁶ CFU/g tissue) at day 127 

three post-infection (FigS1A) . Because C. jejuni levels were highest in the colon, we examined its 128 

localization within colonic tissue. Immunohistochemistry with an anti-Campylobacter antibody 129 

revealed sparse clusters of C. jejuni in the colonic lumen, closely associated with the colonic 130 

epithelium and detectable within crypts. This localization pattern resembles what has been reported 131 

during both human and ferret infection (Fig. 1D) (3, 13). C. jejuni colonized across the colon, with 132 

no significant difference between proximal and distal segments (~10⁷ CFU/g tissue) (FigS1B) . We 133 

also examined whether colonization was sex-dependent by comparing male and female mice and 134 

found no significant difference between sexes (FigS1C) .135 

We evaluated the inflammatory response following DSS treatment and C. jejuni infection. H&E 136 

staining and histopathological scoring showed a moderate inflammatory response from DSS 137 

treatment alone, characterized by disruption of the epithelial cell layer, leukocyte infiltration into 138 

the superficial lamina propria, cryptic edema and cryptic hyperplasia in colonic tissue compared 139 

to the two control groups. Infection with C. jejuni in DSS-treated mice triggered a more severe 140 

inflammatory response, with significantly higher pathological scores and more pronounced tissue 141 

damage compared to DSS treatment alone (Fig. 1D) .142

Infection with C. jejuni in the ferret induces significant epithelial differentiation and 158 

proliferation(3). To determine whether DSS-induced inflammation and C. jejuni colonization 159 

results in similar hyperplasia in the mouse, we quantified goblet cells and Ki67⁺ proliferative cells 160 

in colonic tissue at day three post-infection. DSS treatment alone significantly reduced the number 161 

of Alcian blue–positive goblet cells per crypt, with an approximately two-fold reduction relative 162 

to untreated uninfected controls (Fig. 2B) . This effect was enhanced after C. jejuni infection, with 163 

the DSS-treated, infected mice exhibiting approximately three-fold reduction in goblet cells per 164 

crypt compared with controls, indicating a significant loss of mucus-producing, terminally 165 

differentiated cells during infection (Fig. 2B) .166 

We next assessed epithelial proliferation using Ki67 immunohistochemistry. DSS treatment alone 167 

increased by almost two-fold the number of Ki67⁺ cells per crypt compared to untreated uninfected 168 

mice, consistent with activation of epithelial repair. The Ki67⁺ cell numbers were further increased 169 

to approximately three-fold after C. jejuni infection of DSS-treated mice, demonstrating that 170 

infection amplifies crypt hyperplasia and expansion of proliferating epithelial cells (Fig. 2B). This 171 

phenotype closely mirrors the epithelial remodeling response we previously observed during C. 172 

jejuni infection in ferrets (3). 173 

A major consequence of epithelial differentiation state is its impact on oxygen consumption and 174 

oxygen availability at the mucosal surface(10). Mature colonocytes metabolize microbially 175 

derived butyrate through β-oxidation, a process that consumes oxygen and contributes to 176 

maintenance of physiological hypoxia in the intestinal lumen(10). In contrast, undifferentiated 177 

proliferating epithelial cells, enriched at the base of crypts, lack robust β-oxidation and rely on 178 

aerobic glycolysis, producing lactate from glucose. Because these proliferating cells consume less 179 

oxygen, crypt bases are relatively more oxygenated than the upper crypt regions, and expansion of 180 

undifferentiated epithelial populations is predicted to increase epithelial oxygenation overall(10). 181 

Because DSS treatment and C. jejuni infection both increased Ki67⁺ proliferating cells and 182 

reduced terminally differentiated goblet cells, we hypothesized that these conditions disrupt 183 

physiological epithelial hypoxia and lead to elevated epithelial oxygenation during acute infection. 184 

To test this, we used pimonidazole staining, a well-established in vivo hypoxia probe. Under 185 

hypoxic conditions, pimonidazole is reduced to reactive intermediates that form stable adducts 186

Mice were injected intraperitoneally with pimonidazole one hour before euthanasia. Mice without 189

state conditions. Uninfected, DSS-treated mice showed reduced pimonidazole staining, indicating 192

showed near-complete loss of pimonidazole signal, consistent with severe disruption of epithelial 194 

hypoxia and increased epithelial oxygenation (Fig. 2C) . These results demonstrate that DSS-195 

induced colitis partially disrupts epithelial hypoxia, and that C. jejuni infection further exacerbates 196 

this loss, consistent with our previous observations linking C. jejuni infection to increased 197 

epithelial oxygenation (3). 198 

We observed that C. jejuni can reproducibly colonize and grow in the murine intestinal tract after 199 

DSS treatment. As a proof of principle to determine whether this DSS inflammation model can be 200 

used to identify bacterial fitness factors, we performed an in vivo competition assay using the wild-201 

type strain and a mutant deficient for the genes ( motAB ) encoding the established fitness 202 

determinant motAB , which regulates motility and is critical for colonization in other models(17). 203 

Following DSS treatment, mice were orally inoculated with a 1:! mixture of wild-type C. jejuni 204 

11168 and a motAB::kan derivative at a total dose of 10⁸ CFU. Colonization was assessed in the 205 

colon at day three post-infection. In this competitive setting, the motAB::kan mutant exhibited a 206 

dramatic colonization defect compared with the wild-type strain, demonstrating that motility 207 

provides a strong fitness advantage in the inflamed gut environment (Fig. 2D) . These results 208 

validate the DSS-treated mouse model as a robust system for identifying C. jejuni fitness factors 209 

required for colonization during intestinal inflammation. 210 

Overall, our findings demonstrate that DSS treatment disrupts intestinal homeostasis by inducing 211 

epithelial injury and mucosal inflammation, overcoming colonization resistance and creating a 212 

permissive niche for C. jejuni colonization and growth. This model shows that transient 213 

inflammatory perturbation alone is sufficient to render conventional mice susceptible to robust C. 214 

jejuni growth without the need for prior antibiotic treatment or genetic manipulation of the host. 215 

We also observed that C. jejuni further exacerbates intestinal inflammation following DSS 216 

exposure, amplifying mucosal pathogenicity and pro-inflammatory responses. 217

We first evaluated within-sample microbial diversity using alpha diversity metrics, including 231 

observed richness, Shannon diversity index, and Chao1. Across all three measures, we observed 232

substantially alter overall microbial richness or evenness at day three. suggesting that the ability 234 

of DSS-treated mice to support C. jejuni growth is not driven by a global loss of microbial 235 

diversity. 236

Analysis of beta diversity using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, which accounts for relative taxon 238 

abundance, and Jaccard distance, which is based on presence or absence of taxons, revealed 239 

significant differences in microbial community composition among experimental groups. 240 

Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) derived from both metrics demonstrated clear separation of 241 

DSS-treated mice, whether infected or uninfected, from untreated controls (Fig. 3A,B & SFig.2 242

whereas C. jejuni colonization does not significantly alter overall community composition beyond 244 

DSS-induced changes. 245

Differential abundance analysis (ANCOM-BC) using untreated controls as the reference revealed 247 

pronounced microbiota changes following DSS treatment, again indicating that DSS-induced 248 

dysbiosis is the primary driver of microbial remodeling in this model. In DSS-treated 249 

mice, Akkermansia and Meridibacter were significantly enriched, likely accounting for increased 250 

mucin degradation and altered gut barrier interactions ( SFig.2 D) (20, 21). DSS-treated C. jejuni 251 

infected groups showed a similar pattern, with enrichment of Akkermansia and Meridibacter and 252 

deeper depletion of SCFA producers. Although most of the changes we identified were correlated 253 

to DSS treatment, we note that Ruminiclostridium_E and members of the Muribaculaceae family 254 

were significantly enriched only in the DSS-treated infection group, suggesting infection-specific 255

We conclude that DSS treatment induced a dysbiotic microbiota marked by expanding populations 276 

of mucin-degrading organisms and depletion of SCFA- and indole-producing obligate anaerobes, 277 

thereby creating a gut environment permissive for C. jejuni colonization, with reduced microbial 278 

support for intestinal homeostasis, while infection primarily amplified changes in select taxa rather 279 

than generating a distinct community configuration. 280

Because several indole-producing bacterial taxa were significantly reduced by DSS in both 282 

infected and uninfected groups, we hypothesized that luminal indole levels would be 283 

correspondingly diminished in these mice. To test this, we quantified indole and L-tryptophan 284 

concentrations in colonic samples by LC/MS mass spectrometry, as gut microbiota convert L-285 

tryptophan into indole and related derivatives through tryptophan metabolism. Consistent with the 286 

loss of indole-producing taxa, indole levels were significantly decreased in both DSS-treated 287 

uninfected and infected groups compared with untreated controls, whereas tryptophan levels 288 

showed a relative accumulation, suggesting impaired microbial conversion of tryptophan to indole 289 

(Fig. 3D,E) .290 

The aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) is a key transcription factor that regulates epithelial barrier 291 

integrity, IL-22, IL-10 expression, and mucosal immune homeostasis (29–31). Given reduced 292 

levels of the AhR ligand indole, we assessed AhR transcriptional expression in colonic tissues by 293 

RT PCR. Ahr mRNA levels were significantly reduced in both DSS-treated groups, with an 294 

approximately four-fold reduction in DSS-treated uninfected mice and an approximately eight-295 

fold reduction in DSS-treated infected mice compared with untreated controls ( Fig. 3F) .296

in both DSS-treated groups. Indole levels were significantly decreased, accompanied by reduced 299 

expression of AhR and IL-10. Because AhR activation promotes IL-10–mediated mucosal 300 

tolerance(31), and IL-10 impacts C. jejuni colonization, diminished indole availability may 301 

facilitate C. jejuni pathogenicity through impaired immune regulation. It may also result in loss 302

Microbiota derived indole inhibits C. jejuni growth in vitro and in vivo 305 

Indole is microbiota-derived tryptophan metabolite that accumulates in the colon at physiological 306 

concentrations (~0.25–1 mM) (32). At these levels, it supports intestinal homeostasis by 307 

strengthening epithelial barrier function as a ligand of AhR, regulating mucosal immunity, and 308 

exerting antimicrobial activity by disrupting membrane potential and respiration (32, 35). Given 309 

that indole levels were markedly reduced following DSS treatment and during C. jejuni infection, 310 

we investigated whether indole directly influences C. jejuni growth, as its effect on this pathogen 311 

remains poorly defined. For this, wild-type C. jejuni was cultured in Mueller–Hinton broth 312 

supplemented with physiologically relevant concentrations of indole (0.25–1 mM), with DMSO 313 

serving as the vehicle control, and bacterial growth was assessed by CFU enumeration over a 30-314 

hour time course. 315 

There was little effect on C. jejuni growth in 0.25 mM indole, while significant growth reduction 316 

was detected as early as six hours in 1 mM indole, and by nine hours in 0.5 mM indole ( Fig. 4A) .. 317 

In these latter two concentrations, bacterial viability progressively declined so that by 30 hour of 318 

cultivation in 1 mM indole C. jejuni exhibited near-total loss of viability, an observation that was 319 

not limited to strain 11168 but was also observed in three additional C. jejuni isolates from 320 

different sources tested under the same conditions (SFig. 3A) . These findings demonstrate that 321 

indole at physiological concentrations inhibits C. jejuni growth in vitro. 322 

To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying indole-mediated growth inhibition, we 323 

performed transcriptomic analysis using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq). Based on our growth 324 

kinetics data, we selected the nine-hour time point for analysis, as a significant growth defect was 325 

first observed at this stage in the presence of 0.5 mM indole, without loss of viability ( Fig. 4A) .326 

Wild-type C. jejuni was cultured in Mueller–Hinton broth with 0.5 mM indole or DMSO control, 327 

and total RNA was isolated at nine hours for transcriptomic profiling. We identified 231 transcripts 328 

with lower abundance and 192 genes with greater abundance in the presence of indole (log 2>1, 329 

P<0.05) ( Fig. 4B) . Several respiratory regulatory gene mRNAs were reduced by more than 330 

twofold, including those involved in aerobic respiration (e.g., ccoNPOQ , encoding a terminal 331 

oxidase), nitrate respiration ( napAGHBLD ), hydrogenase ( hydAB ), and formate hydrogenase 332 

(fdhA and fdhB ) ( Fig. 4C, Supplemental table 1) .333 

C. jejuni lacks a complete glycolytic pathway but possesses a complete citric acid (TCA) cycle, 334 

which serves as its primary metabolic pathway(36). Our data show that transcripts associated with 335 

TCA cycle intermediates ( pycA, gltA, acnB, oorC, fumC, mqo, sucC, frdC, and frdB ) were 336 

significantly less abundant (> 2 fold) in the presence of indole ( Fig. 4D, Supplemental table 1) .337 

The acetate switch in C. jejuni , which converts acetyl-CoA to acetate and represents a major ATP-338 

generating pathway, was also affected: transcripts from both pta and ackA were less abundant by 339

To validate the transcriptomic findings further, we assessed bacterial respiration using an assay 350 

that measures reduction of 2,3,5-triphenyl-tetrazolium chloride (TTC). Wild-type C. jejuni was 351 

grown for nine hours in the presence of indole (0.5mM) or DMSO. Cells were washed with PBS, 352 

normalized to OD₆₀₀ = 1 to ensure equal bacterial numbers, and TTC reduction was quantified by 353 

measuring optical density at 496 nm. Treatment with 0.5 mM indole resulted in approximately 10-354 

fold reduction in TTC reduction relative to DMSO-treated bacteria, indicating marked impairment 355 

of respiratory activity (SFig. 3B) . These data support the transcriptomic findings and demonstrate 356 

that indole inhibits C. jejuni growth, at least in part, by suppressing cellular respiration. 357 

In addition to its effects on respiratory gene transcript abundance, indole resulted lower abundance 358 

of transcripts from genes of the TCA cycle genes and the ackA -pta acetate switch pathway, which 359 

are important for ATP generation and metabolic flux in C. jejuni . To determine whether indole 360 

affects cellular energy status, we measured intracellular ATP levels. Wild type bacteria were grown 361 

for nine hours in different concentrations of indole, normalized to OD₆₀₀ = 1, and intracellular ATP 362 

was quantified using a commercial luminescence-based assay. Consistent with impaired 363 

respiration, 0.5 mM indole caused an approximately three-fold reduction in intracellular ATP levels 364 

compared with controls (SFig. 3C) , consistent with previous findings showing that indole reduces 365 

intracellular ATP levels in Pseudomonas putida (38) . This suggests a metabolic mechanism 366 

underlying indole-mediated growth inhibition. 367 

In our transcriptomic analysis, abundance of mRNA from several oxidative stress regulatory genes 368

Discussion 463 

The gastrointestinal (GI) tract is densely populated by microbial communities that maintain 464 

colonization resistance and protect the host from enteric pathogens(42). Resident microbiota 465 

restrict pathogen growth by limiting nutrient availability and maintaining a hypoxic intestinal 466 

environment, partly through microbiota-derived short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, 467 

metabolism of which preserves epithelial hypoxia(10). Pathogenic bacteria disrupt this barrier by 468 

inducing intestinal inflammation, creating conditions favorable for their growth. Inflammation 469 

remodels the intestinal ecosystem by altering epithelial metabolism, oxygen availability, nutrient 470 

flux, and microbial composition, a mechanism well described for facultative anaerobic 471 

Enterobacteriaceae such as S. Typhimurium and E. coli , which exploit inflammation-associated 472 

electron acceptors and increased oxygen to outcompete obligate anaerobic commensals(10). 473 

In previous work with a ferret infection model, we showed that intestinal inflammation supports 474 

growth of C. jejuni by increasing epithelial oxygenation and shifting metabolism towards one that 475 

elevates lactate levels, which is used as a growth substrate by the pathogen(3). In the current 476 

study we generated a similar inflammatory environment in the mouse using DSS-induced colitis 477 

and demonstrated that transient inflammation alone is sufficient to overcome colonization 478 

resistance and allow robust C. jejuni growth in conventional mice without antibiotic treatment or 479 

host genetic modification. This outcome is consistent with the widely used IL10–deficient mouse 480 

model, in which constitutive intestinal inflammation results in high susceptibility to C. jejuni 481 

colonization; these two models provide further evidence that intestinal inflammation is a key 482 

determinant of C. jejuni fitness and disease progression(4). 483 

Inflammation not only facilitates bacterial growth but is also intensified by infection, establishing 484 

a feed-forward interaction between host inflammatory responses and pathogen fitness. These 485 

findings may help explain clinical observations that Campylobacter species are frequently isolated 486 

from patients with active colitis, and acute enteric infection is known to trigger disease flares in 487 

individuals with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) (43). Additionally, epidemiological studies 488 

have reported that acute Campylobacter infection is associated with an increased long-term risk of 489 

developing IBD (44). 490 

This mouse model provides a simple, reproducible, and cost-effective platform to study C. jejuni 491 

pathogenicity. It is well suited for identifying bacterial physiological traits and fitness determinants 492

In patients with inflammatory bowel disease, particularly ulcerative colitis, epithelial metabolic 495 

changes like those observed in DSS-induced colitis have been reported (7). DSS treatment induces 496 

ER stress–mediated epithelial injury, leading to enhanced repair responses characterized by crypt 497 

elongation, proliferation of transit-amplifying cells, and reduced numbers of terminally 498 

differentiated cells such as goblet cells, resulting in thinning of the mucus layer(45). These 499 

histological changes were also observed in our model and were exacerbated following C jejuni 500 

infection. Terminally differentiated colonocytes typically rely on β-oxidation of fatty acids, 501 

consuming high levels of oxygen, whereas proliferating epithelial cells preferentially use 502 

glycolysis, producing lactate with lower oxygen consumption (10). Consistent with previous 503 

reports showing elevated luminal lactate during colitis, this metabolic shift reduces epithelial 504 

oxygen use and increases oxygen and lactate availability at the mucosal surface (8, 9). In our 505 

model, epithelial oxygenation increased after DSS treatment and further after C. jejuni infection, 506 

suggesting higher oxygen levels in the gut lumen. Lactate is also critical for C. jejuni growth under 507 

inflammatory conditions. Because C. jejuni is a microaerophile requiring low oxygen tension (2– 508 

10%) for respiration, inflammation-associated epithelial oxygenation likely creates a microaerobic 509 

niche while simultaneously increasing lactate availability, providing both an electron acceptor and 510 

a carbon source that support C. jejuni growth in the inflamed intestine. Previous studies have 511 

shown similar growth physiology in S. enterica Typhimurium infection (11). 512 

Host-derived nitrate serves as an alternative electron acceptor for facultative anaerobic bacteria 513 

such as E. coli and S. enterica Typhimurium during intestinal inflammation (10, 12, 39). During 514 

colitis, the host inflammatory response generates reactive nitrogen and oxygen species, and 515 

inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) is elevated, leading to increased nitric oxide production in 516 

the gut. Nitric oxide reacts with superoxide to form peroxynitrite, which can subsequently generate 517 

nitrate, thereby increasing luminal nitrate availability (10, 12). We detected elevated iNOS 518 

expression in both DSS-treated and C. jejuni –infected colonic tissue, suggesting increased nitrate 519 

levels in the inflamed intestine. Additionally, the nitrate reductase mutant showed a competitive 520 

colonization defect, indicating that nitrate respiration contributes to C. jejuni fitness in the inflamed 521 

gut. Overall, our study suggests that luminal lactate availability, epithelial oxygenation, and nitrate 522 

levels are key factors supporting bacterial growth in the inflamed gut. Diet can strongly influence 523 

these parameters; for example, long-term high-fat diets increase epithelial oxygenation and nitrate 524 

availability, thereby promoting growth of Enterobacteriaceae such as Escherichia 525 

coli and Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (46). However, little is known about how 526 

dietary factors affect the pathogenesis of C. jejuni . Our findings suggest that diet-driven changes 527 

in gut metabolism and redox conditions may influence C. jejuni colonization and disease 528 

progression, highlighting an important area for future investigation. 529 

We also identified luminal indole as important in this model. DSS treatment and C. jejuni infection 530 

significantly reduced luminal indole levels, correlating with decreased abundance of indole-531

Although we generated inflammation experimentally leading to an altered, less-indole-producing 538 

microbiota, presumably C. jejuni must overcome indole-mediated colonization resistance during 539 

natural infection to establish disease. Microbiota from humans infected with Campylobacter have 540 

similar changes to what we observed with DSS-treatment in the mouse such as lower levels of 541

about if and how C. jejuni alters the gut microbiota in a way that reduces indole-producing bacteria 543

C. jejuni modulates the intestinal metabolic environment to overcome indole-dependent resistance 545

With increasing emergence of multidrug-resistant C. jejuni strains, alternative therapeutic 547 

strategies are needed (2). Our results suggest that a metabolite-based approach to limit infection 548 

could be successful. Consistent with this idea, recent studies have reported that indole and 549 

synthetic indole-derived compounds exhibit antimicrobial or anti-virulence activity against a broad 550 

range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens, supporting this approach for therapeutic 551 

development against multidrug-resistant bacteria (49, 50). 552 

Collectively, this study reframes C. jejuni pathogenesis within the ecology of the inflamed gut. 553 

Less dependent on virulence determinants such as type III secretion effectors or exotoxins that 554 

commandeer host functions, C. jejuni exploits inflammation-induced oxygenation, nitrate 555 

availability, and glycolytic products to fuel respiratory metabolism. Concurrent depletion of 556 

indole-producing commensals removes a critical metabolic constraint on this growth. By 557 

integrating host metabolic remodeling, microbial community restructuring, and pathogen 558 

bioenergetics, our study provides a unified model explaining how inflammation converts the colon 559 

into a permissive niche for C. jejuni . These findings also highlight microbial metabolite 560 

restoration—particularly indole—as a rational therapeutic strategy to limit pathogen growth 561 

without broadly suppressing inflammation or disrupting the microbiota. 562
