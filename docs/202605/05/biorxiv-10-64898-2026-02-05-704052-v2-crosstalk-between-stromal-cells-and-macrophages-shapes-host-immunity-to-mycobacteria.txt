INTRODUCTION 67 

Mycobacteria  have coevolved  with humans  over  tens of thousands of years , as  exemplified by 68 

signs of tuberculosis (TB) in  approximately 5% of skeletons from a 10,000 -year -old 69 

settlement 1,2 . Mycobacterium tuberculosis  (M.tb ) continues to pose a major global health 70 

threat, causing approximately 1.5 million deaths annually 3. Additionally , the  clinical releva nce 71 

of  environmental  non -tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM)  is  increasingly  recogni zed 4. Their 72 

remarkable adaptability enables mycobacteria to survive for years in the host,  leading  to 73 

chronic disease  with a  wide  spectrum  of clinical manifestations 5. Although  pulmonary  TB is the 74 

most common form of the disease, mycobacteria can infect virtually any organ  including  serous 75 

cavities 6. In  the  tissue, they induce granulomas,  heterocellular structures composed of immune 76 

and non ‑immune cells that evolve over time 7,8 . Granulomas  may be viewed as functional 77 

contain ers  for mycobacteria,  as  they  isolate the pathogens  from  the  surrounding tissue. 78 

However, they also represent  potential  survival niche s and  thus sites of  disease  reactivat ion ,79 

when immune control  declines  (e.g.  during  immunosuppression ). Furthermore, the y are 80 

associated with tissue destruction and fibrotic remodeling, both of which impair clearance of 81 

infection and reconstitution of tissue homeostasis 9.82

approximately 5 -10% of individuals with a positive interferon -gamma release assay may 84

Here, we developed a  model of serous cavity  mycobacterial infection that induces formation 469

bacterial uptake by LPM within minutes, t he immune cell wave  in the first 24 hours  was 474

reaction to bacterial in fection;  on the other hand , in the case of mycobacteria , it  seemed to 476
