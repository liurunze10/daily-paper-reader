ABSTRACT [150 words] 1

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) remain a relapsing, treatment-refractory disorder marked by progressive 2

tissue injury and inflammation despite expanding immune-targeted therapies. We established a prospective 3

cohort integrating stromal biobanking, functional phenotyping, cross-cohort benchmarking, and outcome 4

modeling to define disease-anchored cellular states. Colonic myofibroblasts from 34 individuals spanning health, 5

ulcerative colitis, and Crohn’s disease resolved into two dominant states : inflammatory ( IMFs ) and quiescent 6

(QMFs ) myofibroblasts. IMF predominance at recruitment forecasted progressive disease, increasing odds of 7

worsening endoscopic severity despite therapy escalation by ~4.6 during follow-up, thereby linking early stromal 8

biology to clinical endpoints. Unlike QMFs, IMFs exhibited a senescence-associated secretory phenotype that 9

impaired epithelial stemness, barrier integrity, and innate immune fitness. State-guided prioritization identified 10 

EDNRB-antagonism as a high-confidence stromal intervention, reversing pathogenic phenotypes across 11 

orthogonal assays and species. Outcome simulation positioned stromal-state reversibility by EDNRB-12 

antagonism as a precision axis, reducing odds of recalcitrance by ~96.4% and reframing treatment resistance 13 

as a reversible stromal state. 14

Introduction :1

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD) remain a relapsing, often recalcitrant disorder in which therapeutic 2

resistance, treatment escalation, and progressive tissue injury persist despite expanding immune-targeted 3

therapies 1-5 . A substantial proportion of patients fail to achieve durable remission and progress to develop 4

strictures, fistulas, or require surgery 6-8 , with complication rates largely unchanged in the biologic era 9-11 . In 5

Crohn’s disease (CD), this trajectory commonly culminates in fibrostenotic complications, including strictures, 6

obstruction, fistulas 12 , and repeated resections 4, whereas ulcerative colitis (UC) is characterized by persistent 7

inflammation and mucosal dysfunction 13 . These patterns highlight a central unmet need: to define the cellular 8

programs that drive disease progression and enable earlier, mechanism-based intervention. 9

Although IBD has traditionally been viewed through an immune-centric lens, emerging evidence 10 

implicates the stromal mesenchyme as a key regulator of progression and tissue remodeling 14-16 . Intestinal 11 

fibroblasts and myofibroblasts (MF s) reside at the mucosa–submucosa interface, where they integrate 12 

inflammatory cues with extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, epithelial repair, and immune cell behavior, 13 

functioning as architectural hubs that couple active inflammation to structural damage 16-18 . Single-cell 14 

transcriptomic studies have deconstructed stromal heterogeneity to infinitesimal details, creating exhaustive 15 

catalogs of fibroblast subsets linked to epithelial support, immune modulation, therapy resistance, and fibrosis 18-16

(see Table 1 ). However, these cross-sectional analyses, often performed on fibrotic tissues (end-stage 17 

disease), provide limited insight into precedent stromal states or how such states relate to future disease 18 

trajectory. Whether specific stromal programs actively shape clinical course remains unresolved; this is in part 19 

due to the lack of high-fidelity human new approach methodologies (NAMs) that preserve stromal biology in 20 

culture and inform longitudinal clinically relevant outcomes. 21 

Here, we take a fundamentally different approach. We established a prospective, clinically anchored 22 

stromal biobank in which patient-derived cells were collected early and paired with long-term follow-up. This 23 

approach enables direct linkage of stromal states to disease trajectory. By integrating functional phenotyping 24 

with outcome modeling, we identify discrete stromal programs associated with progression and nominate 25 

actionable targets for intervention. 26

Results: 1

Prospective human cohort reveals two stromal states in IBD 2

We established a prospective human cohort integrating endoscopic tissue diversion, primary stromal biobanking, 3

and longitudinal follow-up (~5 years) to anchor cellular states to clinical outcomes ( Fig. 1a; Supplementary 4

Data 1 ). To minimize selection bias, we implemented a label-free, marker-free outgrowth strategy in which 5

mesenchymal cells migrating from biopsies were expanded as primary fibroblast/myofibroblast (MF) cultures 6

(Fig. 1b ). This approach mirrors widely used enzymatic methods 30,31 while reducing potential perturbations 7

associated with tissue homogenization or dissociation, including cellular stress, reduced viability, and altered 8

surface protein integrity in sensitive cell populations 32 . Flow cytometry confirmed enrichment of CD45 ⁻ stromal 9

cell populations and reproducible segregation into fibroblasts (CD90 ⁺αSMA ⁻) and MFs (CD90 ⁺αSMA ⁺), 10 

benchmarked against commercial controls ( Extended Data Fig. 1a, b ). 11 

Three factors led us to focus on the colon as a clinically and biologically convergent site of IBD: (i) It is 12 

universally involved in UC and frequently affected in CD (~60% of patients); while ~half of those CD patients 13 

have synchronous involvement of the small intestine, the other half show disease that is limited to the colon 33 ;14 

(ii) Our prior work demonstrates that colonic epithelium in IBD segregates into two molecular subtypes, with 15 

surprising convergence of phenome, genome and transcriptome 34 , and that these epithelial programs forecast 16 

disease behavior across intestinal segments (such as complicated course of ileal CD) 35 ; (iii) Others have shown 17 

that colonic stromal, but not their ileal counterparts, exhibit greater discriminatory power of complicated disease 18 

course across intestinal segments (fibrostenotic complication) 28 . This provided a rationale and high-yield 19 

substrate for stromal state discovery in the colon. 20 

Our study design was intentional and forward-looking--by prospectively biobanking stromal cells with 21 

matched long-term outcomes, we sought to: (i) test whether stromal states track with objective disease 22 

trajectories (e.g., endoscopic severity, recalcitrant disease, etc.); (ii) identify actionable nodes capable of altering 23 

those trajectories, and (iii) establish biobanked stromal cultures as faithful human NAMs for therapeutic 24 

discovery. These goals distinguish this effort from prior foundational, but descriptive studies (see Table 1 ). 25 

Unsupervised transcriptomic analysis revealed reproducible bifurcation of stromal identity. Principal 26 

component analysis segregated MFs into two near-equally represented clusters across two independently 27

IMFs define a senescent, metabolically active, immunotolerant stromal niche 10 

We next defined the phenotypic programs underlying IMF versus QMF states and anchored these behaviors to 11 

the transcriptional signatures identified in Fig 1 . Donor-derived stromal lines representing exemplar extremes of 12 

the IMF ↔QMF continuum were subjected to orthogonal phenotyping across early passages to assess stability 13 

and functional divergence ( Fig. 3a ). 14 

Consistent with enrichment of inflammatory and stress-response programs ( Fig. 1e–j ), IMFs exhibited 15 

hallmark features of cellular senescence. Relative to QMFs, IMFs showed increased senescence (elevated β-16 

galactosidase activity; Fig. 3b ) and accumulation of DNA damage (abundance of γ-H2AX foci; Fig. 3c ). This was 17 

accompanied by reduced proliferative capacity (fewer Ki67 ⁺ nuclei, Fig. 3d ) and diminished apoptosis (lower 18 

Annexin V staining; Fig. 3e ). Together, these features define a senescent stromal population that resists turnover 19 

and persists. 20 

We next interrogated metabolic programs, guided by enrichment of PI3K–Akt and stress-response 21 

pathways in IMFs ( Fig. 1e–j ). Seahorse-based flux analyses revealed that IMFs are metabolically hyperactive, 22 

with increased oxidative phosphorylation and glycolytic flux relative to QMFs ( Fig. 3f ; Extended Data Fig. 5a,b ). 23 

This dual activation supports a sustained, energetically competent effector state, consistent with enrichment of 24 

transitional and progenitor-like populations within the IMF axis ( Fig 1l ), rather than terminal quiescence. 25 

Secretome profiling linked transcriptional identity to functional output. Multiplex cytokine analysis 26 

demonstrated robust induction of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) mediators in IMFs, 27

IMF paracrine signaling disrupts epithelial integrity and impairs innate immune function 19 

We next asked whether the IMF–QMF stromal programs translate into functional consequences for neighboring 20 

epithelial and immune compartments. We interrogated stromal–epithelial and stromal–myeloid crosstalk using 21 

conditioned media from donor-derived QMF and IMF cultures (Fig. 4a,d,g ).22 

Exposure of healthy human colonic organoids to IMF-conditioned media resulted in marked loss of 23 

viability, with increased uptake of cell-impermeant dye (eTox Red), indicating epithelial injury ( Fig. 4a–c ). By 24 

contrast, QMF-conditioned media preserved organoid integrity, resembling control conditions. These findings 25 

establish that IMF-derived secretomes are sufficient to drive epithelial damage. Given that 3D patient-derived 26 

organoid (PDO) cultures are enriched for stem-like and transient amplifying cells, these findings align with prior 27

Together, these data establish stromal state composition as a driver of epithelial and innate immune 14

orchestrators of a self-reinforcing cycle of tissue damage and immune failure. 17

EDNRB antagonism reverses IMF-driven pathology and restores epithelial and immune fitness 19 

Because of their potential as drivers of recalcitrant IBD, we next sought to therapeutically target the IMF stromal 20 

axis. Target prioritization was guided by the transcriptional architecture defined in Fig. 1 (Fig. 4j ). Among the 21 

most upregulated genes in IMFs (top 5% differentially expressed genes; ~4.5-fold change; Fig. 1d ;22 

Supplementary Data 2 ), endothelin receptor type B (EDNRB) emerged as a uniquely actionable node. It was 23 

the only druggable target within this tier with an available small-molecule antagonist already in clinical 24 

development and with an established safety profile (ENB-003 66,67 ; NCT04205227 and BQ-788 68 ; NCT02442466). 25 

Importantly, expression pharmacology confirmed increased EDNRB protein in stromal compartments of IMF-26 

predominant patient biopsies as compared to QMF ( Fig. 4k; Extended Data 7) , providing in situ validation. 27

Together, these data position EDNRB as a high-confidence, clinically actionable target within disease-associated 1

stromal signaling. In parallel, gene set enrichment analyses highlighted activation of JAK–STAT signaling in IMFs 2

(Fig. 1f ), providing a clinically relevant benchmark because the JAK inhibitor upadacitinib (UPA) is an FDA-3

approved therapy for moderate-to-severe, treatment-refractory CD and UC, often used as salvage therapy 69-77 .4

We first compared EDNRB as a novel actionable node, and benchmarked readouts in human NAMs 5

against UPA (see workflow, Extended Data Fig. 8a ). EDNRB antagonism (using BQ-788) broadly suppressed 6

IMF-associated secretory programs, reducing SASP cytokines and chemokines across multiplex assays ( Fig. 7

5a; Extended Data Fig. 8b ). This effect extended to the defining IMF chemokine CXCL14, which was 8

significantly reduced at the protein level ( Fig. 5b ). While JAK inhibition attenuated select inflammatory mediators, 9

its effects were partial and context-dependent, in contrast to the more uniform suppression achieved with EDNRB 10 

blockade. For example, while both inhibitors reduced CXCL14, IL1 β and IL8 ( Fig. 5b ), only BQ-788, not UPA 11 

reduced levels of TNF α (Extended Data Fig. 8b ). 12 

We next assessed functional rescue across epithelial and immune readouts. Conditioned media from 13 

BQ-788-treated IMFs markedly reduced epithelial toxicity, restoring organoid viability ( Fig. 5c; Extended Data 14 

Fig. 8c ). Barrier function was similarly rescued, with restoration of TEER and reduction of paracellular 15 

permeability ( Fig. 5d,e; Extended Data Fig. 8d ). In parallel, macrophage antimicrobial function improved, with 16 

enhanced clearance of bacteria following exposure to conditioned media from BQ-788-treated IMFs ( Fig. 5f ). 17 

Across these orthogonal assays, EDNRB antagonism with BQ-788 consistently reversed IMF-driven epithelial 18 

and immune dysfunction, comparable to those observed from JAK inhibition with UPA. 19 

To test therapeutic relevance in vivo , we used a chronic Dextran Sulfate Sodium (DSS)-induced colitis 20 

model 78,79 comprising repeated cycles of DSS exposure and recovery to recapitulate persistent inflammation, 21 

weight loss, and fibrosis seen in human IBD. Intervention timing ( Fig. 5g ) was guided by transcriptionally 22 

resolved stromal state dynamics ( Extended Data Fig. 9a ). IMF and QMF programs were suppressed during 23 

acute injury and emerged during remodeling phases, progressively accumulating with repeated cycles 24 

(Extended Data Fig. 9b ) and paralleling induction of stromal drivers such as CXCL14 and ADAMDEC1 25 

(Extended Data Fig. 9c-d ). By contrast, EDNRB expression peaked during acute inflammation and declined 26 

during remodeling phases ( Extended Data Fig. 9e ), providing a rationale for temporally targeted antagonism. 27

Discussion 1

IBD remains defined by an expansion of the therapeutic options without durable disease modification. Here, we 2

identify stromal state plasticity as a central, targetable axis that links mechanism to outcome. This framework 3

enables prediction, intervention, and reprogramming of disease trajectory. There are three major impacts of this 4

study on the field. 5

6

First, we establish that human stromal states can be prospectively captured, propagated, and 7

experimentally interrogated with high fidelity. By integrating biobanking with longitudinal outcomes, we move 8

beyond static atlases to a system in which stromal programs are stable, reproducible, functionally testable and 9

most importantly, determinants of disease-trajectory. Cross-cohort benchmarking using single-cell transcriptomic 10 

atlas derived digital signatures revealed that these states are conserved across cohorts, preserved in culture, 11 

and spatially encoded within tissue, providing a scalable human NAMs platform for the stromal compartment. 12 

Notably, the binary stromal organization (IMF vs QMF) parallels emerging insights from the epithelial 13 

compartment 34 , where patients also segregate into two dominant molecular subtypes in spite of their clinical and 14 

demographic heterogeneity. However, the axes are distinct: epithelial programs stratify fibrostenotic versus 15 

dysbiotic disease 34 , whereas stromal states segregate remitting versus recalcitrant trajectories. While both 16 

stromal states are observed across UC and CD, epithelial programs are largely restricted to CD 34 , suggesting 17 

that convergence of both epithelial and stromal drivers may underlie the most severe complications in IBD that 18 

are unique to recalcitrant CD, including transmural fibrosis, fistulizing disease and need for repeated surgeries. 19 

Consistent with this, we show that IMFs recapitulate transcriptional programs described in fistulizing CD 12 .20 

Although this study does not resolve how epithelial and stromal subtypes interact, it addresses a central 21 

bottleneck in the field—how to move from single-cell description to causal and therapeutic insight—and positions 22 

human stroma as an experimentally tractable human NAM platforms for basic and translational discovery. 23 

24 

Second, our findings reframe stromal biology as a population-level equilibrium, in which the balance 25 

between inflammatory (IMF) and quiescent (QMF) myofibroblast states governs tissue fate. Although both states 26 

coexist within individual tissues and cultures, one predominates, and the dominant state dictates phenotype and 27

A key candidate linking stromal state to these downstream effects is CXCL14, a defining IMF chemokine 13 

that connects stromal activation to immune modulation, including recruitment of monocytes, NK cells and 14 

dendritic cells 86,87 . CXCL14 also functions as an autocrine factor for fibroblast growth, migration and multi-modal 15 

epithelium stimulatory activities 88 , and is expressed in the epithelium 89 (consistent with our own observations in 16 

mice and patients), suggesting bidirectional stromal–epithelial crosstalk 90 . However, CXCL14 is unlikely to act 17 

alone. We posit that it may account for a subset of the paracrine effects we observe, with the broader phenotype 18 

arising from combinatorial signaling. For example, emerging evidence in hematopoietic 91 and non-19

potential and even increasing vulnerability to ferroptosis through metabolic stress pathways. In parallel, CXCL14-21 

driven recruitment and polarization of macrophages toward tolerant states 93,94 , could impair microbial clearance, 22 

while coordinated engagement of recruited immune effectors 86 may be required to fully manifest barrier 23 

dysfunction. Although its precise causal contribution remains to be defined, the consistent enrichment of CXCL14 24 

in IMFs (here, and in most prior studies; see Table 1 ), together with its detectability in the secretome, positions 25 

it as a measurable biomarker of stromal state and therapeutic response, with the potential to translate gut stromal 26

Third, we translate this biology into a precision therapeutic framework. We showed that JAK inhibition, 2

bona fide target of JAK inhibitors, albeit with incomplete reprogramming of the pathogenic state. These findings 6

mechanistically substantiate and align with observations in rheumatologic diseases 84,96,97 and position JAK 7

IMF state-defining, druggable node within the highest-confidence disease genes, enabling coordinated reversal 9

framework, recalcitrant IBD reflects failure of stromal resolution -- a non-healing wound -- rather than incomplete 15 

immune suppression, suggesting periodic, state-guided EDNRB-targeted intervention during flares (as an add-16 

on therapy) may be both efficient and effective, in contrast to continuous treatment, and may represent a radically 17 

different approach from maintenance therapy.. 18

Study Limitations :1

This study has several limitations that frame the next steps. First, although the prospective cohort is deeply 2

phenotyped with longitudinal follow-up, its size is modest, contributing to wide confidence intervals in outcome 3

modeling. Validation across larger, multi-center and multi-ancestry cohorts is needed to confirm the 4

generalizability of the stromal axis and the EDNRB response signature. Second, while primary stromal cultures 5

preserve dominant state programs and enable mechanistic interrogation, they simplify the in vivo 6

microenvironment; future studies incorporating multi-compartment systems, in situ perturbations and orthogonal 7

multiomics studies will be required to resolve intercellular dependencies and deconstruct secretome complexity. 8

Third, although the IMF ↔QMF axis captures a dominant and clinically relevant dimension, it does not exhaust 9

stromal heterogeneity, and longitudinal tracking of state dynamics during disease evolution remains to be 10 

established. Additional intermediate or context-specific states, as well as lineage relationships and plasticity 11 

dynamics, remain to be defined and tracked during disease evolution, particularly in larger or tissue-diverse 12 

cohorts. Finally, while EDNRB targeting is supported by human NAMs, in vivo models, and computational 13 

outcome simulation, clinical validation is required to establish efficacy, timing, and integration with existing 14 

therapies, particularly for state-guided intervention during disease flares. 15 

16 

Conclusions :17 

Together, this work establishes stromal state plasticity as a unifying axis linking cellular state to disease 18 

trajectory, therapeutic response, and intervention in IBD. By converting an elusive fibrotic cell type into a tractable 19 

human system, we bridge stromal atlases to clinical action. Myofibroblast states emerge as conserved, 20 

predictive, pathogenic, and reversible, thereby positioning the stroma as both a biomarker and a therapeutic 21 

target. This shifts the paradigm from reactive immune suppression to predictive, mechanism-based 22 

reprogramming of disease, with the potential to alter the natural history of recalcitrant IBD. 23

Computational Methods 5

RNA sequencing and data processing 6

RNA sequencing libraries were generated using the Illumina TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Gold with 7

TruSeq Unique Dual Indexes (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Samples were processed following manufacturer’s 8

instructions, except modifying RNA shear time to five minutes. Resulting libraries were multiplexed and 9

sequenced with 100 basepair (bp) Paired End (PE100) to a depth of approximately 40 million reads per sample 10 

on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Samples were demultiplexed using bcl2fastq v2.20 Conversion Software 11 

(Illumina, San Diego, CA). Raw FASTQ files were trimmed and filtered using Trimmomatic to remove low-quality 12 

bases and adapter sequences. Reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38, Ensembl release 13 

94) using STAR (v2.6.0a) with transcriptome alignment enabled. Transcript-aligned reads were quantified using 14 

RSEM (v1.3.3 112 ) with the “–forward-prob 0” option. Gene-level transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) values from 15 

RSEM gene result tables were used for downstream analyses. We used TPM as a normalized RNA-seq 16 

quantification unit because it allows for accurate comparisons of gene expression across samples, as the sum 17 

of TPM values is identical in every sample; it enables identifying stable reference genes by evaluating low 18 

variability (low coefficient of variation) across experimental conditions 112,113 . Expression values were transformed 19 

using log2(TPM) for TPM > 1 and TPM – 1 for TPM ≤ 1. This transformation preserves dynamic range for highly 20 

expressed genes while minimizing inflation of low-abundance transcripts. Publicly available transcriptomic 21 

datasets were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) repository and processed using the same 22 

normalization and transformation procedures to ensure analytical consistency across cohorts. 23

Composite gene signature analysis using Boolean network explorer 8

Boolean network explorer (BoNE) 119 provides an integrated platform for the construction, visualization and 9

querying of a gene expression signature underlying a disease or a biological process in three steps: (i) 10 

Binarization : The expression levels of all genes in these datasets are converted to binary values (high or low) 11 

using the StepMiner algorithm 118 . (ii) Normalization : Gene expression values are normalized using a modified Z-12 

score approach centered on the StepMiner threshold [formula = (expr – SThr)/(3*stddev)]. (iii) Scoring :13 

Normalized expression values for each gene in a given signature, are summed to generate a composite score, 14 

for the gene signature, representing the overall activity of the biological pathway in a sample. 15 

As a modified Z-score, the composite score of a gene signature ranges from negative to positive values, 16 

reflecting the dynamic range of each gene in the signature. These composite scores enable the comparison of 17 

biological states between control and query groups within a dataset. However, comparisons between different 18 

gene signatures or across datasets are not valid, due to the lack of cross-signature normalization and dataset-19 

specific normalization processes. 20 

Samples are ranked by composite signature scores, and statistical differences between groups were 21 

assessed using Welch’s t-test (unpaired, unequal variances and sample sizes). Visualizations, including violin, 22 

swarm, and bubble plots were generated using the Seaborn Python package (version 0.10.1). Composite gene 23 

scores were computed using the COMPASS framework 120 (https://compass.precsn.com/), which applies 24 

StepMiner -based thresholding and normalized aggregation of gene expression to derive sample-level pathway 25 

activity scores. 26

To evaluate classification strength and prediction accuracy, Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves 3

The Area Under the Curve (AUC) quantifies the classifier’s ability to correctly distinguish between the various 7

Odds ratio (OR) analysis 4

Associations between molecular features and clinical outcomes were evaluated using odds ratios (ORs) derived 5

from 2×2 contingency tables. Clinical and demographic variables were dichotomized using predefined strategies. 6
