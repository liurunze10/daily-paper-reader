Abstract: Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) signaling is a conserved biological process with 23 parallel functions in all evolutionary branches of life. Here, we identify Receptor for Activated C 24 Kinase 1 (RACK1) as a conserved redox-regulated hub that integrates ROS signals to coordinate 25 cellular stress responses. Using cysteine reactivity profiling in intestinal epithelial cells, we 26 demonstrate that RACK1 undergoes NOX1-dependent oxidation at multiple residues, with C286 27 serving as a key regulatory site. Functional studies reveal that RACK1 negatively regulates 28 NF κB signaling through redox-dependent interactions with upstream signaling complexes. Upon 29 stress stimulation, RACK1 dynamically redistributes into membrane-less condensates that act as 30 redox-privileged microenvironments enriched for hydrogen peroxide. We find that oxidized 31 RACK1 condensates are conserved through evolution with analogous stress response behavior in 32 bacteria and yeast indicating a ubiquitous and ancient stress sensor-effector system. Functionally, 33 redox-dependent RACK1 activity links environmental stress to translational control, with 34 oxidation promoting inhibition of protein synthesis. Furthermore, RACK1 mediates responses to 35 diverse pathogen-associated stimuli, including viral and bacterial infection, highlighting its role 36 in epithelial innate immune responses. Collectively, these findings establish RACK1 as a cellular 37 node for redox signaling, operating within condensate-based microdomains to spatially encode 38 oxidative signals and regulate environmental stress pathways in cells. 39 

Significance Statement: This study defines a mechanism by which cells achieve specificity in 40 redox signaling through compartmentalization within condensates. By identifying RACK1 as a 41 redox sensor-effector, we reveal an ancient and broadly conserved system linking environmental 42 stress to innate immune signaling and translational control. The discovery of redox-privileged 43 condensates provides a conceptual framework for understanding how transient ROS signals are 44 stabilized and interpreted in epithelial biology, with implications for inflammatory diseases, 45 host–pathogen interactions, and fundamental cellular stress responses. 46

Main Text: 48 Life on earth began in anoxic conditions and initial cellular organization and biochemical 49 systems emerged in a reducing environment. (1–3) As oxygen levels increased, early organisms 50 evolved to sense and regulate reactive oxygen species (ROS) formed as byproducts of cellular 51 oxygen utilization. (4) Most organisms co-opted relatively stable ROS species such as hydrogen 52 peroxide as a potential sensor-effector system to regulate major cellular processes including 53 respiration and metabolism as well as signaling pathways through reversible redox modifications 54 of specific amino acids such as cysteines.(5, 6) ROS signaling as a response to environmental 55 and pathological stress is especially relevant in tissues interfacing with the external environment 56 such as barrier epithelial cells.(7, 8) Cellular responses in intestinal epithelial cells are 57 modulated by hydrogen peroxide (H 2 O2) produced by NADPH oxidases (NOXs). NOX1, one of 58 the major oxidases present in colonic epithelial cells, functions in the cell and tissue response to 59 injury and infection, with loss-of-function associated with inflammatory bowel disease and over-60 expression with cancer.(9–11) Previous data showed that H2 O2 produced by NOX1 and 61 transported into cells modulates specific inflammatory responses in colonic epithelial cells.(12) 62 To protect from non-specific or excessive ROS, cells maintain a generally reductive cytosolic 63 environment with rapid removal of oxidizing equivalents.(13) To ensure specificity and veracity, 64 H2O 2 signaling requires cellular spatial and temporal compartmentalization. While the general 65 importance of ROS in both cell physiology and pathology are well appreciated, our 66 understanding of the underlying dynamics and mechanistic modules that enable signaling 67 responses continues to evolve. In this work, we have identified that a highly conserved protein -68 RACK1- acts as a central redox-regulated hub in the sensing and transmission of environmental 69 stress signals in cells. We show that both the protein and its mechanism of redox signal 70 propagation are conserved through evolution and, in the context of the intestine, modulates the 71 response to an array of stressors including pathogens, by negatively regulating NF κB signaling, 72 ribosomal function and protein translation. 73

Oxidation of RACK1 in condensates alters protein translation 212 To investigate the consequence of stress induced RACK1 oxidation and sequestration into 213 condensates we interrogated cellular modules altered following RACK1 cysteine oxidation. We 214 conducted pathway analysis of proteins identified in our redox proteomics screen and found the 215 Gene Ontology terms corresponding to proteosome and translation to be significantly enriched 216 (Figure 5A). Regulation of protein translation is a major and conserved cellular adaptation to 217 environmental stress.(26) Since RACK1 is a known protein component of the 40S subunit of 218 ribosomes we investigated whether redox regulation of RACK1 may be involved in stress 219 induced changes in protein translation. To test this, we conducted puromycin incorporation 220 experiments to determine if loss of cysteine oxidation alters cellular translational responses. We 221 observed that following 17-AAG induced stress, cells overexpressing WT RACK1 exhibited 222 significant translation inhibition as demonstrated by reduced puromycin incorporation relative to 223 untreated controls (Figure 5B, 5C). In contrast, cells containing the RACK1-C-all mutant had 224 significantly lower translation inhibition relative to wildtype following 17-AAG induced stress. 225

## Discussion 248 In this manuscript, we report an ancient stress sensor-effector system comprising of a highly 249 conserved protein, RACK1 as a key transducer of cellular redox responses. We show that 250 RACK1 populates membrane-less condensates which form a spatiotemporal platform to enable 251 stabilization of cysteine oxidation and allow sensing of short-lived redox signals within the 252 reducing cytosolic environment of the cell. 253 Initial conditions on Earth were highly anoxic with oxygen being toxic to organisms. Slowly, 254 cells adapted to consume oxygen although the cytoplasm itself remained reductive. Reactive 255 oxygen species such as H 2O 2 with a short half-life in the cell evolved as signaling molecules to 256 regulate complex cellular processes through post-translational modifications of amino acids, 257 most prominently cysteines.(29) Numerous examples of the influence of redox signaling have 258 emerged across kingdoms of life, ranging from abiotic stress in plants to immune responses in 259 humans.(30, 31) Given the potential for cellular damage via H 2O 2 , cells maintain a generally 260 highly reductive cytosolic environment to prevent non-specific protein oxidation. Targeted 261 oxidative modification of cysteine residues and thiol switch formation to enable signaling is 262 dependent on the local environment around proteins. Environmental conditions alter the 263 chemical state stability of thiol switches, resulting in a wide range of reactivity towards H 2O 2, 264

making local H2O 2 concentrations a key and protein-specific determinant of oxidative 265 modification.(32) Therefore, the enabling of oxidative protein modifications requires the 266 generation of spatially sequestered environments that allow for localized pools of H 2 O 2 that are 267 shielded from the action of cytosolic reducing equivalents. In this context our finding that 268 RACK1 condensates exhibit a spatially compartmentalized redox environment provides evidence 269 for a redox signaling regulatory mechanism with multiple implications for the control of cellular 270 functions. 271 Our finding that oxidation of RACK1 is important for cell signaling stemmed from our 272 proteomic screen and was validated by cysteine mutation studies showing modulation of NF κB273 activity as well as responses to diverse environmental stress signals, suggesting a generalized 274 effector pathway. RACK1 is a multifunction cellular signaling protein that has previously been 275 associated with stress response in cells,(24, 33) often through interactions with stress 276 granules.(34, 35) This recruitment into stress granules and function is associated with interaction 277 with binding to ribosomes and through post-translational modification including 278 MARylation.(33) In keeping with these findings, we found that a variety of stressors induced 279 RACK1 puncta formation in the cytosol of mammalian cells. We show that these phase-280 separated membrane-less condensates form a localized redox-privileged niche environment. 281 Importantly, stress-induced RACK1-enriched puncta were observed in yeast and bacteria 282 suggesting an ancient origin of this mechanism consistent with the early role of condensates as 283 functional entities given that amino acid-RNA coacervates are observed across multiple domains 284 of life.(36) Cell-free in vitro studies along with modeling suggest that the interfacial properties of 285 condensates alter internal electric potential which in turn promote redox reactions.(37) Studies 286 have shown that in different cellular systems, the functional state of condensate-associated 287 proteins can be modulated by the redox state of amino acids, in the context of phase separated 288 granules.(38–40) In our studies, we show that RACK1 resides in a relatively oxidizing niche 289 environment within condensates, that likely maintains and facilitates functions dependent on its 290 cysteine modifications. RACK1 has a well appreciated function as part of the 40S ribosome 291 subunit and our redox proteomic analysis indicated a distinct signature related to translation 292 regulation.(41) We show that RACK1 appears to connect stress induction to translation 293 inhibition via redox-sensitive modifications within the condensate environment consistent with a 294 major known function of stress granules.(40, 42) 295

We postulate that signaling specificity of this redox stress-effector system is likely generated by 296 the association of the protein sensor in the system - RACK1 - with different protein and RNA 297 partners. In this setting RACK1 by itself is likely not a driver of condensate formation but exists 298 as a client to other primary condensate forming proteins in cells. RACK1 is a generally 299 ubiquitously expressed protein, and we speculate that there are cell and context specific proteins 300 that associate and drive RACK1 condensate formation to enable a wide diversity of signal 301 propagation. In intestinal epithelial cells, this protein partner appears to be INAVA, an 302 intrinsically disordered protein, that has functions in both barrier function and innate immunity, 303 although we cannot ascertain if the interaction is initiated in the condensate itself or occurs in the 304 cytosol and then trafficked to the condensate.(23) We also hypothesize that there could be other 305 more ubiquitously expressed proteins, including G3BP that functionally co-localize within these 306 condensates depending on context and type of stress. Other potential candidates that may drive 307 RACK1 condensate formation include SRSF1 and SFPQ proteins. Concurrent studies using 308 methionine substitution in relation to H 2O 2 responses, showed these two proteins are redox-309 responsive and that stress granule responses involved in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis may be 310 dependent on redox state.(39) 311 RACK1 appears to be a conserved node within redox signaling networks with particular 312 preservation of the key cysteine C286 which is unique to WD7 (Figure S3B). Overall, the 313 similarity of the RACK1 ortholog CPC2 protein in Sacchromyces pombe is almost 80% 314 compared to human RACK1. This makes RACK1 more conserved than other prototypically 315 highly conserved proteins such as small ribosomal subunit protein, 40S ribosomal protein SA 316 (RPS-A), which has a sequence similarity of 70% between pombe and human. In bacteria we 317 found several species of proteobacteria, including E. coli and Bacillota, possess WD40-like 318 proteins similar to RACK1 with a conserved cysteine in a position analogous to C286. 319 Interestingly, the putative RACK1-like protein in E. coli has over 70% sequence identity with 320 human RACK1, although not all proteobacteria analyzed have this conserved cysteine (Figure 321 S3D). Interestingly, of all bacterial species, cyanobacteria have the most abundant and variable 322 RACK1-like proteins (Figure S3E). Despite a careful search for cysteines in a WD7 repeat 323 domain, we were unable to find any example of this in cyanobacteria. Given the sequence 324 similarity between higher proteobacteria and eukaryotes, we speculate that eukaryotic RACK1 325 had a bacterial origin arising potentially through an early horizontal gene transfer event similar to 326 mitochondria.
