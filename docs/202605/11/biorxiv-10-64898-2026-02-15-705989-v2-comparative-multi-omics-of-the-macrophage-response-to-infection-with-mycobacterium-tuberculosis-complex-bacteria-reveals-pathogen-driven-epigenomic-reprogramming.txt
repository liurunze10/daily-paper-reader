# Abstract 24 

Background: Bovine tuberculosis (bTB) is a chronic infectious disease primarily caused by Mycobacterium 25 

bovis , which inflicts significant economic losses on the global livestock industry worldwide and can also cause 26 

tuberculosis (TB) disease in other mammalian species, including humans. Alveolar macrophages are the host 27 

cells targeted by the pathogen during the early stages of infection. While they play a crucial role in controlling 28 

infection, the exact nature of the host-pathogen interaction and the genetic and epigenetic factors that modulate 29 

infection outcome remain poorly understood. 30 

Results: Here, we used transcriptomics (RNA-seq), chromatin accessibility (ATAC-seq), and chromatin 31 

configuration (ChIP-seq) analyses to examine the effects of intracellular mycobacterial infection on the bovine 32 

alveolar macrophage (bAM) transcriptome and epigenome. The primary focus was M. bovis infection, but we 33 

also conducted parallel comparative analyses using M. tuberculosis (the primary cause of human TB—hTB), 34 

M. bovis BCG (the vaccine strain), and gamma-irradiated (killed) M. bovis . Integration of RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, 35 

and ATAC-seq data revealed coordinated remodelling of chromatin accessibility and histone modification 36 

landscapes underpinning transcriptional activation of key immune and metabolic pathways in response to 37 

infection. The identification of candidate genes, including ERBB4 , LRCH1 , MRTFA , and RNPC3 , through 38 

integrative analysis with a genome-wide association study (GWAS) for M. bovis infection susceptibility 39 

underscores the functional relevance of these regulatory networks. 40 

Conclusions: Our results demonstrate that M. bovis drives extensive reprogramming of the bAM epigenome, 41 

distinct from the responses elicited by other members of the M. tuberculosis complex (MTBC). The results of 42 

this multi-omics comparison provide new insights into the function of pivotal response genes and support the 43 

hypothesis that pathogen-driven epigenetic reprogramming of the bovine host macrophage is key to M. bovis 44 

survival . It also identifies molecular targets that may inform genome-enabled breeding strategies to enhance 45 

resilience to bTB in cattle. 46 

Keywords: chromatin, genome regulation, host-pathogen interaction, integrative genomics, macrophage, 47 

mycobacteria, tuberculosis 48

# Introduction 49 

Bovine tuberculosis (bTB) is a chronic infectious disease that primarily affects livestock, particularly 50 

taurine ( Bos taurus ), zebu ( Bos indicus ), and B. taurus × indicus hybrid cattle populations. Bovine TB has a 51 

substantial socioeconomic cost through disrupting food production, threatening stockholder livelihoods, and 52 

causing economic damage to global agriculture that runs to billions of dollars annually (Zinsstag et al. 2006; 53 

Azami and Zinsstag 2018). The disease is primarily caused by Mycobacterium bovis , an intracellular 54 

mycobacterial pathogen with 99.95% genome sequence identity to Mycobacterium tuberculosis , the primary 55 

cause of human tuberculosis (hTB) (Garnier et al. 2003). However, unlike M. tuberculosis, M. bovis has a 56 

broad host range and can infect several important domestic species besides cattle, including sheep ( Ovis aries ), 57 

goats ( Capra hircus ), pigs ( Sus scrofa ), and llamas and alpacas ( Lama spp.). (Pesciaroli et al. 2014; Malone 58 

and Gordon 2017). Importantly, M. bovis can also cause zoonotic tuberculosis (zTB), which has serious 59 

implications for human health, particularly in the Global South (Olea-Popelka et al. 2017; Vayr et al. 2018; 60 

Kock et al. 2021). Mycobacterium bovis and M. tuberculosis are both classified within a broader taxonomic 61 

group, the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), consisting of closely related intracellular 62 

mycobacterial species that can propagate and transmit within a spectrum of animal hosts (Malone and Gordon 63 

2017). 64 

Previous research has shown that the pathogenesis of bTB is similar to hTB. There are many shared 65 

characteristics between M. tuberculosis infection in humans and M. bovis infection in cattle, such that bTB can 66 

serve as a valuable large animal model for TB disease in humans (Waters et al. 2014; Buddle et al. 2016; 67 

Williams and Orme 2016; Gong et al. 2020). Transmission of tubercle bacilli occurs through inhalation of 68 

contaminated aerosol droplets, with the primary site of infection establishing in the lungs. Tissue-resident 69 

alveolar macrophages (AM) normally phagocytose airborne bacteria and other microorganisms to contain or 70 

destroy them (Aegerter et al. 2022; Park et al. 2022). Disease-causing mycobacteria, however, have evolved 71 

various mechanisms to interfere with host immune responses and persist and replicate within AM (Cambier et 72 

al. 2014; Schorey and Schlesinger 2016; Awuh and Flo 2017; Chandra et al. 2022; Bo et al. 2023). These 73 

mechanisms include the manipulation of cell surface receptors on host macrophages, inhibition of phagosome-74 

lysosome fusion, neutralisation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen intermediates (ROI and RNI), exploitation of 75 

intracellular nutrient supply and metabolism, suppression of apoptosis and autophagy, interference with 76

The discovery of substantial reprogramming of AM transcriptional networks in response to M. bovis and 81 

M. tuberculosis infection exemplifies the profound impact of intracellular mycobacteria on host macrophages 82 

(Nalpas et al. 2015; Vegh et al. 2015; Lavalett et al. 2017; Jensen et al. 2018; Malone et al. 2018; Papp et al. 83 

2018; Hall et al. 2019; Lavalett et al. 2020a; Pisu et al. 2020; Hall et al. 2021; Hall et al. 2024). These 84 

investigations have also revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and perturbed cellular pathways 85 

and networks are functionally associated with macrophage processes that control or eliminate intracellular 86 

microbes. It has also been shown that the rewiring of host gene expression induced by intracellular pathogens 87 

to promote transmission and persistence can involve reconfiguring and remodelling of chromatin (Hamon and 88 

Cossart 2008; Zhang and Cao 2019; Fol et al. 2020). This phenomenon has been observed in infections caused 89 

by various bacterial pathogens, including Chlamydia spp., Leishmania donovani , Listeria monocytogenes , and 90 

Yersinia enterocolitica (Marr et al. 2014; Bierne and Hamon 2020; Bekere et al. 2021; Stein and Thompson 91 

2023). 92 

Regarding intracellular mycobacterial infections and the host epigenome, an early study by Yaseen and 93 

colleagues (Yaseen et al. 2015) demonstrated that the Rv1988 protein, secreted by virulent mycobacteria, 94 

localises to chromatin during infection and represses host cell genes by methylating histone H3 at a non-95 

canonical arginine residue. Additionally, chromatin immunoprecipitation sequencing (ChIP-seq) analysis 96 

revealed that regulatory sequence motifs embedded in specific subtypes of Alu SINE transposable elements 97 

play a significant role in the epigenetic machinery that modulates human macrophage gene expression during 98 

M. tuberculosis infection (Bouttier et al. 2016). More recently, it has been demonstrated that heterologous 99 

expression of the mycobacterial PE family protein PE17 gene ( PE17 /Rv1646 ) in a Mycobacterium smegmatis -100 

infected macrophage cell line promotes activation of apoptotic signalling pathways via remodelling of host 101 

chromatin through reduced H3K9me3 chromatin occupancy (Abo-Kadoum et al. 2021). Correa-Macedo and 102 

coworkers have also shown, using a combination of RNA-seq, ATAC-seq, and ChIP-seq, that primary human 103 

AM (hAM) obtained using bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) exhibited altered transcriptional programs and 104

Intriguingly, this work also demonstrated that bAM from HIV-positive subjects and HIV-negative subjects 106

Host-pathogen interaction at the level of the macrophage epigenome has also been studied for bTB; our 113

extensive transcriptional reprogramming through differential distribution of H3K4me3 and Pol II at key 115

H3K4 at the transcriptional start site (TSS) of the TNFAIP3 gene, which encodes a key inhibitor of the innate 118 

immune-activating NF-kB pathway. This immunoevasion strategy has also been observed for Leishmania 119 

amazonensis , where the same modification is seen at TNFAIP3 in parasite-infected macrophages, but not in 120 

macrophages stimulated with LPS (Lecoeur et al. 2020). 121 

The present study aims to extend our understanding of macrophage reprogramming in response to 122 

MTBC infection through a comparative multi-omics approach. To achieve this, we used RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, 123 

and ATAC -seq to examine gene expression changes and chromatin remodelling in bAM in response to four 124 

separate infections: (i) virulent M. bovis , the primary agent of bTB and the bovine-adapted MTBC species; 125 

(ii) M. tuberculosis , also virulent and closely related to M. bovis but the human-adapted MTBC species; (iii) 126 

M. bovis Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG), the attenuated vaccine strain which lacks the RD1 locus, a major 127 

virulence determinant required for ESX-1 secretion and pathogenicity in both M. tuberculosis and M. bovis ;128 

and (iv) γ-irradiated M. bovis , dead bacteria that retain an intact cell wall and associated surface ligands for 129 

engagement of macrophage receptors but that lack secretion of virulence effectors. Taken together, these strains 130 

provided a framework for comparing the bovine alveolar macrophage transcriptional and chromatin responses 131 

to infection with virulent, attenuated/dead, and host-adapted mycobacteria. Furthermore, we integrated outputs 132

# Results 135 

In vitro challenge of bovine alveolar macrophages with four types of MTBC mycobacteria 136 

We investigated reprogramming of the bAM regulatory genome after a 24 h in vitro challenge with four 137 

different types of MTBC bacteria ( M. bovis ‒ MBO; M. tuberculosis ‒ MTU; M. bovis BCG ‒ BCG; gamma -138 

irradiated (killed) M. bovis ‒ IRR). We used a combined multi-omics approach, including RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, 139 

and ATAC-seq analyses, comparing each challenged bAM group to a control non-challenged bAM group 140 

(CON) cultured for 24 h post-infection (hpi) (Additional file 2: Table S1). A diagrammatic summary overview 141 

of the experimental design and analytical workflow is provided (Fig. 1). 142 

Bacterial uptake by cultured bovine alveolar macrophages 143 

Fluorescence microscopy was used to generate 10 images of tissue culture plate wells containing the 144 

bAM samples for each experimental challenge group at 24 hpi (MBO, MTU, BCG, IRR, and CON). Combined 145 

analysis of these images yielded quantitative infection/uptake values, including bAM counts, bacterial cell 146 

counts, infected and uninfected cell counts, global multiplicity of infection (MOI) values, and the percentage 147 

of infected cells for each experimental challenge group (Additional file 2: Table S2). These results indicated 148 

that the relative levels of uninfected and infected cells did not differ significantly among the MBO, MTU, and 149 

BCG groups, with a mean 34.5% infection rate for the MBO group ( n = 6), 33.3% for the MTU group ( n = 6) 150 

and 35.0% for the BCG group ( n = 6). The exception was the IRR group ( n = 6), which had a 17.8% uptake 151 

rate for these dead bacterial cells. Examples of the images used to generate these quantitative infection/uptake 152 

data are provided (Additional file 1: Fig. S1a–e). A t-test was performed for each comparison, yielding FDR-153 

Padj. > 0.05 for the three comparisons involving live bacteria and FDR-Padj. < 0.05 for the three comparisons 154 

with live bacteria versus the IRR group (Additional file 1: Fig. S1f). 155

mycobacteria is modulated by histone modifications and chromatin accessibility 253

Malone et al. 2018; Hall et al. 2021). The extent of these gene expression changes is comparable to those 257 

observed in studies that have also incorporated additional functional genomics assays to investigate chromatin 258 

remodelling during bacterial and viral infections. In these cases, the levels of trimethylation of lysine 4 of 259

# Discussion 479 

In this study, we applied a comprehensive multi-omics strategy, including RNA-seq, ChIP-seq for 480 

multiple histone marks, and ATAC-seq, to characterise transcriptional and epigenomic responses of bovine 481 

alveolar macrophages (bAM) challenged with intracellular mycobacteria. To do this, we compared bAM 482 

responses to four distinct types of MTBC: bovine-adapted M. bovis (MBO); M. tuberculosis (MTU), the 483 

primary cause of hTB; the attenuated M. bovis BCG vaccine strain (BCG); and dead, gamma-irradiated M. 484 

bovis cells (IRR). From these experiments, we could delineate how host adaptation and bacterial viability 485 

influence the reprogramming of alveolar macrophages—phagocytic cells that normally play a crucial role in 486 

the immune defence of the respiratory system—to favour the survival and dissemination of intracellular MTBC 487 

pathogens that cause tuberculosis in mammals. In addition, we integrated information on key genes identified 488 

from our multi-omics analyses with GWAS data to improve our understanding of the genomic architecture of 489 

an M. bovis infection susceptibility trait in Holstein-Friesian dairy cattle. 490 

Live, attenuated, and killed MTBC strains elicit distinct transcriptional responses in bovine alveolar 491 

macrophages 492 

This study significantly extends previous work by our group showing that M. bovis and M. tuberculosis 493 

separately induce extensive rewiring of the bAM transcriptome, revealing both a core gene expression program 494 

induced by the two infections, and importantly, a distinct M. bovis -specific bAM transcriptional module 495 

governing the interaction between this MTBC strain and the bovine innate immune system (Malone et al. 496 

2018). In the work presented here, using the same FDR-Padj. threshold of 0.05 but with no fold-change cut-off, 497 

we observed substantially larger numbers of DEGs at 24 hpi compared to this previous study, and an almost 498 

four-fold difference in the numbers of DEGs detected for the MBO/CON compared to the MTU/CON bAM 499 

challenge contrast (8,852 versus 2,312), which is also reflected in the number of DEGs detected for the direct 500 

MBO/MTU contrast (5,052) (Fig. 2b). The marked reprogramming of the virulent MBO-challenged bAM is 501 

even more striking in comparison to the attenuated M. bovis BCG strain. The BCG/CON bAM challenge 502 

contrast at 24 hpi only generated 586 DEGs—less than 7% of the total number of DEGs observed under the 503 

same conditions for the virulent MBO strain; a difference that is also apparent in the 6,741 DEGs detected in 504 

the direct MBO/BCG contrast (Fig. 2b). When considering these results, it is important to keep in mind that 505 

there were no statistically significant differences observed in the bacterial uptake by bAM for the MBO, MTU, 506 

> .CC-BY-NC-ND 4.0 International license made available under a (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is The copyright holder for this preprint this version posted May 5, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.02.15.705989 doi: bioRxiv preprint

26 

and BCG challenges (Fig. S1f). It was somewhat surprising, therefore, that the gamma-irradiated (killed) M. 507 

bovis (IRR), which were phagocytosed two-fold less compared to the live bacterial strains, caused substantially 508 

more perturbation of the bAM transcriptome compared to the MTU/CON (3,320 vs. 2,312 DEGs) and 509 

BCG/CON (3,320 vs. 586 DEGs) bAM challenge contrasts (Fig. 2b). Given that γ-irradiation preserves 510 

bacterial integrity and PAMPs, it was expected that the initial infection response might be similar between live 511 

and dead bacteria. Indeed, Andreu and colleagues (Andreu et al. 2017) previously compared the transcriptional 512 

response of murine macrophages to gamma-irradiated vs live M. tuberculosis . They found that live and 513 

irradiated bacilli triggered a similar transcriptional response at 4 and 24 hours of infection, although the 514 

response in cells infected with irradiated bacilli diminished after 24 hours. Our findings of a more robust 515 

macrophage transcriptional response to M. bovis -irradiated cells compared to M. tuberculosis or BCG may 516 

reflect the inability of dead bacilli to actively suppress the bovine macrophage response to infection, a function 517 

that live MTBC are well adapted to. 518 

Examination of the DEGs specific to the bAM challenged with M. bovis (the MBO/CON contrast), and 519 

the enriched pathways for the MBO/CON and MBO/IRR DEGs (Additional file 1: Fig. S2 and Additional file 520 

3: Table S10), demonstrated that genes related to cytosolic sensing are more apparent in the M. bovis -infected 521 

bAM compared to the bAM challenged with the dead M. bovis cells. In addition, focusing on the results for 522 

the IPA ® Role of RIG1-like receptors antiviral innate immunity canonical pathway, activation of downstream 523 

cytosolic targets of the RIG-I-like receptor (RLR) pathway was evident for several bAM challenge contrasts 524 

involving the live MTBC strains (MBO/CON, MBO/IRR, MTU/CON, IRR/CON and BCG/CON) (Additional 525 

file 1: Fig. S10a–e). M. bovis infection induced coordinated perturbation of upstream RNA sensors and 526 

downstream signalling and transcriptional effectors (including IRF3/IRF7 and NF-κB), consistent with 527 

activation of a canonical type I interferon response. In contrast, M. bovis BCG and irradiated M. bovis triggered 528 

differential expression of selected RLR pathway components without comparable activation of downstream 529

in macrophages and dampening T-cell activation. Our results support this hypothesis and the heightened 773 

activation of the pathway—which may be epigenetically driven—in bAM infected with virulent M. bovis 774 

indicates immunomodulation by the pathogen. Paradoxically, however, this relatively simple interaction is 775 

complicated by accumulating evidence in vivo that immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy for cancer 776 

treatment, which blocks PD1 or PDL1, can increase susceptibility to M. tuberculosis infection and disease 777 

reactivation, albeit contingent on the immune status of individual patients (Vaddi et al. 2024). This picture is 778 

further complicated by recent work using spatial proteomics of granuloma tissue from hTB cases, which 779 

showed that increased PDL1 expression was associated with progression to active TB (McCaffrey et al. 2022). 780 

These observations and our bAM MTBC challenge results suggest that disentangling the role of the PD1/PDL1 781 

pathway in human and animal MTBC infections will require coordinated in vitro , ex vivo , and in vivo research 782 

efforts that leverage all omics technologies that can be used to study genome regulation and host-pathogen 783 

interaction. 784

Conclusions 828 

There are limitations inherent in this study that should be considered. For example, a parallel reciprocal 829 

experiment involving multi-omics analyses of cultured hAM challenged with the same MTBC types would 830 

provide valuable complementary information to deepen understanding of host tropism for tuberculous 831 

mycobacteria. In addition, single-cell multi-omics (i.e., scRNA-seq, scChIP-seq, and scATAC-seq) would be 832 

a superior approach for understanding how macrophage heterogeneity in the lung can impact MTBC infections 833 

(Russell et al. 2025). Notwithstanding these limitations, we have shown in this study that epigenetic 834 

reprogramming of the transcriptome differentiates the bovine alveolar macrophage response to bovine-adapted 835 

M. bovis from that elicited by closely related MTBC strains. We also demonstrate that multiple types of 836 

functional genomics data outputs can be integrated with relevant GWAS data and signpost future multi-omics 837 

research that can exploit the very large GWAS datasets available for cattle and other livestock species. 838
