Abstract 22 

Coenzyme A (CoA) is an essential cofactor required for numerous metabolic reactions, 23 

yet its ability to bind and regulate proteins remains poorly defined. Using a proteomic 24 

approach, we identified malic enzyme 2 (ME2) as a CoA-binding protein. ME2 uses 25 

NAD(P) + to convert malate to pyruvate, generating NAD(P)H to support energy 26 

production and redox homeostasis. ME2 binds CoA at an allosteric site previously 27 

thought to bind NAD(P) +. Reduced CoA has minimal effect on ME2 activity, but the 28

Introduction 38 

Coenzyme A (CoA), discovered by Fritz Lipmann in the 1940s 1, is a central 39 

metabolic cofactor that carries acyl groups via a high-energy thioester bond. It is 40 

required for mitochondrial energy production, fatty acid synthesis and degradation, 41 

cholesterol biosynthesis, and acyl-CoA–dependent protein modifications, including 42 

lysine acetylation, cysteine palmitoylation, and N-terminal glycine myristoylation. 2

43 

CoA has also been proposed to participate in redox signaling through protein 44 

CoAlation. 3, 4 Given its pivotal role in cellular metabolism, disruption of CoA 45 

homeostasis has been implicated in numerous disease states, including cancer, 46 

metabolic disorders, and conditions associated with mitochondrial dysfunction and 47 

oxidative stress. 5 Many important metabolites, including ATP and NADP +, are known 48 

to bind and regulate the functions of proteins 6,7 . Despite the importance of CoA, only 49 

a limited number of proteins—such as pantothenate kinase (PanK) 8, 9 and 50 

calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) 10 —have been identified as 51 

being directly regulated by CoA, highlighting a significant gap in our understanding 52 

of its broader regulatory functions in biology. 53

To systematically identify CoA-binding proteins, we employed a biotinylated CoA 54 

probe (CoA–biotin) to enrich and identify proteins that directly interact with CoA. 55 

Using this approach, we identified malic enzyme 2 (ME2) as a previously 56 

unrecognized CoA-binding protein. ME2 is a mitochondrial enzyme that catalyzes the 57 

oxidative decarboxylation of malate to pyruvate, at the same time converting NAD +

58 

or NADP + to NADH or NADPH. This activity positions ME2 at a critical metabolic 59 

junction connecting different metabolic intermediates in the tricarboxylic acid cycle 60 

> 11–13

. Through this activity, ME2 contributes to cellular energy production via NADH 61 

generation and plays an important role in redox homeostasis by supporting NADPH 62 

availability 14–16 .63 

Interestingly, we found that ME2 directly binds CoA and utilizes CoA to sense the 64 

redox state and tune its enzymatic activity. This reveals a previously unrecognized 65 

function of CoA in cellular defense against reactive oxygen species (ROS). 66 

Furthermore, mice harboring mutations at the ME2 CoA binding site exhibit 67 

significantly impaired muscle performance in treadmill running tests. This phenotype 68 

is accompanied by elevated mitochondrial ROS and diminished mitochondrial 69 

metabolism. Together, these findings suggest the ME2-CoA interaction as a cellular 70 

redox sensor and a critical regulatory mechanism to ensure physiological homeostasis 71 

and fitness. 72

CoA disulfide activates ME2 enzyme activity 109 

We next investigated how CoA binding affects ME2 enzymatic activity. We monitored 110 

purified recombinant ME2 activity by measuring the absorbance of NAD(P)H at 340 111 

nm in prescence of CoASH and several common CoA species, including acetyl-CoA, 112 

propionyl-CoA, butyryl-CoA, succinyl-CoA and malonyl-CoA. Unexpectedly, ME2’s 113 

enzymatic activity was minimally affected by these cofactors, and the largest effect 114 

was observed for CoASH, which increased ME2’s activity by just ~1.2 fold. 115 

To understand why CoASH binding would have such a modest effect on ME2’s 116 

enzymatic activity, we examined ME2’s previously solved X-ray crystal structure, 117 

which reveals that the active ME2 tetramer has two exo sites adjacent to each other at 118 

the tetramer interface 20 . If each exo site binds to CoA, the adjacent CoA molecules 119 

could potentially dimerize to form CoA disulfide under oxidative stress, and bind 120 

ME2 more tightly to promote active tetramer formation. In this scenario, CoA 121 

disulfide may activate ME2 activity better than CoASH. Strikingly, the CoA disulfide 122 

(100 μM) shows a much stronger (~2-fold) effect on ME2 enzyme activity (Figure 123 

2A). This effect is concentration-dependent: even 0.1 uM of CoA disulfide activated 124 

ME2 dramatically, and 10 μM CoA disulfide increased its activity by almost 10-fold 125 

(Figure 2B). However, 100 μM CoA disulfide showed only ~2-fold ME2 activation. 126 

This is likely due to the hook effect - with excessive CoA disulfide, each ME2 127 

monomer will bind one CoA disulfide, decreasing ME2 tetramer formation (tetramer 128 

formation requires one CoA disulfide bound by two ME2 monomers). Next, we 129 

compared CoA disulfide with fumarate, which is known to activate ME2 13 . We 130 

observed that CoA disulfide (10 μM) activates ME2 several fold better than fumarate 131 

(at a previously established concentration of 4 mM), making CoA disulfide the 132 

strongest ME2 activator discovered so far (Figure 2C). 133

CoA disulfide promotes ME2 tetramer formation by binding to the exo site 138 

To determine whether CoA disulfide binding could promote and stabilize the ME2 139 

active tetramer, we used size exclusion chromatography (SEC) to examine the size 140 

distribution of ME2 in the presence of different ligands. Under our experimental 141 

conditions, the inactive apo-ME2 primarily exists as a dimer in solution, with the 142 

major peak exhibiting a volume value of 15.5 mL. Fumarate and NAD + have no 143 

measurable effect on the size distribution of ME2. In contrast, the binding of CoASH 144 

or CoA disulfide allows ME2 to exist mainly as a tetramer, with a peak volume value 145 

of 13.2 mL (Figure 3A). We also used SAXS, a powerful tool for the quantitative 146 

analysis of the aggregation state in a solution, to show that the binding of CoA or 147 

disulfide-CoA transformed ME2 from mainly a dimer into mainly a tetramer (Figure 148 

3B, C, D; Supplementary Table 1). 149 

To visualize the ME2 tetramer promoted by CoA disulfide on an SDS-PAGE gel, we 150

promoted the tetramer formation of ME2 WT but not the R197E mutant (Figure 3E). 153

Me2 K197E mutant mice exhibit impaired muscle function associated with 279 

mitochondrial dysfunction 280 

As ME2 has been reported to play important roles in cancer and neurological 281 

diseases, 27–31 we sought to investigate the physiological consequences of ME2 282 

regulation by CoA and redox state. We initially tested the effect of the ME2 R197E 283 

mutation in several human cancer cell lines but did not observe overt proliferative or 284 

survival phenotypes (Figure S4). Although R197E disrupts the redox regulation of 285 

ME2, it does not affect ME2’s basal activity, potentially explaining the lack of a 286 

strong cancer phenotype under basal conditions. 287

Discussion 329 

Coenzyme A (CoA) is a central metabolic cofactor in mammalian cells. However, 330 

whether it can noncovalently bind to proteins and regulate protein function has not 331 

been well understood. Here, using SILAC-based proteomics combined with 332 

biochemical and structural validation, we identify mitochondrial malic enzyme 2 333 

(ME2) as a novel CoA-binding protein, and CoA serves as a redox-sensing modulator 334 

of ME2 enzymatic activity. Under oxidative stress, the ME2 bound CoA forms CoA 335 

disulfide, which functions as a potent allosteric activator of ME2. We demonstrate 336 

that CoA species bind to a specific allosteric “exo” site that is distinct from the 337 

catalytic site and is essential for stabilizing the active tetrameric conformation of ME2. 338 

The dimeric CoA disulfide acts as a markedly strong activator, exceeding the effect of 339 

previously described activators such as fumarate by more than five-fold. Under 340 

oxidative stress conditions, ME2 facilitates the formation of CoA disulfide, which in 341 

turn enhances ME2 activity to promote NADPH production and maintain 342 

mitochondrial redox homeostasis. 343 

The physiological relevance of this ME2–CoA axis is underscored by our Me2 K197E 344 

transgenic mouse model, in which disruption of CoA binding leads to a profound 345 

reduction in exercise capacity and severe mitochondrial dysfunction in skeletal 346 

muscle. Me2 K197E mice exhibit diminished ATP levels, elevated oxidative stress, 347 

and a dramatic imbalance in mitochondrial redox state, establishing the ME2–CoA 348 

interaction as a critical regulator of muscle energy metabolism. Together, these 349
