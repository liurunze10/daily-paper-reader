Abstract 24 

25 

Ancestry -associated immune differences influence fibrosis risk, however , how fibrosis -26 

associated pathways vary across individuals remains poorly understood. Fibroblasts are a main 27 

cell type involved in fibrosis . The fibroblast response is shaped by cytokine signaling and 28 

macrophage activation. The extent to which these pathways vary across individuals, and how 29 

ancestry -associated immune differences influence fibrosis risk, remains poorly understood. Here, 30 

a poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) -based hydrogel microphysiological system was leveraged to 31 

model fibroblast –macrophage interactions following oxidative stress and to integrate donor -32 

specific immune signals using matched macrophages and serum. Individuals of self -reported 33 

African ancestry exhibited higher monocyte expression of CCL4 , lower monocyte expression of 34 

OXER1, and increased serum IL -10, compared to individuals of European ancestry. Within the 35 

hydrogel, oxidative stress reduced fibroblast prevalence while inducing Ki67 and p16. 36 

Exogenous TGF -β1 increased fibroblast prevalence and collagen 3 production but did not 37 

independently increase α -SMA. Incorporating donor -specific macrophages and serum revealed 38 

that cultures from individuals of European ancestry demonstrated higher fibroblast α -SMA and 39 

p16 expression. Pharmacologic inhibition of IL -10 further increased α -SMA, particularly in 40 

African ancestry –derived cultures, identifying IL -10 as a key protective signal limiting fibroblast 41 

activation. This hydrogel system provides a platform for dissecting inter -individual immune 42 

variation and identifying mechanisms underlying ancestry -associated fibrosis risk. 43

Introduction 46 

47 

Soft -tissue wound repair depends on the coordinated recovery of d amaged cells and the 48 

reconstitution of the extracellular matrix (ECM). 1 Following injury, fibroblasts proliferate to 49 

replace damaged cells and deposit ECM proteins that restore tissue structure. 2,3 When ECM 50 

deposition is dysregulated, the repair response progresses toward fibrosis. During fibrosis, 51 

fibroblasts proliferat e and deposit excessive levels of ECM proteins. 4 This excess ECM 52 

deposition results in the failure to recapitulate normal tissue function. 4 Over 100 million people 53 

each year experience dermal fibrosis in the form of excessive scarring. 5 Understandi ng the 54 

cellular and molecular cues that regulate fibroblast behavior after injury is critical for identifying 55 

strategies to prevent or treat fibrosis. 56 

57 

In vitro fibrosis models improve understanding of the mechanisms driving fibroblast responses to 58 

injury. These models seek to replicate responses observed in vivo , including fibroblast expression 59 

of the activation marker alpha -smooth muscle actin (α -SMA) and fibroblast deposition of ECM 60 

proteins including collagen 3.6,7 Fibroblast proliferation during repair is also associated with 61 

expression of the nuclear protein Ki6 7 in these model systems and in vivo .8,9 62 

63 

Oxidative provides an effective means of inducing and modeling fibroblast injury. Oxidative 64 

stress induced sterile, chemical injury in in vitro models. 2,3,10,11 Following oxidative stress, 65 

fibroblasts increase expression of fibrosis -associated genes. 3 The cyclin -dependent kinase 66 

inhibitor p16 correlates with oxidative stress and senescence in profibrotic contexts. 8,9,12 67 

Fibroblast response can be modulated using serum from patients with fibrotic conditions to 68 

assess the role of soluble factors. 13 69 

70 

Cytokine signaling is a key regulator of fibroblast responses and fibrosis risk. Macrophage 71 

immune cells and their precursor, monocytes, are important sources of fibroblast modulating 72 

cytokines, such as TG F-β and IL -10. The pleiotropic cytokine transforming growth factor -β173 

(TGF -β1) is well -established as a driver of fibroblast survival and profibrotic ECM production, 74 

including collagen deposition. 4,14 Oxidative stress induces TGF -β signaling pathways. 3,15 75 

Inhibition of macrophage recruitment or activation reduces TGF -β levels and mitigates fibrosis 76 

in several disease models. 16 –19 In parallel, interleukin -10 (IL -10) can constrain excessive 77 

inflammation and has been implicated in limiting fibrosis. 20,21 IL -10 further skews macrophages 78 

towards an anti -inflammatory phenotype. 22 How macrophage behavior and cytokine profiles 79 

jointly shape fibroblast survival, activation, and ECM deposition following injury -associated 80 

oxidative stress remains incompletely understood. 81 

82 

Demographic factors, including ancestry, contribute to fibrosis risk. Self -identified Black 83 

individuals have demonstrated greater serum levels of inflammatory markers. 23,24 African 84 

ancestry has been correlated with stronger macrophage inflammatory response. 25 This suggests 85 

that ancestry -associated differences in inflammatory pathways may influence susceptibility to 86 

fibrosis. 26,27 However, there are limited in vitro models into how ancestry -associated differences 87 

in macrophage function and circulating cytokines affect fibroblast responses to injury. 88 

Experimental models that integrate ancestry -linked immune and serum factors with fibroblast 89 

behavior could therefore help elucidate pathways that modulate fibrosis risk. 90

Biomaterial -based models offer a controlled platform to interrogate fibrotic responses. 7,28 92 

Hydrogels can mimic key aspects of the three -dimensional ECM while allowing independent 93 

modulation of cell composition, cytokine signaling, and injury cues. 29 Poly(ethylene glycol) 94 

(PEG) -based hydrogels provide a tunable environment to study fibroblast –macrophage 95 

interactions and quantify injury -induced changes in proliferation, activation, and ECM 96 

composition. 30,31 

97 

98 

Utilizing a functionalized PEG -based system we have developed an in vitro 3D model capable of 99 

sustaining fibroblasts following oxidative stress. We use d this model to study ancestry -associated 100 

differences in fibroblast response and activation amongst individuals of African or European 101 

ancestry. We further assess ed the role of anti -inflammatory macrophage activation, IL -10, and 102 

TGF -β1 signaling in the fibroblast response to oxidative stress . We hypothesized that IL -10 103 

signaling inhibits fibroblast activation in an ancestry -dependent manner following injury. Anti -104 

inflammatory activation and TGF -β1 signaling would promote fibroblast activation and ECM 105 

deposition. Together, these results validate a 3D hydrogel model elucidating mechanisms 106 

underlying the fibroblast response to sterile injury .107

To assess correlations between self -reported ancestry and inflammatory signaling, monocyte 115 

gene expression and serum cytokines were analyzed for individuals of self -reported African or 116 

European ancestry (Fig. 1a). Ancestry classification was based on participant self -report on a 117 

demographic survey. 23,24 Gene expression analysis revealed a 4.3 -fold increase (p < 0.05) in 118 

expression of the chemokine CCL4 for individuals of African ancestry compared to those of 119 

European ancestry (Fig. 1b and 1c). Individuals of European ancestry had a 1.6 -fold increase (p 120 

< 0.05) in expression of OXER1, a receptor associated with monocyte migration (Fig. 1b and 121 

1c). Mass spectrometry of serum proteins isolated from the same participants showed a 2.3 -fold 122 

increase (p < 0.05) in circulating IL -10 for individuals of European ancestry (Fig. 1d). Together, 123 

these ancestry -associated differences in our cohort of individuals highlight differences in 124 

monocyte inflammatory signaling and serum IL -10 which motivates the need to consider 125 

ancestry in our hydrogel model. 126

Discussion 194 

In this study, we used a PEG -based hydrogel model to examine how macrophage activation, 195 

cytokine signaling, and self -reported ancestry influence fibroblast responses to oxidative stress. 196 

We integrated th is in vitro injury model with gene expression and proteomic measurements from 197 

circulating monocytes and serum. Our results indicate that anti -inflammatory macrophage 198 

activation and TGF -β signaling support fibroblast survival and ECM production after injury. 199 

Donors of self -reported African ancestry expressed lower circulating IL -10 levels compared to 200 

European donors. In our injury model, anti -inflammatory macrophages from those of African 201 

ancestry w ere associated with less fibroblast activation compared to macrophages from those of 202 

European ancestry. IL -10 signaling constrained fibroblast activation for African donors but not 203 

European donors. These results further elucidate the role of ancestry, anti -inflammatory 204 

macrophage signaling, and IL -10 signaling in the fibroblast response to injury. 205 

Our hydrogel model recapitulates several markers observed during in vivo tissue repair and 206 

fibrosis , while modeling skin stiffness and permitting three -dimensional cellular interactions .207 

Following H₂O₂ exposure, fibroblasts exhibited increased Ki67 expression, a nuclear protein 208 

associated with fibroblast proliferation during repair. 35 We also observed robust induction of 209 

p16, a cyclin -dependent kinase inhibitor associated with oxidative stress and senescence in 210 

profibrotic contexts. 3,9 Approximately 60% of fibroblasts expressed p16 at later time points in 211 

our model, compared to ~35% of dermal cells reported in mature keloid scars 9 and less than 10% 212 

during earlier phases of the repair response. 8,12 This suggests that our system may capture an 213 

earlier or more acute phase of the response to injury. Oxidative stress from hydrogen peroxide 214 

treatment may have also increased p16 expression. In parallel, we detected increased α -SMA 215 

expression , indicating fibroblast activation toward a myofibroblast -like phenotype. 9216 

Approximately 40% of fibroblasts expressed α -SMA following oxidative stress, similar to the α -217 

SMA expression observed in fibrotic scars. 9 Together, these features support the use of this 218 

hydrogel platform to study fibrosis -associated fibroblast states. 219 

Exogenous TGF -β1 increased the prevalence of fibroblasts and collagen 3 expression following 220 

oxidative stress, consistent with prior work. 3,4,15 Interestingly, TGF -β1 did not significantly 221 

increase α -SMA in our system, in contrast to models where TGF -β robustly drives myofibroblast 222 

differentiation. 36 This suggests that TGF -β1 alone is sufficient to support survival and ECM 223 

deposition, but additional cues may be required to fully induce α -SMA in this 3D context. One 224 

such cue is likely IL -4-induced a nti -inflammatory macrophages which increased fibroblast α -225 

SMA in our model system. 226

Our data highlight IL -10 as a pathway for macrophage activation to modulate fibroblast 227 

activation , consistent with prior literature on the role of IL -10 in the repair response. 37,38 Donors 228 

of self -reported African ancestry had higher serum IL -10 levels compared to donors of European 229 

ancestry, along with increased expression of CCL4 and OXER1 , markers associated with 230 

increased migration to sites of inflammation. 39,40 In the hydrogel model, fibroblasts cultured with 231 

macrophages and serum from European donors exhibited higher α -SMA and p16 expression than 232 

those cultured with serum and macrophages from African donors, particularly in the presence of 233 

oxidative stress. Pharmacological inhibition of IL -10 signaling with Stattic further increased α -234 

SMA after injury. This increase was especially large in cultures with unactivated or anti -235 

inflammatory macrophages from African donors. These findings support a model in which IL -10 236 

acts as a protective anti -fibrotic signal that limits fibroblast activation. Lower IL -10 in some 237 

donor contexts may permit greater activation after injury. 238 

Several limitations should be considered in this work. First, our macrophages were derived from 239 

circulating monocytes and polarized in vitro , and thus may not fully capture the diversity of 240 

macrophage subsets present in specific tissues. 41 With respect to macrophage activation, IL -4–241 

driven anti -inflammatory macrophages are only one of several phenotypes that can arise during 242 

repair. Second, we modeled injury using oxidative stress in a sterile context; responses to 243 

mechanical wounding or infection may involve additional pathogen -driven inflammatory signals 244 

that alter the balance between pro - and anti -fibrotic pathways. 42 Cellular stress from hydrogel 245 

encapsulation may have also increased p16 expression , in addition to oxidative stress from 246 

hydrogen peroxide exposure . Third, we focused on a 7 -day recovery window, which may not be 247 

sufficient to fully capture long -term ECM remodeling and chronic fibrosis. Extending the time 248 

course and incorporating additional injury modalities will be important future directions. Fourth, 249 

our analysis include d only donors aged 18 to 45 with limited serum from each donor and thus 250 

may not extend to healing responses in pediatric or elderly populations . Fifth, Stattic may have 251 

inhibited pathways other than IL -10 signaling. We used Stattic due to improved diffusion 252 

through the hydrogel when compared to neutralizing antibodies against IL -10 receptor .43 Future 253 

work may assess the feasibility of other neutralizing antibodies in our system . Sixth, our analysis 254 

include d only adults between the ages of 18 and 45, before the onset of postmenopausal changes 255 

in hormone levels. It remains unclear if these results apply to other populations. 256 

Finally, while we observe ancestry -associated differences in IL -10 signaling and fibroblast 257 

activation, our study is not designed to disentangle genetic from environmental or sociocultural 258 

contributors to these patterns. 44 –46 Self -reported ancestry reflects a complex combination of 259 

genetic background and lived experiences that can shape immune and inflammatory states. 27,47,48 260 

Our findings should therefore be interpreted as correlations that generate hypotheses rather than 261 

definitive causal mechanisms. 262 

Overall, our work identifies IL -10 signaling and anti -inflammatory macrophage activation as 263 

important modulators of fibroblast activation in response to oxidative stress. This suggests that 264 

differences in these pathways across self -reported ancestry groups may contribute to variability 265 

in fibrosis -associated phenotypes. The PEG -based hydrogel platform described here recapitulates 266 

key features of the fibroblast response to injury. This model can be used to incorporate 267 

circulating cells and serum from individual donors to assess fibrosis risk or evaluate candidate 268 

therapeutics. Future studies leveraging this system to interrogate additional immune subsets, 269

Isolation of serum and monocytes from recruited participants 276 

277 

Participants were recruited in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and with IRB 278 

approval (2047810 -4) from the University of Maryland College Park (UMCP) IRB, College 279 

Park, MD, KIRB 0223 . Thirty -two individuals aged 18 to 45 of self -reported Black/African or 280 

White/European descent were recruited using fliers. Recruited individuals provided informed 281 

consent before participation. Participants filled out a survey providing information on age, sex, 282 

self -reported European or African ancestry, and other demographic information. A phlebotomist 283 

drew blood (45 mL) from each participant. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were 284 

isolated from whole blood using BD Vacutainer® CPT ™ Mononuclear Cell Preparation Tubes 285 

(BD Biosciences, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Monocytes were isolated from the 286 

peripheral blood mononuclear cell (PBMC) fraction using pan -monocyte negative selection with 287 

QuadroMACS ™ separator (Miltenyi). Cells were cryopreserved using CryoStor® CS10 288 

(STEMCELL Technologies). Serum was also obtained and stored at -80°C. 289

Imaging and image analysis 395 

396 

Hydrogels were imaged using the BC43 benchtop confocal microscope (Andor) using the blue, 397 

green, red, and far red channels. Cells were identified using DAPI. Identical gain and exposure 398 

were used across samples within the same experiment. Images within the hydrogel were taken as 399 

Z-stacks with a step unit of 1.5 μm and a range of 40 μm. Image planes were selected in a 400 

random and unbiased manner. Individual planes were imported into Imaris, and a Z -projection 401 

was made by combining slices. Cells were identified using the Imaris spot analysis. The Imaris 402 

machine learning algorithm was then trained to identify positive cells. The output of the 403 

algorithm was checked for accuracy and then applied to the Z -projections. The number of 404 

positive and negative cells was then quantified. 405
