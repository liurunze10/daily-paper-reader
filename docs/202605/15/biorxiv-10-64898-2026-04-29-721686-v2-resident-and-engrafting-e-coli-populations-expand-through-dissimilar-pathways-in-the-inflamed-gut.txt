Abstract 13 

Intestinal inflammation increases the abundance of Enterobacteriaceae in the 14 

gastrointestinal tract by several orders of magnitude. These population expansions, or 15 

blooms, are associated with disease progression and have been suggested to exacerbate 16 

intestinal pathologies in some settings. Murine studies have shown that during the early 17 

stages of Escherichia coli colonization, i.e., during engraftment, inflammation enhances 18 

fitness through processes that depend on Moco, an enzyme cofactor found in a variety of 19 

oxidoreductases that consists of molybdenum coordinated by a pterin molecule. Using a 20 

murine commensal E. coli isolate and a DSS-induced colitis model in mice, we 21 

investigated whether Moco is also important for blooms of E. coli that are part of the 22 

resident microbiota, that is, for E. coli that have engrafted well before the onset of 23 

inflammation. We show that resident wild-type and Moco – E. coli exhibit comparable 24 

expansions in response to inflammation, indicating that, in this context, Moco-dependent 25 

processes such as nitrate respiration or formate oxidation were not important for 26 

inflammation-induced blooms. We find that Moco is important, however, for E. coli 27 

colonization in the absence of inflammation, suggesting that alternative respiratory 28 

pathways or other Moco-dependent processes are necessary for E. coli colonization of a 29 

healthy murine gut. Our findings demonstrate that the mechanisms underlying 30 

inflammation-induced blooms can depend on the temporal relationship between 31 

engraftment and inflammation, and also highlight the importance of considering 32 

colonization stage in identifying and interpreting the factors that affect the fitness of 33 

microbes colonizing the intestine. 34

Importance 37 

Inflammation in the intestine disrupts the bacterial populations that inhabit this 38 

environment. One characteristic feature of this disruption is the large increase in 39 

abundance—blooms—of Escherichia coli and related species. These blooms have 40 

received considerable attention because they have been associated with disease 41 

progression of various forms of colitis. Previous work in mice identified respiratory 42 

pathways important for the fitness of E. coli that are introduced into the intestine around 43 

the same time that inflammation is induced. However, there is a common assumption that 44 

the same pathways account for blooms of E. coli that have colonized well before the onset 45 

of inflammation. Here, we show an example in which this assumption is incorrect. 46 

Therefore, one may need to distinguish between early and late colonization stages to 47 

understand some of the mechanisms underlying inflammation-induced blooms. This 48 

distinction is also likely to be important for many other aspects of microbial colonization of 49 

the intestine. 50

Fitness determinants of microbes in the intestine are likely to depend on colonization 52 

stage. Upon invading the GI tract, successful colonizers must transit myriad environments, 53 

with varying nutrients, stressors, and other properties, before successfully establishing a 54 

foothold in a suitable niche. The temporal organization of this process is poorly described, 55 

but one can at least distinguish between the earliest stages, where the colonizers are still 56 

in the process of engrafting, and much later stages, where they have reached a stable or 57 

quasi-stable state and can be considered members of the resident microbiota. Many of the 58 

physiologic requirements for growth, survival, and retention in the intestine are likely to be 59 

different for these early and late stages, and therefore, many of the genes most important 60 

for fitness are likely to differ as well. This view is supported by longitudinal studies, 61 

including competitions, transposon sequencing, and transcriptomic experiments (1, 2). 62 

For the same reasons, a microbe’s response to intestinal perturbations, such as a change 63 

in diet or the onset of inflammation, could also depend on colonization stage. In this work, 64 

we show that this is indeed the case for the role of molybdenum cofactor (Moco) in the 65 

response of an E. coli isolate to dextran sodium sulfate (DSS)-induced inflammation in the 66 

murine intestine. 67 

68 

Intestinal inflammation has a profound effect on the microbiome and can cause the 69 

abundance of E. coli and other Enterobacteriaceae to increase by several orders of 70 

magnitude (3-5). These population expansions, or “blooms,” have been associated with 71 

disease progression and might also play a causal role in some intestinal pathologies (6-9). 72 

Although much of the microbial physiology and ecology underlying inflammation-induced 73 

blooms has not been fully characterized, studies focused on E. coli have identified Moco to 74 

be a key fitness determinant for the colonization of the inflamed murine intestine (10). E. 75 

coli contains nineteen Moco-dependent enzymes, which function in various metabolic 76 

pathways such as electron transport chains and repair reactions (11). Through analysis of 77 

specific mutants, two such processes—nitrate respiration and formate oxidation—have 78 

been shown to be important for E. coli colonization of DSS-treated mice as well as in other 79 

inflammation models (10, 12, 13). Furthermore, these studies showed that inflammation 80 

increases intestinal nitrate and formate levels. Most mouse studies comparing the fitness 81 

of wild-type E. coli to that of mutants defective in Moco synthesis or in a Moco-dependent 82 

pathway have focused on intestinal inflammation occurring early in the colonization 83 

process (i.e., inflammation was induced either prior to, concurrently with, or shortly after 84 

E. coli inoculation.) Here, we explore whether Moco-dependent processes necessarily play 85 

a similar role in blooms of E. coli that are part of the resident microbiota prior to the onset 86 

of inflammation. 87 

88

Moco - derivative by deleting moaA , which encodes the enzyme catalyzing the first 90 

committed step of Moco biosynthesis (11). Since Moco is required for nitrate respiration 91 

and E. coli cannot ferment glycerol (11, 15), we verified that the Δ moaA strain was unable 92

Next, we tested whether Moco plays a similar role in E. coli blooms when inflammation is 103 

induced well after E. coli have engrafted in the intestine. Mice were pre-treated with 104 

streptomycin and then co-colonized with Δ moaA and wild-type strains. We waited for 105 

stable colonization (two weeks following inoculation) before introducing DSS into the 106 

drinking water. In two repeats of this experiment performed on different dates, E. coli counts 107 

declined more slowly (Fig. S3A,B) and stayed well above the colonization levels that we typically 108 

observe for stable colonization of mice by E. coli MP1 (Fig. 2), (14) . This difference between 109 

(Fig. 1C) and the two repeats in (Fig. S3A,B) likely reflects differences in the recovery of the 110 

microbiota following streptomycin pre-treatment. Therefore, for the experiments in (Fig. 111 

S2A,B), the mice were gavaged with a slurry of stool from untreated mice three weeks after 112

In all three experiments, following DSS administration, fecal counts of wild-type E. coli 114

competitive index immediately before DSS treatment was started (Fig. 1D; S3A,B). 121 

122 

We also tested whether Δ moaA and wild type showed similar DSS-induced blooms when 123 

mice were engrafted with only a single strain. For these experiments, we took advantage of 124 

the fact that E. coli MP1 stably colonizes SPF C57BL/6 mice without antibiotic pre-125 

treatment. Mice were colonized with either wild type or Δ moaA without streptomycin 126 

pretreatment, and colonization levels stabilized to ~10 5-10 6 cfu per gram of stool within 72 127 

hours. After nine days, DSS was added to the drinking water, and we again observed that 128 

ΔmoaA and wild type showed comparable blooms (Figs. 1E,F). 129 

130 

In the above competition experiments, levels of the Δ moaA strain declined markedly 131 

relative to wild type during the several weeks prior to DSS treatment (Figs. 1C; S3A,B) . 132

141 

Taken together, our findings indicate that the mechanisms underlying E. coli MP1 142 

population expansions in response to DSS-induced colitis can differ for early and late 143 

stages of colonization. When inflammation was present during the engraftment process, 144 

i.e., before or very shortly after E. coli was introduced into the GI tract, we found that Moco 145 

was a key fitness determinant (Figs. 1A,B), consistent with previous reports (10). On the 146 

other hand, for E. coli that had already engrafted and were effectively part of the resident 147 

microbiota when inflammation commenced, Moco’s absence had no detectable effect on 148 

the population expansion. Thus, at least for the E. coli strain and colonization model used 149 

here, nitrate respiration and formate oxidation, both Moco-dependent processes, are not 150 

important for blooms of E. coli that have colonized well before DSS treatment, even though 151 

they are important when E. coli is engrafting in an inflamed intestine (10, 12). We note, 152 

however, that a prior study showed that tungstate, which inhibits Moco synthesis by 153 

competing with molybdate, suppresses the DSS-induced bloom of resident 154 

Enterobacteriaceae in mice. This suggests that Moco-dependent processes are important 155 

for the expansion of at least some resident Enterobacteriaceae , including possibly some E. 156 

coli strains. We also note that the results for our colonization model do not rule out a role 157 

for respiration since some terminal reductases, such as those that reduce oxygen or 158 

fumarate, do not use Moco. Indeed, aerobic respiration has been suggested to play a role 159 

in E. coli population expansions in response to colitis, at least for early colonization stages 160 

(12, 17). 161 

162 

Although we found that deleting moaA did not impact DSS-induced blooms of resident E. 163 

coli , it did lower E. coli colonization fitness in competitions with wild type when 164 

inflammation was not induced (Figs. 1C; S3A,B; 2A). This finding may indicate a role for 165 

Moco-containing respiratory enzymes in the colonization of a healthy intestine. However, it 166 

could also reflect the importance of other Moco-dependent pathways in supporting 167 

colonization, such as pathways associated with detoxification, repair, or nutrient 168 

acquisition (18-22). 169 

170 

Our results also highlight the importance of considering colonization stage when assessing 171 

the effects of genetic and environmental perturbations on bacterial fitness in the gut. The 172 

factors that influence the success of an initial colonization, i.e., engraftment, and those 173 

that influence persistent colonization are both important for the microbial ecology of the 174
