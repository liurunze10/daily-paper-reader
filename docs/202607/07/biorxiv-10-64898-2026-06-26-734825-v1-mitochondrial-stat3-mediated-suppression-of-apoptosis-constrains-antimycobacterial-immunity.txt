SUMMARY 35 

Maintenance of mitochondrial homeostasis is required to balance the host-pathogen interface 36 during Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) infection. Here, we identify the non-canonical TRIM 37 family member Trim14 as a critical regulator of mitochondrial integrity in Mtb-infected 38 macrophages. Specifically, we demonstrate that Trim14 preserves mitochondrial membrane 39 polarization and limits macrophage apoptosis by controlling phosphorylation and mitochondrial 40 targeting of Stat3. When targeted to mitochondria, Stat3 restricts opening of the mitochondrial 41 permeability transition pore, which raises the macrophage threshold for apoptotic commitment. 42 

In vivo , loss of Trim14 enhances apoptosis of macrophages and dendritic cells, leading to 43 augmented antimycobacterial immunity marked by increased CD8 + T cell activation and effector 44 function. Together, these findings define a Trim14–mitochondrial Stat3 axis that suppresses 45 host-protective apoptosis during Mtb infection and pinpoint mitochondrial Stat3 as a potential 46 target for therapies aimed at boosting antimycobacterial immunity. 47

INTRODUCTION 69 

During Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) infection, cell death pathways dictate whether infected 70 cells restrict bacterial growth or promote dissemination 1. Apoptosis, a form of programmed cell 71 death characterized by maintenance of membrane integrity, is largely protective during Mtb 72 infection, as containment of bacilli within apoptotic bodies limits bacterial spread and reduces 73 tissue damage 2–5 . Apoptotic cell death further enhances antimycobacterial immunity by 74 promoting efferocytosis, antigen cross-presentation, and protective T cell responses 6–10 .75 Inflammatory forms of cell death, on the other hand, such as necroptosis, inflammasome-76 mediated pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, are associated with worsened Mtb outcomes 11–16 . These 77 lytic cell death modalities can compromise infected macrophages, release bacilli into the 78 extracellular space, amplify inflammatory cytokine production (e.g. type I IFN), and worsen lung 79 tissue pathology 17–19 . Consistent with cell death playing a key role in dictating Mtb outcomes, 80 Mtb has evolved ways to polarize macrophages to push them towards pro-bacterial cell death 81 modalities. For example, the ESX-1 secretion system promotes phagosomal membrane 82 damage and cytosolic access of Mtb virulence factors, triggering inflammatory macrophage 83 death and facilitating bacterial spread 20,21 . Likewise, virulent Mtb strains inhibit apoptosis by 84 expressing proteins such as NuoG and SecA2 that limit mitochondrial apoptotic signaling and 85 oxidative stress-induced apoptosis 22,23 . Thus, the balance between apoptosis and inflammatory 86 cell death represents a critical determinant of host resistance to tuberculosis infection. 87 

88 

Mitochondria serve as central metabolic and signaling platforms that coordinate innate immune 89 responses in Mtb-infected macrophages 24–26 . During infection, macrophages undergo extensive 90 bioenergetic remodeling, dynamically shifting how they make and store energy downstream of 91 pathogen sensing and other inflammatory cues. In addition to controlling cellular bioenergetics, 92 mitochondrial fitness—defined by membrane potential, respiratory capacity, organelle integrity, 93 and quality-control pathways— is a key determinant in driving cell death modality usage 24,27–30 .94 We know that mutations in host mitochondrial genes are associated with chronic inflammation 95 and increased susceptibility to mycobacterial disease 27,29,31,32 . We also know that Mtb perturbs 96 mitochondrial homeostasis through ESX-1-dependent phagosomal membrane damage, which 97 exposes the host cytosol to Mtb virulence factors that promote mitochondrial stress and 98 necrosis 20,24,25 . However, despite clear links between mitochondrial dysfunction and Mtb 99 pathogenesis, it has been difficult to identify mitochondrial-associated proteins that play clear 100 pro- or anti-bacterial roles in Mtb-infected macrophages or in pre-clinical models of tuberculosis. 101

Members of the multifunctional tripartite motif-containing (TRIM) family of innate immune 103 regulatory proteins are well-positioned to regulate the host-pathogen interface. Canonical TRIM 104 proteins are defined by a conserved RBCC architecture composed of RING, B-box, and coiled-105 coil domains, with the RING domain often conferring E3 ubiquitin ligase activity 33 . A smaller 106 number of TRIMs, among them TRIM14, comprise a non-canonical subset of TRIM proteins that 107 lack a RING domain and function via protein–protein interactions through their C-terminal PRY-108 SPRY domain 34 . Notably, beyond its role in innate immunity 35–37 , TRIM14 has been linked to cell 109 survival programs in cancer. Elevated TRIM14 expression is associated with poor outcomes in 110 several malignancies, including colorectal, hepatocellular, papillary thyroid, and breast 111 cancers 38–41 . Mechanistically, TRIM14 has been reported to promote tumor progression by 112 restraining apoptosis and modulating autophagy, proliferation, and inflammatory signaling 113 pathways 38,39,42,43 . These findings position TRIM14 as a regulator of both immune signaling and 114 cell fate across various biological contexts. 115 

116 

We previously found that Trim14 scaffolds Tbk1 and Stat3 during Mtb infection of murine 117 macrophage cell lines, promoting Stat3 activation and limiting excessive type I IFN responses 37 .118 Signal Transducer and Activator of Transcription 3 (STAT3) is best known as a transcriptional 119 regulator of inflammatory and pro-survival programs in cancer and infection, where it can 120 promote resistance to apoptosis and shape immune cell function 44–47 . More recently, we have 121 come to appreciate non-canonical roles for STAT3 at mitochondria, where it promotes electron 122 transport chain activity and reduces sensitivity to oxidative stress 48–50 . Here, we identify Trim14 123 as a regulator of mitochondrial homeostasis that sets the threshold for apoptotic cell death in 124 Mtb-infected macrophages. We report that macrophages lacking Trim14 exhibit increased 125 sensitivity to intrinsic apoptosis triggers and impaired mitochondrial function, due to defects in 126 phosphorylation and mitochondrial targeting of Stat3. In vivo , loss of Trim14 elicits host 127 protective mechanisms—namely increased CD8+ T cell activation and effector functions— 128 resulting in enhanced survival of Mtb-infected mice. Our data highlight an unappreciated role for 129 mitochondrial Stat3 in promoting apoptosis of Mtb-infected macrophages and argue that 130 therapeutic targeting of the Trim14-Stat3 axis could help boost antimycobacterial immunity in 131 patients. 132

Previous work from our lab has established a role for Trim14 in regulating type I IFN expression 136 during Mtb infection of a murine macrophage cell line (RAW 264.7) 37 . Underlying this 137 phenotype, we found that Trim14 interacts with the innate immune kinase Tbk1 and influences 138 Tbk1’s ability to phosphorylate substrates, including the transcription factor and non-canonical 139 regulator of mitochondrial health, Stat3. Because TBK1 and STAT3 have previous links to cell 140 death and because balancing cell death modalities is critical for controlling Mtb pathogenesis, 141 we asked how Trim14 affects the propensity of primary macrophages to undergo different types 142 of cell death. Briefly, we isolated bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs) from WT and 143 

Trim14 -/- mice ( Fig. S1A-B ), infected them with Mtb (Erdman, MOI=5), and followed propidium 144 iodide (PI) incorporation over time. We did not observe any spontaneous cell death in Trim14 -/-145 BMDMs ( Fig. S1C ), but Mtb triggered more cell death in Trim14 -/- BMDMs relative to WT 146 controls over a 24h infection (MOI=5) ( Fig. 1A ). Given that Mtb infection of macrophages 147 simultaneously elicits several forms of cell death (e.g., apoptosis 2,3 , necroptosis 5, and 148 inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis 13,15 ), we used a panel of standard agonists to identify the 149 cell death modalities at play. We measured no Trim14-mediated differences in cell death 150 triggered by the NLRP3 inflammasome (3h LPS + ATP) ( Fig. S1D ), the AIM2 inflammasome 151 (poly dA:dT) ( Fig. S1E ), or extrinsic necroptosis (TNF α/birinapant/z -VAD-FMK) ( Fig. S1F ). We 152 did, however, observe dramatic differences in cell death when Trim14 -/- BMDMs were treated 153 with apoptotic triggers including etoposide, which induces DNA breaks ( Fig. 1B ), the kinase 154 inhibitor staurosporine ( Fig. 1C ), or the BCL-2 inhibitor ABT-737 ( Fig. 1D ). Enhanced cell death 155 in response to Mtb, staurosporine, and ABT-737 was also observed in Trim14 -/- BMDMs 156 immortalized via the Cre-J2 retrovirus (iBMDMs; Fig. 1E, S1G, 1F ), providing us a more 157 genetically tractable system for studying the mechanisms underlying these phenotypes. 158

1G ) and increased annexin V following ABT-737 stimulation ( Fig. S1H ), as well as enhanced 164 caspase-3 cleavage via western blot ( Fig. 1H ). We also measured an increase in cytochrome c 165 release into the cytosol of Trim14 -/- macrophages following ABT-737 treatment via cellular 166 fractionation ( Fig. 1I ). Importantly, we were able to rescue most cell death in Trim14 -/- BMDMs 167 with the pan-caspase inhibitor Q-VD-OPH, indicating that enhanced cell death in Trim14 -/- cells 168 is caspase-dependent ( Fig. 1J ). Given that loss of Trim14 sensitizes macrophages to programmed cell death in response to Mtb 173 infection and apoptotic triggers, we hypothesized that overexpression of Trim14 would reduce 174 sensitivity to these triggers. To test this hypothesis, we generated doxycycline (Dox)-inducible 175 RAW 264.7 macrophages overexpressing 3xFLAG-GFP or 3xFLAG-Trim14. We optimized the 176 timing of doxycycline treatment, settling on 6h, to reduce off-target effects associated with 177 prolonged doxycycline exposure ( Fig. S1I ). We found that 3xFLAG-Trim14 overexpression was 178 sufficient to significantly dampen Mtb-induced and ABT-737 triggered cell death ( Fig. 1K-M ). 179 DOX-inducible iBMDMs overexpressing 3xFLAG-GFP or 3xFLAG-Trim14 showed the same 180 phenotype ( Fig. S1J-L ). Together, these 182 data firmly implicate Trim14 in controlling the susceptibility of macrophages to undergo apoptotic 183 cell death. 184

Trim14 regulates mitochondria dynamics at rest and following apoptosis induction 186 

We next wanted to understand the molecular mechanisms underpinning Trim14’s role in Mtb-187 and ABT-737-triggered apoptosis. Because alterations in the expression of pro- and anti-188 apoptotic genes can influence sensitivity to apoptotic stimuli and is frequently observed during 189 infection and in cancer 51,52 , we first wanted to test if loss of Trim14 results in transcriptional 190 reprogramming of macrophages. We performed RNA-seq on WT and Trim14 -/- BMDMs at rest 191 and at 4h post-Mtb infection and analyzed differences in relative transcript abundance by 192 DESeq2. Overall, we observed few significant transcriptional changes in Trim14 -/- cells in either 193 condition ( Fig. 2A-B ) and RT-qPCR confirmed that loss of Trim14 does not dramatically impact 194 expression of anti-apoptotic genes ( Bcl2 and Bcl2l1 ) or the pro-apoptotic gene Bax during Mtb 195 infection ( Fig. S2A ) or after ABT-737 treatment ( Fig. S2B ). Protein levels of Bax and Bcl-xl were 196 also unchanged between the two genotypes ( Fig. S2C ). Apoptotic gene expression at the 197 transcript or protein level was similarly unchanged in cells overexpressing Trim14 ( Fig. S2D-F ). 198 Thus, we concluded that gene expression differences were unlikely to be influencing 199 susceptibility to apoptosis in Trim14 -/- or Trim14 overexpression macrophages. 200

Our next question was how Stat3 function might be altered in Trim14 -/- cells. Having measured 245 few transcriptional differences in Trim14 -/- BMDMs ( Fig. 2A-B, S2A-D ), we concluded that 246 Stat3’s role in Trim14-regulated apoptosis was unlikely to stem from its role as a transcription 247 factor. To functionally link apoptosis sensitivity with Stat3 mitochondrial localization, we ectopically 276 expressed either 3xFLAG-STAT3 or a mitochondria-targeted variant (3xFLAG-MTS-STAT3) in 277 WT iBMDMs ( Fig. 4A ) 61 . To first test if this construct efficiently targets Stat3 inside mitochondria, 282 we performed a proteinase K protection assay as in 64,65 . Unlike the outer mitochondrial 283 membrane protein Tom20, whose surface-exposed domain is cleaved by proteinase K, a portion 284 of 3xFLAG-STAT3 and 3xFLAG-MTS-STAT3, as well as the resident inner mitochondrial 285 membrane protein NDUFB8 were proteinase K resistant ( Fig. 4B ), consistent with Stat3 286 accumulating inside mitochondria. We next asked how targeting Stat3 to the mitochondria 287 impacts cell death. We found that while overexpression of 3xFLAG-STAT3 was sufficient to 288 reduce cell death in iBMDMs, cell death was further reduced by 3xFLAG-MTS-STAT3 ( Fig. 4C ). 289 These findings support a model whereby Trim14 directs Stat3 mitochondrial localization and the 290 levels of mito-Stat3 set the threshold for macrophage apoptosis. 291

Mitochondrial-associated Stat3 controls apoptosis via mPTP dynamics 313 

We next sought to define the mechanism through which Stat3 mitochondrial recruitment alters 314 apoptosis sensitivity in macrophages. Mito-Stat3 has previously been shown to interact with 315 Cyclophilin D (CypD), a mitochondrial peptidyl-prolyl cis-trans isomerase that acts as a key 316 regulator of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP). To test if Stat3’s ability to 317 protect against apoptosis occurs through regulation of the mPTP, we used an inhibitor of CypD, 318 cyclosporin A (CsA), to inhibit its activity and induce mPTP closure 66,67 . We found that CsA 319 reduced cell death following ABT-737 treatment in WT cells as expected and returned the 320 enhanced cell death in Trim14 -/- BMDMs back to WT levels ( Fig. 5A ). Having implicated the 321 mPTP in Trim14-dependent cell death, we next employed a Calcein AM-Cobalt (II) chloride 322 (CoCl 2) assay to directly measure mPTP dynamics. Briefly, WT vs Trim14 -/- iBMDMs were 323 loaded with the non-fluorescent dye Calcein AM, which is fluorescent in live, intact cells 68,69 .324 When cells are healthy and the mPTP is closed, CoCl 2 quenches calcein in the cytosol but 325 cannot reach the mitochondria. If the mPTP is open, CoCl 2 can access the mitochondria and 326 quench calcein signal there as well ( Fig. S4A ). We found that Trim14 -/- iBMDMs had decreased 327 calcein signal after quenching, suggesting mPTPs are more open in Trim14 -/- mitochondria ( Fig. 328 

5B ). This trend was maintained during Mtb infection of WT and Trim14 -/- iBMDMs ( Fig. 5C ). 329 Consistent with our finding that ABT-737 treatment promotes what looks like release of mito-330 Stat3, ABT-737 treatment enhanced mPTP opening in both WT and Trim14 -/- iBMDMs whereas 331 CsA closed pores, maintaining calcein signal ( Fig. 5D ). Overexpression of Trim14 limited mPTP 332 opening ( Fig. 5E, S4B ), consistent with earlier observations that Trim14 overexpression limits 333 cell death in macrophages ( Fig. 1K-M ). Also consistent with earlier cell death measurements 334 (Fig. 4C ), overexpression of 3X-FLAG STAT3 and 3X-FLAG-MTS-STAT3 dramatically 335 decreased mPTP opening ( Fig. 5F, S4C ). Collectively, these data suggest that the Trim14-Stat3 336 axis limits apoptosis by controlling mPTP dynamics. 337

Our in vitro macrophage experiments demonstrate that Trim14 negatively regulates 340 macrophage apoptosis by targeting Stat3 to mitochondria and controlling opening of the mPTP. 341 Given that cell death modality usage is a decisive factor in tuberculosis pathogenesis—whereby 342 apoptosis promotes bacterial clearance over pro-inflammatory necrosis 3—we hypothesized that 343 increased apoptosis due to Trim14 deficiency would enhance host resistance to Mtb in vivo .344 

345 

To first assess the ability of WT and Trim14 ⁻/⁻ mice to survive Mtb infection, we infected male 346 and female mice from both genotypes with ~750 Erdman bacilli by aerosol delivery. We 347 deliberately selected a medium-dose inoculum for this experiment to speed disease progression 348 and impose a stringent challenge on the Trim14 ⁻/⁻ mice. We found that Trim14 -/- mice are more 349 resistant to Mtb infection, with a median survival time of 123 days compared to 84 days in WT 350 mice ( Fig. 6A ). Increased survival correlated with decreased weight loss ( Fig. 6B ). Consistent 351 with female mice being more resistant to Mtb in general 70 , we measured higher median survival 352 times in females (144 days) compared to males (109 days) ( Fig. S5A-C ). 353 

354 

Having confirmed that loss of Trim14 confers protection against Mtb in a mouse model, we 355 wanted to more closely examine the immune responses driving this protection. Briefly, we 356 carried out a low-dose aerosol infection (~100 bacilli) and sacrificed WT and Trim14 -/- mice at 357 day 21 and 70, to capture the culmination of the innate immune response and a fully engaged 358 adaptive immune response, respectively ( Fig. 6C ). Overall, we did not see major differences in 359 bacterial burdens in the lungs and spleens of Trim14 -/- mice on day 21 ( Fig 6D, S5D ). There 360 were, however, significantly fewer bacilli in the lungs of Trim14 -/- female mice at day 70, 361 consistent with enhanced protection in Trim14 -/- females ( Fig. 6E, S5E ). Gross examination of 362 lungs with H&E staining did not reveal major qualitative or quantitative differences in hallmarks 363 of excessive pulmonary inflammation, including neutrophilic aggregates, necrotic debris, or 364 necrotic inflammation, in Trim14 -/- mice compared with WT controls. ( Fig. S5F-I ). 365

DISCUSSION 432 

Mitochondrial integrity represents a critical determinant of innate immunity, integrating metabolic 433 homeostasis, inflammatory signaling, and cell death programs to shape host outcomes during 434 

Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection. This report identifies Trim14-dependent localization of 435 mitochondrial Stat3 as an anti-apoptotic checkpoint that preserves mitochondrial integrity and 436 limits apoptosis during Mtb infection. In vivo , loss of Trim14 promotes apoptosis of macrophages 437 and dendritic cells, which amplifies CD8 ⁺ T cell responses and confers an approximately 30% 438 improvement in survival compared to wild-type mice. These findings suggest that therapeutic 439 disruption of the Trim14–Stat3 axis may enhance host-protective immunity against Mtb. 440 

441

Collectively, our studies hint at two non-mutually exclusive mechanisms through which Trim14 442 regulates Stat3 mitochondrial targeting and function. The first possibility is that Trim14 primarily 443 acts as a scaffold, physically bringing Stat3 to the mitochondria. We know that Trim14 and Stat3 444 can physically interact 37 , but where this complex forms in macrophages remains unexplored. 445 Earlier studies of antiviral responses identified TRIM14 as a mitochondrial adaptor that localizes 446 to the outer mitochondrial membrane and promotes assembly of the MAVS– NEMO/IKKγ 447 signalosome 36 . It has also been shown to form a mitochondrial complex with WHIP and PPP6C 448 that is required for efficient RIG-I-dependent signaling 36,72 . Since Trim14 can assemble protein 449 complexes at mitochondria, it is possible it does the same for Stat3, bringing it nearby the outer 450 membrane protein Tom20 and the ETC-associated protein GRIM19 (aka NDUFA13), which 451 have been linked to Stat3 mitochondrial import 50,64 . Studies that make use of Trim14 mutants 452 that fail to localize to the mitochondria (Trim14 R30A, L34A, and L46A) 36 could help test this 453 Trim14 handoff-import model. 454 

455 

The second possibility is that Trim14 control of Stat3 is driven not by its mitochondrial 456 localization but by its ability to bind Tbk1 and direct the Stat3 residues it phosphorylates. Other 457 studies have linked Tbk1 to phosphorylation of Stat3 at both S727 and S754 56,73 . Prior studies 458 have linked Stat3 phosphorylation at S727 to mitochondrial Stat3 function and, in some 459 contexts, to GRIM19-dependent mitochondrial import 61,64 . However, more recent work in S727A 460 KI mice suggest that phosphorylation at S727 is dispensable for mitochondrial abundance and 461 instead regulates Stat3 mitochondrial function once localized 74 . Our findings fit nicely with this 462 newer model, whereby (1) Tbk1 preferentially phosphorylates Stat3 at S754 when Trim14 is 463 absent, (2) pS754 negatively regulates mitochondrial targeting of Stat3, and (3) pS727 is 464 important for controlling Stat3 activity once imported ( Fig. 4E-G ). 465 

466 

Our findings also raise questions about how mPTP opening is functionally coupled to specific 467 cell death modalities during Mtb infection. In many contexts, sustained mPTP opening causes 468 mitochondrial depolarization, bioenergetic collapse, and necrotic cell death 75,76 . For example, 469 Mtb infection has been reported to promote pathogenic necrosis in macrophages in a TNF-470 driven mitochondrial ROS and calcium overload dependent manner. In contrast, our data 471 suggest that mPTP opening in Trim14 -/- macrophages preferentially lowers the threshold for 472 apoptosis rather than driving necrosis. Importantly, despite altered mitochondrial function, 473 

Trim14 -/- macrophages did not undergo the major bioenergetic collapse during Mtb infection that 474 would be expected to favor necrotic cell death ( Fig. 2G-H ). These findings suggest that the 475

It is made The copyright holder for this preprint (which this version posted July 1, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.06.26.734825 doi: bioRxiv preprint

consequences of mPTP opening during Mtb infection are context-dependent and may be 476 shaped by the magnitude, timing, and duration of pore opening. This context dependence may 477 be especially important when considering CypD as a therapeutic target during tuberculosis. 478 Pharmacologic inhibition or genetic loss of CypD has been reported to limit mitochondrial 479 permeability transition and necrotic cell death in Mtb-infected macrophages 77 . However, CypD-480 deficient mice are more susceptible to Mtb infection despite enhanced Mtb-specific T cell 481 responses, suggesting that systemic loss of CypD can impair host protection through effects 482 beyond macrophage necrosis, including altered T cell metabolism 78 . Together with our findings, 483 these studies argue that CypD-dependent mPTP activity cannot be viewed as uniformly 484 detrimental during tuberculosis but instead may have cell-type specific effects that impact 485 whether host immunity is altered during Mtb infection. 486 

487 

A growing body of literature suggests that canonical STAT3 signaling favors Mtb persistence. In 488 Mtb-infected human macrophages, STAT3 represses inflammatory cytokine production and 489 nitric oxide synthesis and pharmacological inhibition of STAT3 limits Mtb replication 79 . In mice, 490 loss of Stat3 in the myeloid compartment reduces bacterial burden, increasing IL-6 and IL-23 491 production and enhancing IL-17 responses from mycobacteria-specific CD4 + T cells 80 . Our 492 report, which demonstrates that Stat3 constrains macrophage apoptosis, highlights yet another 493 way that Stat3 represses antimycobacterial immunity. Together, these findings make a powerful 494 argument for inhibition of Stat3 as an adjunct therapy to help control tuberculosis. Promoting 495 apoptosis itself may also prove a useful antimycobacterial strategy, as combined BCL-2/MCL -1 496 inhibition and treatment with the BCL-2 inhibitor navitoclax 81,82 was recently shown to reduce 497 Mtb intracellular growth in human and murine macrophages and limit Mtb pathogenesis in mice. 498 

499 

Lastly, by revealing a function for Trim14 in regulating cell death programs, our findings extend 500 the role of Trim14 beyond canonical innate immune signaling. This may be particularly relevant 501 in cancer. Notably, TRIM14 expression has been repeatedly associated with breast cancer, 502 colorectal cancer, and melanoma 38,39,83 , with several reports implicating STAT3 signaling in 503 tumor progression. In this context, Trim14-dependent control of mitochondrial Stat3 may 504 represent an additional mechanism through which cancer cells raise the threshold for apoptosis 505 and acquire more aggressive or therapy-resistant phenotypes. Defining this axis across various 506 disease settings may reveal new opportunities to modulate mitochondrial cell death programs 507 for therapeutic benefit. 508

Limitations of the study 510 

Although our study identifies Trim14 as a regulator of mitochondrial Stat3 abundance, the 511 precise mechanism by which Trim14 dictates Stat3 import or retention within mitochondria 512 remains unresolved. Likewise, while we identify a mechanism in which Trim14 negatively 513 regulates mPTP opening during apoptosis in a Stat3-dependent manner, we have not fully 514 defined the mitochondrial binding partners through which Stat3 modulates pore dynamics. 515 Intriguingly, a recent study found that immunoprecipitation of mitochondrial Stat3 enriched 516 several proposed components of the mPTP, including adenine nucleotide translocases and ATP 517 synthase subunits 84 . Future studies will be needed to determine whether Stat3 directly interacts 518 with these proteins in Mtb-infected macrophages and whether these interactions are regulated 519 by Trim14. Lastly, while Trim14 -/- mice exhibit multiple hallmarks of host resistance to Mtb 520 infection, the requirement for CD8+ T cells in this protective phenotype remains unresolved. 521 CD8 + T cell depletion, adoptive transfer, or bone marrow chimera approaches will help define 522 whether enhanced CD8 + T cell immunity is necessary and sufficient for protection in Trim14 -/-523 mice. 524
