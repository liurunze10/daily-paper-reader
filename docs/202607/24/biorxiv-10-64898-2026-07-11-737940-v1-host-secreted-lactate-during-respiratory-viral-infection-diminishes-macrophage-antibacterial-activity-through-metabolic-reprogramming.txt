Abstract. 12 

In polymicrobial infections, how the host recognizes and responds to pathogens 13 

influences which species will persist to cause chronic infections. The human respiratory 14 

tract is a common anatomical site for viral-bacterial co-infections, where primary viral 15 

infections predispose to secondary bacterial infections, leading to increased morbidity 16 

and mortality. Additionally, co-infections are disproportionately prevalent in people with 17 

chronic lung diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and cystic fibrosis. 18 

We previously reported that primary viral infections and antiviral interferon (IFN) signaling 19 

stimulate Pseudomonas aeruginosa (PA) biofilm formation on airway epithelial cells 20 

(AECs). IFN signaling induces aerobic glycolysis in AECs and generates lactate as a 21 

cellular byproduct. Given that innate immune systems play an integral role in co-infection 22 

dynamics, we investigated the role of host-secreted metabolites (i.e. lactate) on innate 23 

immune cell activity during respiratory co-infections. We found that exposure to the apical 24 

secretions from IFN β-treated AECs significantly compromised macrophage antibacterial 25 

activity, with the soluble metabolite lactate playing an important role. Macrophages used 26 

monocarboxylate transporters and G-protein receptors to transport and/or sense lactate, 27 

respectively, and this exposure to lactate diminished their bacterial-killing activity in a 28 

time-exposure dependent manner. Lactate exposure particularly reprogrammed 29 

macrophage cellular metabolism towards an anti-inflammatory state by increasing 30 

oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation. Collectively, these findings provide 31 

insight into metabolites as complex regulators of trans-kingdom interactions and 32 

epithelial-macrophage crosstalk during respiratory co-infections. 33

The host innate immune system is the first line of defense in both cytokine signaling and 51 

pathogen clearance, where macrophages play a pivotal role (12,13). Macrophages are 52 

highly plastic populations; (i) based on the origin, they can be differentiated into tissue-53 

resident or recruited (monocyte-derived) macrophages, and (ii) relying on the 54 

environmental cues, they can polarize towards anti-inflammatory or pro-inflammatory 55 

states (14–16). Generally, a pro-inflammatory state in macrophages is highly phagocytic 56 

with antimicrobial and tumoricidal responses, while an anti-inflammatory state promotes 57 

macrophages to resolve inflammation, phagocytize apoptotic cells, and promote tissue 58 

repair and remodeling. In people with chronic diseases like CF, recruited macrophages 59 

are the predominant macrophage populations and their function to clear infection is 60 

impaired (17–19), an effect heightened in a co-infection environment due to impaired 61 

macrophage clearance of secondary bacterial infections (18,20–22). 62 

63 

Using a viral-bacterial co-culture biofilm model in association with airway epithelial cells 64 

(AECs), our lab has previously shown that RSV infection and subsequent antiviral 65 

response (i) dysregulates iron homeostasis in the airway (2), (ii) releases antiviral 66 

extracellular vesicles (EVs) to impair epithelial-macrophage crosstalk (21), and (iii) drives 67 

epithelial metabolic reprogramming (23). During RSV infection, interferon signaling is 68 

induced and AEC metabolism shifts toward aerobic glycolysis. In a metabolic shift to 69 

aerobic glycolysis, cells increase extracellular glucose uptake, which is quickly converted 70 

to ATP and produces lactate as a byproduct (23). This metabolic rearrangement is a 71 

hallmark of highly proliferative cells (such as cancer cells) and is commonly known as the 72 

Warburg effect (24). Lactate is a complex modulator in the tumor microenvironment and 73 

has long been considered a waste product. However, recent studies highlight lactate as 74 

an energy source, substrate for post-translational and epigenetic modifications, and 75 

signaling molecule of the innate and adaptive immune cells (25,26). A key study in tumor 76 

research by Colegio et al. demonstrated that tumor cell-generated lactate can signal to 77 

or be transported into the local macrophage population, thereby polarizing them towards 78 

an immunosuppressive (anti-inflammatory) state (27). 79

Our observation of increased aerobic glycolysis and lactate secretion in virus-infected 81 

AECs highlights surprising similarities between the metabolic remodeling during viral 82 

infection and the metabolic setting of the tumor microenvironment. It also suggests that 83 

host-derived lactate in the respiratory milieu can dysregulate immune (i.e., macrophage) 84

reducing their ability to clear secondary bacterial infections, the mechanistic details of 86

(2,21) in this work, we sought to determine whether host-secreted soluble metabolites 88 

(i.e. lactate) can alter naïve macrophage physiology in the local co-infected environment. 89 

Our study reveals that lactate-driven immunometabolic regulation of macrophages during 90

Results. 93 

Host-secreted metabolites drive macrophage dysfunction during respiratory viral-94 

bacterial co-infections. A hallmark of viral-bacterial co-infections is antiviral signaling 95 

leading to impaired antibacterial defense, where macrophage dysfunction plays a vital 96 

role (1,11,23). We investigated if antiviral interferon signaling in the respiratory epithelium 97 

triggers the secretion of factors that impair macrophage antibacterial activity. Previously, 98 

our lab showed that primary viral infections and the subsequent antiviral interferon 99 

response reprograms airway epithelial metabolism, leading to the secretion of host 100 

metabolites in the respiratory milieu (23). To investigate the role of host-secreted 101 

metabolites during primary viral infection in regulating antibacterial activity of innate 102 

immune cells, we treated macrophages with respiratory epithelial secretions and 103 

evaluated macrophage bacterial killing efficiency using an antibiotic-killing assay (21,28). 104 

Polarized respiratory epithelial cells CFBE41o- (hereafter AECs) were treated with 105 

interferon-β (IFNβ) for 18 hrs to mimic the host antiviral response, and apical secretions 106 

were collected (Fig. 1A). Monocytes isolated from the peripheral blood mononuclear cells 107 

of healthy human donors were cultured with M-CSF for 8 days to generate monocyte-108 

derived macrophages (MDMs). MDMs were incubated for 60 min with AEC apical 109 

secretions, then challenged with the respiratory pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa 110 

(MPAO1) at a multiplicity of infection of 10. Using bacterial update and survival CFUs 111 

(colony forming units) ( see Methodology ) we next evaluated MDMs bacterial killing 112 

efficiency. We observed that MDMs incubated with the apical secretions from IFNβ-113 

treated AECs are significantly impaired in MPAO1 killing (Fig. 1B). Previously, our lab has 114 

reported that antiviral interferon signaling triggers AEC metabolic reprogramming and 115 

produces metabolic byproducts, such as lactate, which are secreted into the apical milieu 116 

(23). In the current study, we asked if these secreted metabolites can alter macrophage 117 

antibacterial activity. We purified the soluble metabolites from interferon-treated AECs 118 

and incubated with macrophages before performing the antibiotic killing assay. 119

Host-secreted lactate during viral infection reduces macrophage antibacterial 126 

activity. Viral infections are known to trigger metabolic remodeling in AECs, including 127 

increased levels of aerobic glycolysis (23,29,30). L-lactate is a by-product of aerobic 128 

glycolysis and is present in the infection microenvironment (23,24,26,31–33). It is well 129 

established in the cancer biology literature that L-lactate present in the tumor 130 

microenvironment can act as a signaling molecule and/or energy source for neighboring 131 

immune cells and skew macrophage polarization towards an anti-inflammatory state 132 

(24,25,34,35). Therefore, we sought to explore whether lactate present in apical 133 

secretions of virus-infected AECs contributes to MDMs' reduced bacterial-killing 134 

efficiency. We incubated MDMs with 10 mM L-lactate, which approximates the lactate 135 

concentration during respiratory viral infection (23) and performed the antibiotic-killing 136 

assay. MDMs bacterial-killing efficiency was significantly reduced after 60 min incubation 137 

with lactate (Fig. 1D), but not after 24 hrs incubation (Fig. S1A); suggesting that MDMs 138 

exhibit a time-dependent response to lactate. To determine whether the observed 139 

phenotype is a bacterial strain-specific phenotype, we performed the assay using cystic 140 

fibrosis lung PA isolate DH1137 (36). Consistent with the findings with PAO1 strain of PA, 141 

MDMs showed similar defects in DH1137 killing efficiency (Fig. 1E). Lactate can exist in 142 

two isomeric forms, L-lactate and D-lactate; L-lactate is mostly produced by host cells and 143 

D-lactate by bacterial species, while some Lactobacillus species and Staphylococcus 144 

aureus can generate L-lactate (37,38). To investigate if MDMs respond similarly to D-145 

lactate, we carried out antibiotic-killing assays after D-lactate exposure, and the data 146 

showed no reduction in MDMs killing efficiency (Fig. 1F). Finally, we exposed MDMs to a 147 

high concentration of L-lactate that resembles the lactate level in the tumor 148 

microenvironment (39) and performed the antibiotic-killing assay to examine if 149 

accumulation of lactate can exhibit a similar response. Interestingly, we found that 150 

exposure to 30 mM L-lactate for 60 min did not skew macrophage antibacterial activity 151 

(Fig. S1B). Together, our experiments support the conclusion that L-lactate (hereafter 152 

referred to lactate) negatively impacts macrophage anti-bacterial activity in an exposure 153 

time and dose-dependent manner. 154

Lactate present in the co-infection setting signals through pathways similar to 171 

those in the tumor microenvironment. The conventional view of metabolites as merely 172 

a “fuel or energy” source to support metabolic pathways has been challenged over the 173 

last decade. It is now established that many metabolites can function as intracellular or 174 

extracellular signaling molecules to regulate the activity of different enzymes and cellular 175 

pathways (42–44). In hypoxic or acidic environments, such as in tumor cells, lactate is 176 

transported into the cell via the monocarboxylate transporters MCT1 and MCT4 (35,45) 177 

and also sensed by membrane receptors (G-protein-coupled receptors; GPR81/GPR132) 178 

(46,47). To test whether lactate present in co-infection settings signals through similar 179 

GPCRs and MCTs, we performed the antibiotic-killing assay using specific GPCR and 180 

MCT inhibitors (Fig. 3A), as reported in literature (33,48). Pertussis toxin (PTX), a 181 

bacterial AB5 toxin, inhibits Gαi/o G protein signaling cascades (49–51), pathway used 182 

by the GPR81 and GPR132 for intracellular signaling (46,47). We incubated 183 

macrophages with PTX for 18 hrs, followed by 60 min lactate treatment and MPAO1 184 

challenge (Fig. 3A). The presence or absence of PTX did not affect macrophage killing 185 

efficiency in the lactate-free condition; however, in the lactate condition, PTX restored 186 

macrophage killing efficiency to levels similar to those in the untreated condition (Fig. 3B). 187 

Consistent with this idea, we also treated macrophages with 2-cyano-3-(4-188 

hydroxyphenyl)-2-propenoic acid (CHC), an inhibitor of MCT1 and MCT4. Combined 189 

treatment with CHC and lactate also restores macrophage killing efficiency to levels 190 

comparable to those of untreated macrophages (Fig. 3C). Together, these data suggest 191 

that both GPCR-mediated lactate signaling and MCTs-mediated lactate transport 192 

suppress macrophage antibacterial activity during respiratory co-infection, consistent with 193 

recent literature observation (33). 194

Anti-inflammatory macrophages also exhibit changes in lipid metabolism with an increase 260 

in mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO) (62,63). Long-chain fatty acids are imported 261 

into the mitochondrial membrane, where they are oxidized by mitochondrial 262 

dehydrogenases, such as ACADVL and HADHA/B, generating acetyl-CoA, NADH, or 263 

FADH2; these fuels the TCA cycle and the electron transport chain to generate ATP 264 

(55,64). Therefore, as a secondary confirmation of macrophage metabolic remodeling 265 

after lactate exposure, we analyzed (i) the level of expression of a mitochondrial fatty acid 266 

β-oxidation pathway enzyme HADHA, and (ii) the FAO activity of MDM lysates. Exposure 267 

to lactate significantly increases both the HADHA-positive cells and FAO activity 268 

compared to the untreated control (Fig. 5C, 5D). Collectively, these findings demonstrate 269 

that host-secreted lactate during viral infection facilitates macrophage metabolic 270 

remodeling, thereby suppressing their antibacterial efficacy and thus, promoting 271 

secondary bacterial infections in the airway environment. 272

Discussion. 274 

The mechanism by which preceding or concurrent viral infections predispose secondary 275 

bacterial infections is complex and multifaceted. Viral infections can subvert airway 276 

physiology and mucosal immunity, which results in susceptibility to bacterial attachment 277 

and failure to control bacterial replication, respectively (8). These defects in the airway 278 

environment are exaggerated in people with chronic respiratory conditions, such as cystic 279 

fibrosis (CF) or chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder (COPD) (17,18,20). 280 

Macrophages, the first line of host innate immune defense, play a decisive role in the co-281

infection environment by orchestrating cytokine and chemokine responses. Using a 282 

specialized in vitro model of well-differentiated airway epithelial cells (AECs), we 283 

previously reported that antiviral response shifts AECs metabolism towards aerobic 284 

glycolysis, resulting in increased secretion of byproducts (i.e., L-lactate) in the apical 285

the airway by promoting biofilm formation (23); however, the role of the AECs metabolic 287

demonstrated that AECs-secreted soluble metabolites during antiviral response impair 289

metabolites, L-lactate, is pivotal to this defective physiology of macrophages by 291

294 

Viral infections and antiviral responses enhance host cells' glucose metabolism through 295 

aerobic glycolysis, resembling the widely studied Warburg Effect in cancer (45,65). 296 

Aerobic glycolysis supports rapid generation of ATP to accommodate the cellular energy 297 

demand and it also funnels metabolites to other pathways, including the pentose 298 

phosphate pathway (PPP) for nucleosides, fatty acids, and antioxidant synthesis 299 

(23,66,67). A hallmark feature of the Warburg effect is the increased production and 300 

secretion of lactate into the extracellular environment, which has historically been 301 

considered as a waste product. However, growing evidence confirms that lactate 302 

modulates immune responses in cancer (27,68–70) and other infection environments 303 

(33,71); suggesting that lactate could be one of the critical molecules that link metabolism 304 

and immunity. One of such examples is the muscle tissue after exercise, where tissue 305 

resident macrophages consume lactate produced by endothelial cells and polarize into 306 

an anti-inflammatory state (72). A parallel phenomenon has been observed in the tumor 307 

microenvironment, where tumor cell secrete lactate as a means to “hijack” the 308 

surrounding macrophage population, leading to an immune shielding of the tumor (73). 309 

In addition, some studies have linked respiratory viral infections and higher levels of L-310 

lactate with pulmonary exacerbation, including worsened bacterial infection (23,31,74), 311 

although the precise mechanisms are not known. Collectively, these findings have led us 312 

to more broadly consider that macrophage consumption and/or sensing of lactate may 313 

facilitate homeostatic and pro-regenerative phenotypes in co-infection environments, 314 

which inadvertently contribute to secondary bacterial infections in the airway. 315

Lactate typically exists as two isomers, L- and D-lactate. L-lactate is the main form found 317 

in vertebrates, and D-lactate is mainly synthesized by bacteria under anaerobic conditions 318 

(75). In a healthy physiological state, lactate (L-lactate) concentration in blood or tissues 319 

is approximately 1.5–3 mM, which can reach over 20 mM during sepsis, 40 mM in the 320 

inflamed tissues or tumor microenvironment, and ~9 mM in CF sputum (76,77). We 321 

previously reported that, in the co-infection environment lactate concentration increases 322 

up to 10 mM (23). Studies using a broad range of lactate concentrations demonstrated 323 

that lactate promotes macrophage anti-inflammation polarization and suppresses pro-324 

inflammatory cytokines production (i.e., TNF a and IL-6) (27,78–80). In line with the 325 

existing literature, we also observed that 10 mM L-lactate, but not D-lactate, significantly 326 

reduces macrophage killing efficiency (Fig 1), indicating that lactate present in the AECs 327

Studies have established mechanisms of lactate transport and sensing via transporters 337 

and/or receptors that affect immune cells' pro- and anti-inflammatory phenotypes by 338 

altering cytokine production or cellular metabolic reprogramming via kinase activation and 339 

protein modification (i.e., lactylation) (34). Monocarboxylate transporter (MCT) is a 340 

member of the solute carrier 16 transporter family, of which there is a total of 14 subtypes 341 

(34,82). Among them, MCT1-4 are proton-dependent transporters involved in 342 

bidirectional transport of monocarboxylic acid (82–85). Under physiological conditions, 343 

MCT1-4 can shuttle lactate between glycolytic and oxidizing cells; however, due to it’s 344 

high affinity to lactate, MCT1 transports lactate according to the transmembrane gradient 345 

to maintain an intracellular and extracellular homeostasis (44,86). Conversely, MCT4 is a 346 

hypoxia-inducible MCT with low affinity for lactate and is adapted to release lactate from 347 

tumor cells (87,88). These MCT-mediated lactate transport contribute to the activation or 348 

repression of transcription factor nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB), thereby regulating the 349 

inflammatory response (33,44,89). Consequently, inhibition of MCTs suppress tumor 350 

growth and enhance immunotherapy, leading to the emergence of lactate metabolism-351 

targeting drug development (35,48). We, therefore, incubated macrophages with MCTs 352 

inhibitor (non-selective) 2-cyano-3-(4hydroxyphenyl)-2-propenoic acid (CHC) and 353 

examined their antibacterial activity with or without lactate exposure. Our data showed 354 

that inhibition of MCTs restores macrophage bacterial killing efficiency (Fig 2), suggesting 355 

lactate uptake and potential intracellular accumulation are associated with the 356 

immunosuppressive effect. In addition to the transporters, two G-protein-coupled cell 357 

surface receptors (GPCRs), GPR81 and GPR132, for lactate have been reported (46,47). 358 

GPR81 is relatively high in adipose tissue but is expressed in other cell types (80). A 359 

broad range of lactate concentrations (0.1-30 mM) can activate GPR81, suggesting the 360 

metabolite can function as autocrine and paracrine molecule (90). GPR132 is a member 361 

of the pH-sensing GPCR family, exclusively expressed in macrophages and other 362 

hematopoietic lineages but is absent in adipocytes or cancer cells (91). GPR81 and 363 

GPR132 share amino acid homology and signal through the Gαi pathway (92). 364 

Accordingly, we incubated macrophages with Gαi pathway inhibitor pertussis toxin (PTX) 365 

and examined their antibacterial activity upon exposure to lactate. We found that inhibition 366 

of Gαi pathway also restores macrophage bacterial killing efficiency in the presence of 367 

lactate (Fig 2). Together, this observation suggests that both GPR-mediated lactate 368 

sensing and MCT-associated lactate transport are important to skew macrophage 369 

antibacterial activity; which is similar to the recent report by da Silva et al. that lactate 370 

uses both MCT-1 and GPR81 mediated pathways to suppress macrophage NF-κB activity 371 

(33). 372

The anti-inhibitory effect of lactate on LPS-stimulated macrophages has been shown 374 

previously by others (79,93). However, all those studies have used longer incubation of 375 

macrophages and lactate. When investigating macrophage anti-inflammatory 376 

phenotypes, such as cytokines, cell markers, reactive oxygen level or lysosomal pH, we 377 

did not observe any significant change of these properties after 60 min of lactate pre-378 

incubation (Fig 4). This observation is not atypical, given that 60 min exposure may not 379 

be enough to visualize changes in those physiological properties. A study by Shi et al also 380 

supported this, as their RNAseq analysis of BMDMs (bone marrow derived macrophages) 381 

stimulated with LPS and lactate for 3 hr showed no significant induction of anti-382 

inflammatory marker genes. Further kinetic experiment by qPCR revealed that inhibition 383 

of pro-inflammatory genes by lactate occurs as early as 1 hr, while the induction of anti-384 

inflammatory genes (i.e., Arg1 and Retnla) is a later event (>3–6 hr) (48). Besides these 385 

changes, pro- and anti-inflammatory macrophages are also characterized by specific 386 

metabolic adaptations to support their activities and polarization in specific contexts. Pro-387 

inflammatory macrophages show increased glycolysis and accumulation of itaconate and 388 

succinate due to TCA cycle brakes, leading to Hypoxia Inducible Factor 1α (HIF1α) 389 

stabilization and sustained glycolytic metabolism. Conversely, anti-inflammatory 390 

macrophages rely on oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), and fatty acid oxidation 391 

(FAO), that provide substrates for the TCA cycle and electron transport chain (55). When 392 

investigated for these immunometabolic changes, we found that lactate exposure 393 

significantly elevates macrophage OXPHOS metabolism, particularly basal respiration, 394 

and also induces FAO and expression of enzymes associated with the FAO pathway (Fig 395 

5). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that lactate present in the co-infection 396 

environment shifts macrophage polarization towards an anti-inflammatory phenotype by 397 

driving metabolic reprogramming (Fig 6). 398

contribute to macrophage function in the clinical setting. The use of a reliable co-infection 402 

model will potentially strengthen our interpretation, which is the focus of future studies in 403 

the lab. In sum, our findings highlight a mechanism behind the widespread clinical 404 

observation that viral infection predispose the acquisition of secondary bacterial 405 

infections, particularly in people with chronic diseases such as CF. By better 406 

understanding the mechanistic details of the complex interactions among respiratory 407 

pathogens, host metabolites, and immune system, we can refine existing therapeutic 408 

targets including the use of metabolic inhibitor therapeutics. 409

Monocyte-derived macrophage isolation: Human monocyte-derived macrophage 423 

(MDMs) were generated from de-identified healthy donor peripheral blood mononuclear 424 

cells (PBMCs) purchased from the DartLab (Dartmouth Hitchcock Medical Center). 425 

PBMCs were isolated from blood LRS cones via a Ficoll-density gradient, and monocytes 426 

were negatively selected from PBMCs using the Classical Monocyte Isolation Kit 427 

(Miltenyi) (21). Isolated monocytes were seeded in non-tissue culture plates and cultured 428 

with 100 ng/mL recombinant human macrophage colony-stimulating factor (rhM-CSF; 429 

R&D) in cRPMI media (RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM GlutaMAX-I, and 430 

100 U/mL penicillin–100 μg/mL streptomycin) for 6-7 days to differentiate into 431 

macrophages. Twenty-four hours prior to all treatments (days 6-7 of macrophage culture), 432 

the culture media were changed to antibiotic-free cRPMI (RPMI-1640 supplemented with 433 

100 ng/mL rhM-CSF, 10% FBS, and 2 mM GlutaMAX-I). 434 

435 

Antibiotic killing assay: Macrophage ability to clear bacterial pathogens after lactate 436 

pre-treatment was examined using the well-established antibiotic killing assay (21,28). 437 

Macrophages were cultured in duplicate per condition in 48-well non-tissue culture plates 438 

as described above. 60 min/ 24 hr before the assay, macrophages were treated with 10 439 

mM lactate or left untreated. Next, cells were challenged with bacteria (MOI = 10) and 440 

centrifuged at 500× g for 5 min to promote macrophage-bacteria interaction, followed by 441 

30 min incubation at 37°C with 5% CO 2. Afterward, cells were incubated for 1 hr with 442 

antibiotic cocktail media (RPMI supplemented with 500 μg/mL gentamicin, and 500 U/mL 443 

penicillin–500 μg/mL streptomycin) to kill extracellular bacteria. One duplicate well cells 444 

were then washed twice with DPBS (-/-), immediately lysed with 0.1% Triton X-100, 445 

serially diluted in PBS, and spotted on LB agar plates to measure bacterial uptake CFU. 446 

The remaining duplicate well was given low-antibiotic cRPMI media (RPMI supplemented 447 

with 10% FBS, 25 μg/mL gentamicin, and 500 U/mL penicillin–500 μg/mL streptomycin) 448 

and incubated for an additional 2 hr before being lysed to collect bacterial survival CFUs. 449 

Results are calculated as Macrophage killing efficiency (%) [{(Avg. CFU Uptake- Avg. 450 

CFU Survival) / Avg. CFU Uptake} ∗ 100]; or % bacteria remaining [(Avg. CFU Survival / 451 

Avg. CFU Uptake) ∗ 100]. 452

Antibiotic killing assay with apical media from AECs: Influence of host-secreted 454 

metabolites on macrophage antibacterial activity was examined using an antibiotic killing 455 

assay with apical media. AECs were grown as mentioned earlier and basolaterally treated 456 

with 1000 U/mL IFNβ (R&D) for 18 hr at 37°C with 5% CO 2. After 18 hr incubation, the 457 

apical media were collected and centrifuged at 3000x g for 3 min to remove cell debris. 458 

The collected supernatants were used to treat macrophages for 60 min prior to performing 459 

the antibiotic killing assay. To separate extracellular vesicles, the supernatants were again 460 

ultracentrifuged at 100,000x g for 60 min using Optima TLX Ultracentrifuge. The soluble 461 

fraction was used in the antibiotic-killing assay, and pellet (EV) was solubilized in 900 μL 462 

of RPMI and used in the antibiotic-killing assay. Macrophage killing efficiency was 463 

calculated as above and normalized to the IFNβ- control. 464
