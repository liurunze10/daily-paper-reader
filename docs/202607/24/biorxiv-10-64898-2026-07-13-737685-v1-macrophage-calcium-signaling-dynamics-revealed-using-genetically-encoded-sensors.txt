Summary 30 

Macrophages (M Φs) inhabit all mammalian organs, adapting to and influencing the tissues in which 31 

they reside. These versatile cells rapidly respond to tissue-specific signals, clean up debris and serve 32 

as the first line of defense against pathogens. Their ability to act quickly relates to their abundance and 33 

specific arrangement in tissues, as well as the systems they possess for detecting, processing and 34 

integrating emergent signals. Ca 2+ is a fast second messenger that has been linked to many of the core 35 

functions of M Φs. However, spatiotemporal features of physiologically-relevant Ca 2+ signals in M Φs36 

remain largely uncharacterized. Using mouse models expressing genetic biosensors and fluorescently 37 

tagged channels, we visualize M Φ Ca 2+ signal dynamics and heterogeneity, uncover a remarkable 38 

degree of mechanosensitivity in these cells, and characterize the physiological consequences of genetic 39 

ablation of Piezo1 channels via analysis of knockout models across their lifetime. Our in-depth 40 

investigation of M Φ Ca 2+ signaling dynamics has broad relevance for the field of M Φ biology and the 41 

tissues that these cells support. 42

Introduction 43 

MΦs are an abundant resident cell population in every organ in the body  (1). They are uniformly and 44 

regularly distributed and are associated with basement membranes of epithelia and endothelia, the 45 

surfaces of bone, muscle and connective tissue fibers, and the capsules of all major organs  (1, 2). Their 46 

survival, proliferation and differentiation are controlled by signals from the M Φ colony-stimulating 47 

factor receptor (CSF1R), a tyrosine kinase receptor expressed exclusively in the M Φ lineage  (3).48 

Transgenic reporter genes based upon regulatory elements of the Csf1r gene have enabled visualization 49 

of M Φs in many different tissue types  (4–9). Resident M Φs are traditionally viewed as sentinels, 50 

providing the first line of defense against pathogens, but there is increasing evidence of their 51 

importance in homeostasis and sensing of tissue injury  (10 , 11 ).52 

53 

Regulated flux of Ca 2+ across the plasma membrane and from intracellular Ca 2+ storing organelles are 54 

important signal transduction pathways in nearly all mammalian cells  (12 ). The emergence of cell-55 

permeant, Ca 2+ -sensitive dyes in the 1980s  (13 –15 ) permitted visualization of global intracellular Ca 2+ 

56 

responses in immune cells and a subsequent eruption of studies describing ligand-mediated signal 57 

transduction events. One limitation to the use of these probes for the study of M Φ biology became 58 

evident very early, namely the relatively rapid extrusion of dyes from the cell by organic acid 59 

transporters  (16 ). There have also been concerns that Ca 2+ -reporters dampen intracellular Ca 2+ 

60 

responses  (17 ), and/or have off-target effects, potentially modifying or prolonging the response being 61 

measured  (18 ). Notwithstanding these concerns, global changes in intracellular Ca 2+ have been 62 

measured in M Φ and M Φ-like cell lines from mice and humans in response to a wide range of stimuli, 63 

including adhesion, migration, phagocytosis, and activation of purinergic and other plasma membrane 64 

receptors (reviewed in Ref  (19 )). 65 

66 

The ability of intracellular Ca 2+ to regulate and coordinate diverse cellular processes throughout the 67 

body is attributable to the properties of this signal in space, time and magnitude  (20 ). Ca 2+ signals may 68 

arise due to release of Ca 2+ from intracellular stores [e.g., by opening of inositol-1,4,5-trisphosphate 69 

receptors (InsP 3 Rs) on the endoplasmic reticulum (ER)] or by Ca 2+ entry across the plasma membrane 70 

(e.g., via Ca 2+ release-activated Ca 2+ channels or ionotropic purinergic receptors). Store-operated Ca 2+ 

71 

entry (SOCE), involving ORAI channels and their classical activators, the ER Ca 2+ sensors STIM1/2, 72 

has been detected in M Φs in response to a range of stimuli, including activation of endocytic and 73 

chemotactic receptors. However, knockout of STIM1/2 (producing complete inhibition of SOCE) 74 

demonstrate that this pathway is redundant in these cells  (21 ). Ca 2+ oscillations involving SOCE have 75

also been implicated in the differentiation of osteoclasts  (22 –24 ). Regulated Ca 2+ flux in M Φs is 76 

induced by numerous stimuli, including activation of purinergic receptors (e.g., P2RX7 and P2RX4) 77 

(25 , 26 ), C5AR1  (27 ), transient receptor potential vanilloid (TRPV) receptors 2 and 4  (28 , 29 ), and the 78 

channel-kinase TRPM7  (30 , 31 ), and is key to activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome  (32 , 33 ). More 79 

recently, the mechanosensitive, non-selective cation channel PIEZO1 was added to the set of Ca 2+ -80 

permeable regulatory proteins in M Φs during various pathologies  (34 , 35 ).81 

82 

An alternative to the use of Ca 2+ -sensitive dyes for the visualization of intracellular Ca 2+ came with 83 

the introduction of genetically-encoded Ca 2+ indicators, e.g., GCaMP and its derivatives, which are 84 

based upon fusions of calmodulin, the calmodulin binding M13 peptide and green fluorescent protein 85 

(36 –38 ). The GCaMP scaffold has since been modified by numerous groups, to optimize speed, 86 

sensitivity and brightness  (39 ). GCaMP6f emerged to enable ultrasensitive imaging of rapid Ca 2+ 

87 

transients associated with neuronal activity  (40 ). The majority of studies utilizing GCaMP6f to date 88 

have focused on Ca 2+ signals associated with neuronal activity in vitro and in vivo . However, important 89 

studies in astrocytes, cardiomyocytes, and epithelial cells have illustrated its potential for imaging sub-90 

cellular Ca 2+ events, as well as fast Ca 2+ transients and large intercellular responses, across a range of 91 

cell and tissue types  (41 –44 ). We reasoned that expression of GCaMP6f in M Φs could offer a valuable 92 

platform for visualization and analysis of regulated Ca 2+ flux in vivo and in vitro. For this purpose, we 93 

took advantage of a Cre-dependent GCaMP6f line  (45 ) and a Csf1r -cre line  (46 ) that was anticipated 94 

to drive reporter expression in cells of the M Φ lineage. This model enabled us to visualize Ca 2+ events 95 

from the scale of sub-cellular dynamics to the level of tissue-wide organization. 96

as a tamoxifen-inducible cre transgene  (48 ). We wished to avoid the use of tamoxifen, which can have 101

We next isolated peritoneal M Φs from GCaMP6f +;tdT +;Csf1r-cre cre/+ mice and generated bone 145 

marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM Φs) from femoral bone marrow. Expression of tdT and 146 

GCaMP6f fluorescent proteins were confirmed in F4/80-positive BMDM Φs using endogenous tdT 147 

fluorescence ( Fig 2A ) and an anti-GFP antibody recognizing GCaMP6f  (55 ) (Fig S2A ). Phagocytosis 148 

by immune cells is believed to be associated with local elevations in intracellular Ca 2+  (56 ). However, 149 

to our knowledge, this has never been confirmed in tissue M Φs with Ca 2+ imaging with subcellular 150 

resolution. GCaMP6f was sufficiently sensitive to detect localized Ca 2+ events associated with particle 151 

uptake in M Φs ( Fig 2B , blue box) but limitations on temporal resolution under these imaging 152 

conditions precluded a detailed characterization of the nature of wave propagation from the phagocytic 153 

cup. Nevertheless, we observed that peritoneal M Φs exposed to fluorescent zymosan exhibited large 154 

cytosolic Ca 2+ oscillations as they interacted with and ultimately engulfed fluorescent particles ( Fig 155 

2B and Movie S4 ). Ca 2+ oscillations continued for approximately 40 s and resumed when M Φs156 

engulfed subsequent units. 157 

158 

We next explored M Φ responses to agents that have previously been linked to Ca 2+ signaling in 159 

primary M Φs and/or cell lines. C5a, a central effector produced through complement activation, binds 160 

to the G-protein coupled receptors, C5aR1 and 2. Consistent with previous findings in fura2-loaded 161 

MΦs (57 ), C5a (100 nM) caused a large transient increase in intracellular Ca 2+ in nearly all GCaMP6f-162 

expressing BMDM Φs ( Fig 2C, S2B-C, Movie S5-6 ). The Ca 2+ response was similar in nature (peak, 163 

% responding) to the Ca 2+ response caused by the Ca 2+ ionophore ionomycin, which was sustained 164 

(Fig 2C , S2B-C and Movie S7 ). By comparison, relatively high concentrations of the purinergic 165 

receptor agonist adenosine triphosphate (ATP), known to activate ionotropic P2X and metabotropic 166 

P2Y receptors in M Φs (58 ), produced Ca 2+ responses in only ~50% of cells bathed in Ca 2+ -containing 167 

buffer ( Fig 2D-F , Movie S8 ), suggestive of intriguing heterogeneity in purinergic receptor expression, 168 

modulation and/or localization amongst neighboring BMDM Φs. The ATP-induced Ca 2+ signal 169 

propagated through these cells as a slow (6–8 s) intracellular wave ( Fig 2D ). 170

MΦ Ca 2+ responses to fluid shear stress 184 

PIEZO1 is involved in the sensing of shear stress in endothelial cells and shear stress-mediated 185 

elevations in cytosolic Ca 2+  (63 , 64 ), which are phenomena important for normal vascular development 186 

and homeostasis  (65 , 66 ). To better understand the mechanisms by which M Φs sense shear stress, we 187 

took the simple qualitative approach of vigorously flushing an adherent monolayer of M Φs with 188 

physiological salt solution and recorded resulting intracellular Ca 2+ responses ( Fig 2K, Fig S2E and 189 

Movie S10 ). Using this approach, we detected robust intracellular Ca 2+ responses to fluid shear stress 190 

in adherent BMDM Φs ( Fig 2K-L ). To assess whether shear stress also increases in Ca 2+ in M Φs in 191 

suspension, we investigated whether a similar response could be detected using flow cytometry. 192 

Indeed, GCaMP6f-expressing M Φs produced a fluorescent signal that was dependent on flow rate ( Fig 193 

2M ). 194

Ionic currents can be mechanically evoked in cells in vivo in response to a range of physiological 225 

stimuli, including shear stress, stretch and compression, as well as by cell-generated forces, which can 226 

be modelled and studied in various assays  (67 )). Previous studies have demonstrated that small, high 227 

threshold PIEZO1-mediated currents can be evoked by the application of membrane stretch to 228 

BMDM Φs (68 ). Here, we evoked stretch-activated currents with a similar kinetic profile and 229 

appearance to these reports ( Fig 3A ). However, the kinetics and sensitivity of these currents were 230 

distinct from those reported for PIEZO1 activation in many other cells and tissues, potentially relating 231 

to differences in PIEZO1 interacting partners among these different cell types and subtypes  (59 , 69 ,232 

70 ) (Fig 3B-C ). When stimuli were applied by displacing substrate elements subadjacent to the cell 233 

(71 , 72 ), however, sensitive currents were evoked ( Fig 3D-E ). As may be expected of this cell type, 234 

BMDM Φs plated on these arrays would occasionally try to phagocytose individual pili, and caution 235 

had to be exercised (using the tdT signal) to avoid compressing these regions. Further, when the 236 

stimulus produced a current that did not return to baseline or evoked a current with stimuli adjacent to 237 

the pilus (in the absence of pilar movement), the stimulation point was discarded (9 out of 23 cells). 238 

In all remaining cells (14 of 14), currents were evoked by substrate deflections. Current kinetics 239 

(latency, activation time constant, inactivation time constant) were consistent with previous 240 

measurements of PIEZO1 activity using pillar arrays ( Fig S3 ) (70 , 72 , 73 ), and the latency and 241 

activation time constant were suggestive of a direct coupling between mechanical input and channel 242 

activation. The median threshold of activation (calculated as the smallest applied stimulus that evoked 243 

a current) for these currents was measured at 24.8 nm (mean ± s.e.m. = 31 ± 8 nm). 244 

245 

These data confirm that mechanically evoked currents, activated within the cell-substrate interface in 246 

BMDM Φs, exhibit the hallmarks of direct activation by mechanical input and are characterized by a 247 

low threshold for activation ( Fig S4 ) (35 ). Such exquisite sensitivity of mechanically evoked currents 248 

has previously been measured in low threshold somatosensory neurons  (72 ), in additional systems 249 

where PIEZO1 is expressed alongside accessory molecules that sensitize the channel (i.e. STOML3) 250 

(72 ) and when heterologously expressed in cells with high prestress  (73 ).251

MΦs are an abundant cell type in all tissues and are recruited during injury and infection  (1, 2). Our 345 

electrophysiological recordings support roles for PIEZO1 at the M Φ cell-substrate interface, leading 346 

us to investigate whether PIEZO1 regulates M Φ movements during injury-mediated recruitment. Our 347 

investigations in scratch-wounded BMDM Φs revealed three key findings. Firstly, we observed a 348 

robust increase in intracellular Ca 2+ in cells on the wound edge in response to first and subsequent 349 

injuries ( Fig 5A and Movie S11 ). The amplitude and temporal profile of the response was shaped by 350 

the presence of extracellular Ca 2+ (Fig 5B and Movie S12 ). However, Ca 2+ events were still observed 351 

in cells bathed in Ca 2+ -free buffer ( Fig S8 ), suggesting a role for Ca 2+ mobilization from intracellular 352 

stores. While locally released ATP is a likely candidate in this damage response  (78 ), through both 353 

ionotropic and metabotropic purinergic receptors, all cells on the wound edge respond with an increase 354 

in Ca 2+ , in contrast with Ca 2+ recordings in ATP-stimulated BMDM Φs ( Fig 2D ), implying additional 355 

mechanisms of activation. Secondly, we made the interesting observation that after BMDM Φs enter 356 

the denuded area, these cells often oriented themselves in a “patrol line” along the wound edge ( Fig 357 

5C and Movie S13 ). By examining channel movements in patrolling BMDM Φs isolated from Piezo1-358 

tdT mice (expressing tdT fused to the C-terminus of PIEZO1  (65 )), we observed PIEZO1 channels to 359 

transiently coalesce at sites where BMDM Φs meet ( Fig 5D and Movie S14 ). We occasionally 360 

observed PIEZO1 channels in the trailing edge of migrating BMDM Φs ( Fig S9A and Movie S15 ), 361 

similar to that observed in migrating keratinocytes  (79 ), but we rarely observed PIEZO1 channels to 362 

accumulate along inert surfaces, such as silicone dish inserts ( Fig S9B and Movie S16 ). These results 363 

demonstrate that, in adherent motile M Φs, PIEZO1 channels rapidly and transiently assemble at sites 364 

of dynamic cellular encounters. 365

Our generation of transgenic mice expressing the sensitive genetically-encoded Ca 2+ indicator 384 

GCaMP6f in M Φs has provided an opportunity to study chemically- and mechanically-evoked Ca 2+ 

385 

signaling events in these cells at high spatial and temporal resolution in vitro and in situ . A previous 386 

study has used the Cx3cr1-Cre driver to express GCaMP6s or a membrane tethered version of 387 

GCaMP6f in microglia  (54 ). These authors detected spontaneous, localized Ca 2+ transients and 388 

increased signaling in response to changes in neuronal activity. We used Csf1r-Cre to express a fast 389 

Ca 2+ reporter for visualization of M Φs in the periphery. The Csf1r-Cre proved less lineage-specific 390 

than expected  (46 ). Nevertheless, using this model in select cells and tissues, we were able to detect 391 

spontaneous Ca 2+ events in ex vivo preparations and elicit a propagated response to mechanical 392 

stimulation. Our findings suggest that mechanosensing is a common property of tissue resident M Φs393 

and pave the way for future studies using serial intravital imaging to observe and monitor acute- and 394 

long-term M Φ responses to a range of physiological and pathological stimuli. These experiments 395 

necessitate careful implantation of imaging windows to maintain tissue sterility and avoid M Φ396 

activation prior to imaging  (78 ), and may benefit from the use of flexible or openable window 397 

technologies for chemical and/or mechanical stimulation  (80 , 81 ).398 

399 

Our in vitro investigations demonstrated that the GCaMP6f reporter was sufficiently sensitive to detect 400 

intracellular Ca 2+ waves within individual M Φs. We were able to localize Ca 2+ transients to the vicinity 401 

of the phagocytic cup associated with particle uptake in peritoneal M Φs and observe decaying 402 

oscillations following stimulation with the PIEZO1 agonist Yoda1 in BMDM Φs. Interestingly, we 403 

detected Ca 2+ responses arising from experimental manipulations that are routinely carried out in the 404 

study of M Φ biology – flushing of cells on the culture plate and flow cytometry. However, it is not yet 405 

clear whether such manipulations lead to temporary or sustained alterations in cellular function nor is 406 

it clear what the consequences of these perturbations are on downstream cellular readouts. In addition, 407 

we observed propagating intracellular Ca 2+ signals in response to supraphysiological concentrations 408 

of extracellular ATP. We uncovered that not all cells utilize intracellular Ca 2+ to respond to ATP, 409 

demonstrating an intriguing degree of signaling heterogeneity that may be a cause or consequence of 410 

MΦ functional diversity  (82 ). Notably, robust Ca 2+ signals were consistently observed in cells adjacent 411 

to the wounded edge of cell monolayers, where ATP is released locally in response to cell damage  (83 ,412 

84 ). However, in this case, all cells in the immediate vicinity exhibited large intracellular Ca 2+ 

413 

responses, suggesting that additional (purinergic receptor-independent) factors are involved in the 414 

MΦs response to wounding. 415

We focused on mechanically evoked ion fluxes and the basic biology of PIEZO1 in M Φs. In contrast 417 

to the currents produced by stretching BMDM Φs at the apical surface, we observed exceptional 418 

sensitivity of mechanically evoked currents at the cell-substrate interface. Such striking sensitivity to 419 

mechanical inputs is typically only seen in low threshold somatosensory neurons or in systems where 420 

PIEZO1 is expressed alongside accessory molecules that sensitize the channel  (72 , 85 ). Whilst our 421 

study focused on PIEZO1 channels in M Φs, these cells also express TRPV4, and signaling at the cell-422 

substrate interface may reflect the combined activity of these two channels, as previously reported in 423 

primary chondrocytes  (70 ). Nevertheless, taken together, our results suggest that BMDM Φs have the 424 

capacity to distinguish between distinct types of physiologically-relevant mechanical stimuli, which 425 

may be relevant as these highly motile cells infiltrate diverse tissue environments. 426 

427 

One study published in 2024, which also examined PIEZO1 activation in BMDM Φs, reported that the 428 

Yoda1-mediated intracellular Ca 2+ signals observed in these cells were not present in tissue resident 429 

(cardiac) M Φs (28 ). Based on this observation, the authors concluded that BMDM Φs fundamentally 430 

differ from tissue resident M Φs, suggesting that the differences observed between these two cell types 431 

could be an artifact of the in vitro differentiation process. In contrast, we observed robust Yoda1 432 

responses in both BMDM Φs and freshly isolated peritoneal M Φs, suggesting that tissue M Φs can 433 

indeed exhibit cellular responses to Yoda1. We suggest that the differences observed between 434 

BMDM Φs and cardiac M Φs (isolated by enzymatic digestion and purified by flow cytometry) may 435 

reflect the chemical and mechanical stress imposed by these processes, or alternatively the markedly 436 

different cellular environments experienced by distinct tissue resident M Φs populations. 437 

438 

Recently, PIEZO1 has been shown to have numerous roles in the M Φ mediated defense against disease 439 

or infection  (34 , 60 , 61 ). However, to our knowledge, no study has comprehensively characterized 440 

potential roles for this channel in these cells during tissue development and homeostasis. M Φs offer a 441 

fascinating and relevant system to study PIEZO1 function, as they are highly motile cells exposed to 442 

a range of tissue properties, and exhibit diverse phenotypes and distribution patterns  (1). To determine 443 

the homeostatic function of PIEZO1 in tissue M Φs, we generated conditional knockout mice and 444 

studied them across their lifetime. Effective loss of function was demonstrated by the complete loss of 445 

response to Yoda1, complementing our gene expression analysis. However, we were not able to detect 446 

biologically relevant changes in M Φ distribution or function in the absence of PIEZO1, including in 447 

bone, where one may anticipate an important role in osteoclasts  (86 ). Moreover, intracellular Ca 2+

Our studies using reporter mice that express endogenous PIEZO1 tagged with the red fluorescent 453

another. PIEZO1 channels were not observed to coalesce at sites where M Φs interacted with inert, 455

Cell culture 879 

To generate BMDM Φs, mouse femurs were isolated and flushed twice with 5 mL cold PBS to extract 880 

bone marrow. Marrow was centrifuged for 5 min at 400 × g prior to resuspension in either freezing 881 

media (90% FBS and 10% DMSO) or M Φ cell culture media (10% heat-inactivated FBS, 2 mM L-882 

glutamine, 100 U/mL Penicillin 100 μg/mL Streptomycin, 100 ng/mL Human M-CSF in RPMI 1640 883 

medium). Cells were plated on Sterilin square petri dishes and maintained in a humidified cell culture 884 

incubator at 37°C, 5% CO 2 for at least 7 days prior to use in experiments. Media was replenished after 885 

3–4 days, if required. To replate BMDM Φs, monolayers were lifted from plasticware by flushing with 886 

an 18G needle. Peritoneal M Φs were isolated by injecting and removing 5 mL PBS in the peritoneal 887 

cavity. Recovered cell suspensions were centrifuged for 5 min at 400 × g, resuspended in M Φ cell 888 

culture media and plated onto imaging dishes. Dishes were incubated in a humidified cell culture 889 

incubator at 37°C, 5% CO 2 for at least 2 h to allow attachment prior to use in experiments. 890

Two distinct methods were utilised to apply mechanical stimuli to delimited regions of the membrane, 1033 

a pillar array assay to apply mechanical inputs directly at cell-substrate contact points and high speed 1034 

pressure clamp (HSPC) to stretch a region of membrane within an outside-out patch. Pipettes for 1035 

whole-cell patch clamp and outside-out patch clamp analyses were prepared using a pipette puller 1036 

fitted with a box filament (P-1000, Sutter Instruments, USA) from thick-walled filamented glass 1037 

(Harvard Apparatus, USA). Before use, a homemade micro-forge was utilised to heat-polish the glass, 1038 

resulting in a final resistance of 3– 6 MΩ. For measurements, the internal solution contained 110 mM 1039 

KCl, 10 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EGTA and 10 mM HEPES (pH 7.3) and the extracellular 1040

(pH 7.4). Data were obtained using a Nikon Ti-E inverted microscope and an Axopatch 200B with 1042 

pClamp 10 software (Molecular Devices, USA). Pipette and membrane capacitance were compensated 1043

activated currents were recorded at a holding potential of −60 mV. Patch clamp data were subsequently 1045
