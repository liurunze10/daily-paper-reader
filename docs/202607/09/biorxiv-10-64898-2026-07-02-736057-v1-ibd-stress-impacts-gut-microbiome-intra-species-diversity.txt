Environmental stress can be regarded as the ultimate 27 accelerator of these processes, especially for microorganisms, which can alter their DNA if 28 presented with nutrient limitation, toxins, or pathogen attack. Chronic intestinal inflammation, as 29 in inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), may be regarded as prolonged environmental stress for 30 gut commensal bacteria, bringing many enteric species down to undetectable levels. 31 However, it remains unclear how the microbes that survive the IBD gut environment actually 32 respond to IBD stress, and whether their stress response may leave a transient or permanent 33 signature in their genomes. 34 To investigate whether IBD stress induces and selects for certain genetic diversity, we performed 35 metagenomic analyses on gut species in IBD patients and Controls. We focused on strain 36 diversity within single individuals, which might be the result of more recent diversification 37 processes under stress. We found measurable differences at the genome level between IBD and 38 Controls, yet this was species-dependent. We then investigated gene-level diversity and found 39 that certain functions were more likely to be enriched with either neutral divergence, functional 40 divergence, or both. Functions that were enriched in IBD with both kinds of diversity were 41 associated with motility and iron-scavenging, among others. These results may point towards 42 functions that are under selection in the context of IBD stress, and could inform future mechanistic 43 work, exploring previously unknown routes of bacterial diversification and adaptation to stress in 44 the gut microbiome. 45

# Introduction 55 56 Host-associated microbiomes are generally considered more stable than free-living ones(1), as a 57 host actively regulates its own internal bodily environment, promoting microbiome stability over 58 time. Yet, host-associated microbiomes need to adapt to fluctuating conditions that affect the 59 body, such as feeding cycles, seasonal shifts and climate changes(2–6). 60 In modern times, the human microbiome has been radically altered by the adoption of 61 industrialized diets, sedentary lifestyle and use of medications(7–12). Many chronic diseases are 62 globally on the rise, and many of these seem to be accompanied by changes in the human 63 microbiome, particularly in the gut microbiome, the largest and most complex in the human body. 64 Chronic intestinal inflammation, as in inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD)(13), alters profoundly 65 both the host luminal environment and the resident gut microbiome composition(14). In order to 66 clear invading organisms and defend the mucosal barrier, the host inflammatory response 67 induces ample secretion of both biotic (defensins, lysozymes, secretory immunoglobulins) and 68 abiotic (reactive oxygen and nitrogen species) antimicrobial agents(15–19). This antimicrobial 69 response is meant to kill bacteria indiscriminately, but factors such as stress duration, intrinsic 70 stress-tolerance and cell-to-cell interaction can affect whether certain strains are able to survive 71 over others(20–23). 72 IBD is a complex and multifactorial disease that can be diagnosed as early as pediatric age, 73 radically affecting the patient’s quality of life(24–27). Reduced gut microbial richness is a well-74 recognized hallmark of IBD, but beyond species-level shifts in the microbial composition, more 75 research is needed to understand what characterizes the strains that tolerate IBD-driven stress 76 in the gut(28, 29). 77 Tracking strain diversity has been largely used to determine the timeline and dynamics of single 78 or multi-strain synthetic communities, while they acquire, accumulate and share genomic 79 adaptations, both in stable and high stress conditions(30–33). Metagenomic sequencing allows 80 the retrieval of genomes as they are present in their complex native communities(34), and with 81 sufficient sequencing depth it can capture the presence of conspecific strains(35). To better 82 characterize microbiome complexity and decipher microbial evolutionary dynamics, it is 83 imperative to employ genomic approaches that take advantage of comprehensive genomic 84 information. In human microbiome studies, metagenomics has been paired with multi-strain 85 isolations and sequencing to resolve within-host diversification of phylogenetically related 86 strains(36, 37). Employment of large cross-sectional metagenomic collections has been also used 87 to infer species-specific evolutionary processes across hosts(38). These kinds of approaches 88 have been used to identify and monitor individual strains over time, or describe scenarios of strain 89 colonization, persistence and turnover, but mostly within healthy microbiomes, or specifically in 90 the context of bacterial infections and fecal microbial transplants(39–41). More studies 91 investigating microbiomes under stress are needed to understand the biological factors underlying 92 bacterial survival to perturbation. This could potentially unlock avenues of clinical intervention in 93 the context of antibiotic resistance, where exploiting dynamics of niche exclusion seems to hold 94 promise(42). 95 Here, we employed a simple approach to track intra-specific genomic diversity across different 96 gut commensals, comparing IBD patients to healthy individuals. Examining genomic positions 97 that are polymorphic within a subject we evaluated the impact of IBD-stress on the gut 98

We then expanded our analysis to evaluate 100 the distribution and protein-changing potential of gene-level variants, distinguishing between 101 nonsynonymous and synonymous ones. To better understand mutational and selective processes in the context of stress, 104 investigating metagenomic diversity could potentially inform microbiome remediation strategies 105 in clinical and environmental settings. 106

## Gut commensals exhibit decreased or unchanged genome nucleotide 109 

## diversity in IBD 110 In order to describe global strain-level diversity within individual hosts, we employed nucleotide 111 

diversity , a commonly used metric in population genetics ( Methods ). Nucleotide diversity is 112 calculated as the average, per-position degree of polymorphism, over any nucleotide sequence 113 length. It increases with the number of polymorphic positions and with the frequency of their minor 114 alleles, peaking as alleles approach equal split. Coverage affects the number of positions that are 115 included in estimating nucleotide diversity, as well as the number of minor alleles that can be 116 confidently detected ( Methods ). In the context of this metagenomic analysis, nucleotide diversity 117 is used as a proxy for within-host strain mixture or richness .118 To compare nucleotide diversity (ND) levels between inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) patients 119 and healthy individuals, we gathered metagenomic sequencing data from four different cohorts 120 and three different countries, including gut microbiome samples from the Control group and both 121 IBD subtypes (Crohn’s disease - CD, Ulcerative colitis - UC; Methods ). We identified 20 gut 122 commensals that could be robustly analysed across disease groups based on their prevalence 123 across cohorts, and calculated their genome-level ND ( Supplementary Table 1, Methods ). 124 As displayed by the Control samples alone, ND estimates spanned different ranges across 125 species, with Blautia_A faecis exhibiting the highest and Agathobacter faecis exhibiting the lowest 126 median average ND ( Figure 1a ). Using per-species linear regression models, 13/20 species 127 exhibited higher ND in Control compared to both IBD subtypes, while the remaining 7 species 128 showed no difference, irrespective of existing differences in coverage breadth, coverage depth, 129 relative abundance levels and different age group ( Figure 1b, Supplementary Figure 2a,c; 130 

Methods ). 131 Reliable ND estimation relies on accurate sequence alignment, hence we wondered whether 132 systematic non-specific mapping across sequences of the same or different species could have 133 potentially confounded our estimates. To account for this possibility and validate our results, we 134 annotated species-specific marker genes within our species genomes (according to MetaPhlAn 135 DB, Methods ) and reran our models on this collection of genes ( Methods, Supplementary 136 

Figure 2b) . When analyzing only the marker genes, we observed similar results to the full genome 137 models, and the differences between IBD and Control groups were globally confirmed. 138

## Only a few genes display increased nucleotide diversity in IBD 151 To explore whether there could be differences in nucleotide diversity (ND) levels across different 152 regions of the genome, we next built a large set of linear regression models, one for each gene 153 within a species genome ( Methods, Supplementary Figure 3 ). We applied coverage-based and 154 prevalence-based quality filtering of genes, and modelled disease differences, identifying gene-155 specific trends in ND levels. As a result, we categorized the genes based on whether their ND 156 was invariant across study groups ( ND-invariant, gray), or their ND was either lower or higher in 157 IBD compared to Control ( ND-decreased, blue ; and ND-increased, orange , respectively; Figure 158 

2a, left-center ). Analogously to what was observed at the genome level, the same species that 159 exhibited a prominent reduction in nucleotide diversity had a large fraction of their genes 160 categorized as ND-decreased (such as B. wexlerae and A. hadrus ). These species had ~75% of 161 their genes categorized as ND-decreased, with at most 47% gene overlap between CD and UC 162 samples (intersection over union; Figure 2a, right ). In a similar manner, species that exhibited 163 no change in nucleotide diversity at the genome level, had most of the genes categorized as ND-164 invariant, with at most 82 individual genes categorized as ND-increased (in case of B. uniformis ), 165 which counted at most a 18% gene overlap between CD and UC. In order to validate the gene 166 categorization that resulted from gene-level linear regression coefficients, we also calculated 167 mean gene ND values (mean over subjects), distinguishing between Control and IBD, and 168 calculated the IBD-over-Control ratio for ND-decreased and ND-increased genes ( Figure 2b ). 169 Here, we confirmed that our gene categorization was valid, since ND-increased genes had higher 170 IBD-to-Control ratio compared to ND-decreased genes (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, ND-decreased 171 genes=586 [randomly sampled subset], ND-increased genes=45 [all]; Methods ). 172 Since nucleotide diversity estimates are affected by the number of polymorphic positions, as well 173 as their allele frequencies, we investigated the contribution of each of these components to the 174 ND levels measured ( Figure 2c-d ). We employed a similar strategy as before by using gene 175 polymorphic fraction as the basis for calculating the IBD-to-Control ratio ( Figure 2c ). Also here, 176 ND-increased genes had significantly higher IBD-to-Control ratio compared to ND-decreased 177 genes (Wilcoxon rank-sum test; Methods ). Finally, we tested the differential distribution of minor 178 allele frequencies in ND-decreased genes and ND-increased genes, comparing IBD to Control 179 subjects. ## Nonsynonymous variants are enriched in IBD across species 186 To reason whether the observed trends in gene-level nucleotide diversity (ND) could be linked to 187 adaptational processes happening in the IBD gut, we next investigated gene-level enrichment of 188 nonsynonymous variants at polymorphic positions within subjects ( Methods, Supplementary 189 

Figure 4a ). We modelled genes’ relative accumulation of nonsynonymous and synonymous 190 variants at polymorphic positions ( Nsyn-enrichment ) using a binomial generalized linear mixed-191 model on variant counts ( Methods ). For most of the 20 species, global Nsyn-enrichment was 192 identified in both CD and UC, with few exceptions where only CD reached statistical significance 193 (Supplementary Figure 4b ). In the model, we included dNdS, which accounts for 194 nonsynonymous enrichment at non-polymorphic positions, to approximate long-term genetic 195 divergence between strains. Across species, dNdS was negatively correlated with Nsyn-196 enrichment, as shown by the negative model coefficients ( Supplementary Figure 4b ). Using 197 model-derived gene-specific coefficients, we categorized genes in Nsyn-decreased (purple) and 198 

Nsyn-increased (yellow), based on whether they respectively exhibited a lower or higher Nsyn-199 enrichment in IBD compared to Control ( Figure 3a , Supplementary Figure 4c ). Some of the 200 species that previously had no ND-decreased genes and few ND-increased genes, exhibited the 201 largest fraction of Nsyn-increased genes (e.g. A. faecis and A. sp900066565 ; Figure 3a, left ). 202 CD and UC had a much more consistent overlap in Nsyn-increased genes (~90%), with few 203 species such as B. ovatus , B. longum and P. distasonis reaching only 51% or less overlap 204 (intersection over union; Figure 3a, right ). 205 To validate that the Nsyn-enrichment identified by the model could be observed directly on genes 206 using pNpS (nonsynonymous enrichment at polymorphic positions , Methods ), we calculated IBD-207 to-Control ratios based on group-level mean pNpS, and tested gene category statistical difference 208 (Nsyn-decreased=1283, Nsyn-increased=43263, Figure 3b-c ). We confirmed Nsyn-increased 209 genes had significantly higher mean pNpS IBD-to-Control ratio (Wilcoxon rank-sum test), as well 210 as higher mean dNdS IBD-to-Control ratio ( Figure 3d, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). Both pNpS- and 211 dNdS-derived statistics confirmed that Nsyn-variants were specifically enriched in IBD across a 212 subset of genes within the species’ genomes. Moreover, we also tested whether ND-categorized 213 genes carried IBD Nsyn-enrichment, and only pNpS resulted to be differential between ND 214 categories ( Figure 3e-g, Wilcoxon rank-sum test). 215 These results showed that nonsynonymous polymorphisms were more prominent in IBD-216 associated strains compared to Control-associated ones and this was a common feature across 217 the species investigated. The accumulation of nonsynonymous polymorphisms in IBD strains 218 suggests specific mutational and selective processes happening in the IBD gut, potentially 219 facilitating bacterial adaptation to environmental stress. 220

## Gene-level nucleotide diversity and nonsynonymous variants enrichment 221 

## inform on gene evolutionary propensity under IBD stress 222 Thus far, we measured gene-level nucleotide diversity (ND) and gene-level nonsynonymous 223 variant (Nsyn) enrichment. To offer a unified framework describing gene propensity to 224 evolutionary change under IBD stress, we explored using ND and Nsyn model coefficients as 225 axes of diversity, describing a two dimensional space, where genes can be localized in one of 226 four gene categories: (1) adaptive with ND-increased & Nsyn-increased; (2) neutrally-divergent 227 with ND-increased only; (3) functionally-divergent with Nsyn-increased only; and (4) invariant with 228 ND-decreased & Nsyn-decreased ( Figure 4a ). As defined, adaptive genes exhibit higher genetic 229 diversity in IBD along both ND and Nsyn axes, while neutrally-divergent and functionally-divergent 230 genes exhibit higher genetic diversity along only one of the two axes. According to this framework, 231 species exhibited differences in gene distribution and occupancy of the different categories, with 232 visible differences between CD (top) and UC (bottom) patients ( Figure 4b, Supplementary 233 

Figure 5a ). Some species, such as B. faecis and B. wexlerae exhibited large variance along the 234 ND axis, while B. longum and P. vulgatus exhibited only variance along the Nsyn axis. To 235 investigate whether gene distribution across the four categories could be correlated with certain 236 metabolic pathways or cellular functions, we computed functional enrichment propensities 237 (logarithmic odds-ratios, log(OR)), grouping genes in KEGG pathways, BRITE hierarchical 238 groups, EC numbers and KO terms ( N functions with ≧5 genes=242; Methods ). Specifically, we 239 computed the odds-ratios of being categorized as adaptive , functionally-divergent or neutrally-240 

divergent for each functional group ( Methods ). Odds-ratios for the adaptive category identified 241 62 functional groups, as either strongly enriched with- or depleted of diversity in IBD [ absolute 242 

log(OR)>=1 ] ( Figure 4c, left ). For these functional groups, we highlighted both neutrally-divergent 243 and functionally-divergent odds-ratios, together with species-specific gene contributions to the 244 functional group ( Figure 4c, center-right ). # Discussion 267 268 Healthy microbiomes may support large and diverse populations of conspecific strains, which can 269 usually stably colonize and diversify for years within the host(36, 40, 43–46). Factors that alter 270 the diversity of the existing pool of strains in a community can be seen as ecological disturbances 271 (e.g. bottlenecks), which filter out poorly adapted strains before allowing for recolonization 272 events(47–50). 273 IBD acts as a strong and prolonged ecological filter, with escalating bouts of gut inflammation 274 causing drastic remodeling of the microbial community within the gut(51). As we showed in the 275 first section, more than half of the species tested in our analyses exhibited signs of reduced strain 276 richness in IBD, while the remaining few showed no change. On one hand, reduced strain 277 richness in IBD could result from the lack of either strain retention or recolonization processes, 278 but in both cases the IBD gut exhibits overall lower ecosystem carrying capacity, lacking open 279 and stable niches that can be occupied, potentially due to mucosal inflammation(52). On the other 280 hand, the lack of change in the diversity of some species across disease groups is surprising, 281 given that drastic compositional changes are observed ( Supplementary Figure 2c ). For these 282 species, one possible interpretation could be that already at homeostasis, a few strains tend to 283 dominate, and the IBD gut has the potential to accommodate all of them(40, 53). Alternatively, 284 these species might be characterized by intrinsic capacity to survive and adapt to the IBD stress 285 environment. The species that exhibited no change in our analysis, such as P. vulgatus , B. 286 

uniformis and B. longum , are not opportunistic facultative anaerobes, such as the ones belonging 287 to the Enterobacteriaceae family, which easily adapt to the more aerobic gut environment driven 288 by inflammation(54, 55). These species are prevalent and at times highly abundant human gut 289 species, suggesting that high prevalence and abundance might indeed be signs of high resistance 290 to ecological stress. 291 Resilience to IBD stress could be encoded in a species metabolic capacity, in particular by 292 encoding complete metabolic modules enabling for the biosynthesis or assembly of essential 293 nutrients and cofactors, which are often turned into rare commodities under gut inflammatory 294 conditions(29, 56–58). Thus, our analysis focused heavily on genetic diversity at the individual 295 gene level, where we identified a specific gene subset across species that exhibited an increase 296 of nucleotide diversity in IBD, in contrast to the rest of the genome (ND-increased genes). This 297 suggested that a gene-level diversity analysis has the potential to identify gene functions that tend 298 to accumulate polymorphisms, and that the retention of these polymorphisms might be explained 299 by an acquired survival advantage in the context of IBD stress. 300 Additionally, we measured the overall enrichment of nonsynonymous diversity at the gene level 301 and identified gene-specific enrichments unique to IBD strains. These analyses suggest that 302 overall IBD-associated strains seem to undergo a specific selection process, where functional 303 innovations (nonsynonymous variants) are selected for. Also in this case, some species exhibited 304 a more prominent nonsynonymous variant enrichment than others, not necessarily overlapping 305 the nucleotide diversity enrichment. Assuming IBD stress exerts high purifying selection pressure 306

on the gut microbiome, the identified functional variants in IBD-associated strains could be likely 307 the result of recent adaptive diversification, rather than relaxed purifying selection(59). The trigger 308 to these nonsynonymous mutations could be the exposure to oxidative stress (ROS and NOS), 309 which triggers DNA damage(60–62). When DNA damage occurs, such as the oxidation of a 310 guanine (8-oxo-G), stalled DNA replication triggers recruitment of an error-prone DNA 311 polymerase, which often incorporates the wrong nucleotide, increasing the likelihood for 312 nonsynonymous codon change. The process of stress-induced mutagenesis is very prominent in 313 the context of a variety of environmental stresses, including antibiotic stress(63, 64). Another 314 trigger for the biased incorporation of nucleotides under stress could be nutrient limitation. It has 315 been described that nutrient limitation was the basis of evolutionary variants among marine 316 bacterial genomes, specifically under limited availability of nitrogen(65). In IBD stress, many 317 nutrients are limited, though whether this kind of nutrient limitation is responsible for specific 318 evolutionary innovations in IBD remains unknown. 319 Finally, we focused on combining all the evidence in one unified framework. We defined four gene 320 categories describing differential propensity to evolutionary change under stress, and identified 321 functions that were enriched in overall nucleotide diversity and nonsynonymous variants in IBD 322 (adaptive functions). We highlighted motility, secretion system components, oxidative stress 323 management proteins and many others that open interesting avenues for future mechanistic work. 324 Specifically, the gene group associated with flagellar assembly was one of the biggest functional 325 groups enriched in the adaptive category. Some studies have highlighted that mutated flagellar 326 components may induce cell aggregation, allow for better host colonization, or possibly reduce 327 intracellular ROS production and mutagenesis(66–70). In the context IBD stress in the gut, further 328 experimental work will be needed to elucidate whether increased genetic diversity in flagellar 329 components positively or negatively impact survival in the gut by optimizing or inactivating 330 bacterial motility. 331 We also identified diversity enrichment in proteins associated with housekeeping functions. 332 Among them, we found ribosomal proteins, which are commonly used as universal marker genes 333 to infer phylogenetic relationships between distantly related bacteria(71, 72). Recent work has 334 performed a large-scale phylogenetic analysis using a set of universal marker genes (including 335 ribosomal proteins and an RNA polymerase subunit) and identified IBD-associated strains that 336 were later confirmed to be likely IBD-adapted, based on the metabolic functions that they 337 encoded(29). This work suggested that IBD-associated strains might carry an evolutionary 338 signature in their universal marker genes, and this signature might derive from stress exposure. 339 Other in vitro studies on E. coli confirm that, in response to long-term starvation, strains 340 reproducibly acquire mutations in RNA polymerase subunits (rpoB and rpoC), to the extent that 341 coexisting strains can be tracked over time by means of RNA Pol subunit barcoding(73–75). 342 Collectively, these findings point towards the occurrence of global genetic remodeling in bacteria 343 under stress, and understanding the nature of these genetic changes might further uncover 344 unknown strategies of bacterial tolerance and interaction with the environment. 345 This work provides robust findings on the group-level relationship between disease and strain 346 diversity, but also ignores sources of heterogeneity within disease groups, such as severity level 347 and disease duration. These factors might be investigated in future work to elucidate whether 348 remissive or active disease states are associated with different levels of strain diversity and 349 whether disease duration could be associated with different kinds of genetic diversity. 350

This work highlights the potential of using intra-species gene-level diversity as a sensor 356 for environmental stress, and suggests the presence of stress-derived evolutionary signatures in 357 the genomes of commensal species in the IBD gut. 358 359

## Metagenomic data cohorts 385 We compiled a collection of gut metagenomic sequencing data amounting to 2397 samples from 386 the following cohorts: (1) the SZ pediatric cohort including 134 samples 387 (Control=29,CD=61,UC=44), available at BioProject PRJNA1265906 ; (2) the LifeLines-388 DEEP_followup adult cohort including 337 samples from healthy individuals available under Data 389 Access Agreement with the UMCG Department of Genetics to access EGAD00001006959 data 390 repository; (3) the 1000IBD adult cohort including 352 samples (CD=203,UC=149) available 391 under Data Transfer Agreement with the University Medical Center Groningen and Prof. Dr. R.K. 392 Weersma to access EGAD00001003991 and EGAD00001004194 data repositories; the HMP2 393 mixed cohort including 1574 samples (Control=424,CD=714,UC=436) available at 394 https://ibdmdb.org. 395 

## Selection of the species 396 We identified species suitable for our strain-level metagenomic analysis by calculating species 397 prevalence across the cohorts described above. We used Metaphlan4(76) and Metaphlan DB v. 398 mpa_vJan21_CHOCOPhlAnSGB_202103 to taxonomically profile the samples, then we filtered 399 out species instances with relative abundance < 0.01% and selected species whose prevalence 400 reached at least 50% in each study group (Control, CD, UC). Since SZ and HMP2 cohorts include 401 multiple samples from the same individuals, we calculated prevalence by considering any one 402 sample with the species present per subject. From here we retrieved a list of 20 species whose 403 prevalence and median relative abundance is reported in Supplementary Table 1 .404

## Strain-level metrics from metagenomic reads with inStrain 421 

inStrain database 422 We used inStrain(77) to retrieve strain-level metrics directly from metagenomic reads. We first 423 downloaded the UHGGv1 full set of representative genomes, identified coding regions with 424 Prodigal(78) and built a bowtie2(79) database including genome GCA_964248265.1 as a 425 representative for Phocaeicola vulgatus. Then we mapped all samples against the UHGGv1 DB 426 using bowtie2 and samtools(80) to sort and index the mapping files. Then we ran inStrain with -427 

-database_mode which sets some parameters that are appropriate for mapping metagenomic 428 reads to an external DB of genomes. This mostly ensures that the genome ANI threshold used is 429 (1) not too stringent to allow for sample-specific strains to be mapped to their representative 430 genome in the database, and (2) not too relaxed to ensure accurate, species-specific sequence 431 mapping. Additionally we used different read pairing parameters based on whether the 432 metagenomic samples were paired-end or single-end: for the single-end read samples (SZ 433 cohort) we used --non_discordant , while for all the other paired-end read samples we used 434 

--paired_only (default). 435 inStrain by default filtered out samples that had less than 1x coverage depth for a species of 436 interest, so the number of samples that were positive for at least one of the 20 species selected 437 was 1435. 438 

Coverage breadth filtering and the B. longum case 439 We gathered all inStrain genome-level outputs and filtered out instances where a species of 440 interest did not reach a minimum coverage breadth threshold. The recommended minimum 441 coverage breadth by inStrain documentation is 0.5, but we noticed that, applying this threshold, 442 one of the 20 species selected, Bifidobacterium longum , was underrepresented in our dataset 443 compared to our prevalence expectations. The UHGGv1 database indeed includes a B. longum 444 

subsp. infantis representative genome and this likely resulted in the lower coverage breadth 445 observed for B. longum in our data, which likely belongs to B. longum subsp. longum . In 446 

Supplementary Figure 1a it is visible how all instances of B. longum genomes are indeed right 447 below the recommended coverage breadth threshold, so we decided to use 0.45 as the coverage 448 breadth threshold specific to B. longum . Additionally, we filtered out species instances where the 449 genome had at least a coverage breadth equal to 0.2 at a minimum coverage depth equal to 5x 450 (breadth_minCov >=0.2 , minCov=5x , default). Applying these coverage filtering thresholds, 451 1408 samples were positive for at least one species of interest. 452 

Coverage depth and nucleotide diversity 453 Among the metrics provided by inStrain, we started analysing nucleotide diversity (ND). inStrain 454 calculates ND according to its original implementation(81) and by default only using positions that 455 reach a minimum coverage depth threshold ( minCov=5x ). ND is provided both at the genome 456 and gene level, but to have better control on ND estimation accuracy we decided to use only 457 gene-level ND estimates for genes that had breadth_minCov >=0.2. 458

## Linear regression modelling 460 

Genome-level nucleotide diversity 461 To obtain genome-level nucleotide diversity averages, we calculated the mean nucleotide 462 diversity across genes previously filtered by breadth_minCov , adjusting nucleotide diversity by 463 the product of breadth_minCov and gene length, to account for nucleotide diversity estimates 464 calculated from poorly covered genes, without raising the general threshold for gene inclusion. 465 We did the same for calculating the genome-level mean for coverage depth and breadth and 466 compared the unweighted and weighted genome-level nucleotide diversity means as shown in 467 

Supplementary Figure 1b . Then we used these weighted metrics to run a linear regression 468 model estimating the effect of disease on genome-level nucleotide diversity per species, adjusting 469 for coverage depth and breadth and age group, as follows: lm( calc_genome_metric ~470 

Phenotype + log(calc_genome_coverage) + calc_genome_breadth_minCov + cohort, 471 

weights = breadth_minCov* gene_length) .472 Results from this set of models are reported in Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary 473 

Figure 2a . As input to the linear regression model, we used only cross-sectional data, so for 474 subjects with more than one sample available, we selected the sample with the highest genome-475 level mean breadth_minCov . Finally, we collected model results for all 20 species, used the 476 lmerTest (v.3.2-1) R package to retrieve statistical significance for the model coefficients and 477 applied the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to account for multiple hypothesis testing. 478 

Marker gene-level nucleotide diversity 479 To evaluate whether the results from the genome-level nucleotide diversity means could reflect 480 variability across genes included in the mean rather than the whole genome, we decided to rerun 481 the linear regression model using nucleotide diversity means coming from core genes only. To 482 identify genes that could represent the core genome, we searched for the Metaphlan DB species-483 specific marker genes in our set of genes. For this, we mapped the 20 species representative 484 genomes against the Metaphlan DB (v. mpa_vJan21_CHOCOPhlAnSGB_202103) using 485 BLAST(82) and then selected for the best alignments using their bitscore. Out of 2388 total 486 markers associated with the 20 species, we confidently identified 2296. Then we calculated 487 genome-level mean as described previously and ran the linear regression model as before. 488 Results from this set of models are reported in Supplementary Table 4 and Supplementary 489 

Figure 2b .490 

Gene-level nucleotide diversity 491 To obtain gene-level disease effects on nucleotide diversity we ran a large set of gene-level linear 492 regression models, since parametrization for the full set of genes in the previous model set did 493 not manage to converge, even with the introduction of different optimizers. For this reason, we 494 ran a linear regression model per gene and then collectively applied bayesian shrinkage using 495 the R package ashr (2.2-63) to reduce overinflated coefficients. Genes that did not reach per 496 study group a prevalence>0.2 and were present in a minimum number of subjects of 10 were not 497 used to build a model. Gene-level nonsynonymous variants enrichment 510 To model the relative gene-level accumulation of nonsynonymous and synonymous variants, we 511 decided to use raw variant counts ( SNV_N_count and SNV_S_count in inStrain, respectively) in 512 a binomial generalized linear mixed-effects model using glmmTMB function from glmmTMB (v. 513 1.1.13) R package, as follows: SNV_N_count, SNV_S_count ~ Phenotype +514 

dNdS_substitutions + log_cov_scaled + breadth_minCov + (1 + Phenotype 515 

|| gene), family = binomial(), control = glmmTMBControl( optimizer =516 

nlminb). Thanks to the binomial structure, the model interprets data from genes with few 517 variants as less reliable, and with gene-specific random effects, as in (1+Phenotype|gene), 518 we are able to estimate disease-specific effects for all genes, while reducing uncertainty by 519 borrowing confidence from the entire distribution of coefficients (internal shrinkage). Moreover, 520 gene-specific effects help accounting for baseline differences across genes (codon usage, GC 521 content, gene-specific mutation tendencies) which, if left unaccounted for, can skew modelling 522 results. In this respect, instead of using variant counts, we could have used pNpS (polymorphic 523 nonsynonymous-to-synonymous ratio). pNpS is similar to its non-polymorphic version, dNdS 524 (non-polymorphic nonsynonymous-to-synonymous ratio), but less known. Metrics like pNpS and 525 dNdS normalize for the actual number of nonsynonymous and synonymous opportunities in the 526 sequence, however, ratio-based metrics have the disadvantage of being less interpretable in 527 modelling. For these reasons, we decided to model gene-level nonsynonymous variant 528 enrichment at polymorphic positions using variant counts, but then to use pNpS to confirm the 529 model results. In fact, after modelling, we selected the genes that were categorized concordantly 530 across CD and UC, for both Nsyn-decreased and Nsyn-increased genes, and then calculated 531 their mean pNpS over subjects, for Control and IBD separately. Finally, we used these pNpS 532 means to compute IBD-to-Control ratios and test statistical difference (Nsyn-decreased=1283, 533 Nsyn-increased=43263). In the model, we also included dNdS, to approximate long-term genetic 534 divergence existing between the strains within the subject and the reference genome, which could 535 confound our results, when considering that different subjects could harbor strains that differ quite 536 a lot in their genomic background. For this reason, we did not want to detect more 537 nonsynonymous polymorphisms, only because some individuals might harbor a different 538 uncharacterized subspecies. 539

## Functional enrichment 540 To retrieve gene’s KEGG Orthology (KO) terms and definitions we ran KofamScan on all the 20 541 genomes protein coding sequences provided by Prodigal, and selected only confident annotations 542 (score > 0, N=361). Then, for each KO term we (1) extracted EC numbers from the KO terms 543 definition, (2) retrieved KEGG pathway identifiers and definitions (from 544 https://rest.kegg.jp/link/pathway/ko and https://rest.kegg.jp/list/pathway respectively), and (3) 545 used the KEGGREST R package in batch mode to collect BRITE hierarchies. These last three 546 steps allowed us to collect general functional annotation encompassing more KO terms together, 547 while providing annotation for most of them. Before calculating odds-ratios we removed BRITE 548 annotations reporting “Unknown function” or “General function prediction only” and excluded 549 functions with less than 5 genes to reduce weakly supported results. Finally we manually curated 550 annotations that reported eukaryotic-associated functions, opting for homologous annotations 551 suitable for prokaryotes. neutrally-divergent and functionally-divergent ), based on their ND and Nsyn coefficients, with a 554 smoothing correction (+0.5). In particular, odds-ratios were defined as follows = cat_pos_in +555
