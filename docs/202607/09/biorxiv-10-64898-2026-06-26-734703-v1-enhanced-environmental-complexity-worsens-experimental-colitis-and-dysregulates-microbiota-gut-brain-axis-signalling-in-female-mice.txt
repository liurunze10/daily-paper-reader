# Abstract 30 31 Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by colonic 32 inflammation and bloody diarrhoea. Accumulating evidence suggests that UC not only affects 33 the intestinal tract, but also distant organs including the brain. Environmental factors are key 34 determinants of the disease course, yet the impact and potential disease modifying effects of 35 living environment complexity on microbiota-gut-brain axis signalling during colitis remain 36 unclear. To address this gap, we investigated how enhanced environmental complexity (EC) 37 affects the disease course and gut-brain axis signalling during experimental colitis in mice. 38 Our results show that EC exacerbates dextran sulphate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis in 39 female mice, but not in male mice, as evidenced by greater weight loss and higher disease 40 activity. Immune cell profiling across the gut-brain axis reveals strong effects of DSS 41 treatment on colonic, circulating and brain immune cell populations and a restriction of 42 central nervous system (CNS) T cell infiltration due to EC. In addition, female EC/DSS mice 43 have higher circulating corticosterone levels than controls indicating chronic stress. 44 Metabolomics across the gut-brain axis revealed that EC exacerbates colitis-induced 45 metabolite perturbations in plasma, brain tissue, brain interstitial and cerebrospinal fluid. 46 Notably, microbiota-derived metabolites, including deoxycholic acid and trimethylamine-N-47 oxide (TMAO), are increased in EC/DSS mice, concordant with EC-associated microbiome 48 changes and anxiety-like behaviour. Overall, this study indicates that EC worsens 49 experimental colitis in female mice and directs microbiota-gut-brain axis signalling during 50 colitis towards a less favourable state. From a translational perspective, this study highlights 51 the importance of environmental factors for a sex-specific disease course of UC and 52 associated neurobehavioral comorbidities. 53 54 55 56 57 58 Highlights 59

# Introduction 72 Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) denotes a family of chronic gastrointestinal disorders, 73 including the two major entities Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC). These 74 diseases are characterized by symptoms such as abdominal pain, diarrhoea and bloody 75 stools, and they affect between five and ten million people worldwide (Fakhoury et al. 2014; 76 Wang et al. 2023). In many cases, IBD not only involves the gut, but also affects other 77 organs such as joints, eyes, skin, liver or even the brain, in so-called extraintestinal 78 manifestations (Veloso 2011; Morís 2014; Rogler et al. 2021). Regarding the latter, it has 79 been found that IBD patients are at a higher risk for mood disorders such as anxiety, 80 depression and bipolar disorder (Bernstein et al. 2019; Bisgaard et al. 2022; Massironi et al. 81 2025; Petracco et al. 2025), suggesting a link between peripheral pathophysiological 82 processes and neuropsychiatric disorders. 83 Psychological stress has been identified as a key factor in triggering IBD exacerbations 84 (Fairbrass et al. 2022; Ge et al. 2022). It is known that chronic psychosocial and physical 85 stress have a marked impact on both brain (McEwen et al. 2015) and gut function 86 (Elsenbruch and Enck 2017), and that these effects are interrelated. Animal studies confirm 87 these relationships, as experimental gastrointestinal inflammation, an endogenous stressor 88 itself, alters emotional-affective behaviour and stress reactivity (Painsipp et al. 2011; Hassan 89 

et al. 2014; Reichmann et al. 2015; Nyuyki et al. 2018) and experimental psychosocial stress 90 is known to aggravate inflammation and trigger relapses in animal models of IBD (Reber 91 2012). Furthermore, dysbiosis plays an important role in the pathogenesis of IBD (Iliev et al. 92 2025). Microbial factors are contributing to IBD-associated neuropsychiatric comorbidities via 93 the microbiota-gut-brain (MGB) axis, a term that denotes the multi-directional communication 94 between its components (Skonieczna-żydecka et al. 2018). For example, acute 95 administration of dextran sodium sulphate (DSS) induces colitis, dysbiosis as well as anxiety 96 and recognition memory deficits in mice, which can be normalized by Lactobacillus -based 97 probiotics, directly linking intestinal inflammation, microbial shifts, and CNS function (Emge et 98 

al. 2016). In addition, when colitis is induced at weaning, mice exhibit long-lasting cognitive 99 and anxiety-like deficits with hippocampal neuroinflammation and dysbiosis persisting into 100 adulthood, demonstrating long-term MGB axis disruptions by early-life intestinal inflammation 101 (Salvo et al. 2020). Targeting the MGB axis therapeutically has been suggested as a 102 promising way to alleviate comorbid neurobehavioral disturbances and multiple potential 103 therapeutics are under active investigation, such as faecal matter transplantation, 104 psychobiotics, microbial metabolite supplementation and vagus nerve stimulation (Chen et 105 

al. 2018; Sinniger et al. 2020; Feng et al. 2023; Brown et al. 2024; Petracco et al. 2025) 106

While it is clear that adverse environmental factors, such as chronic stress, can markedly 107 disrupt MGB axis signalling during IBD (Bisgaard et al. 2022; Bonaz et al. 2024), it remains 108 to be determined whether putative beneficial environmental factors such as environmental 109 enrichment exert an equally strong effect. Usually, enrichment refers to a number of 110 procedures used in animal husbandry and scientific research aiming at the improvement of 111 animal welfare and the promotion of species-specific behaviour under captivity (Kulpa-Eddy 112 

et al. 2005). These procedures typically involve more complex home cage environments 113 (high environmental complexity, EC) with the aim to provide sufficient space for the 114 expression of a wide spectrum of normal behaviours and the provision of natural and/or 115 artificial objects to interact with, in order to stimulate mental and/or physical activity (Taylor et 116 

al. 2023). EC is also known for its powerful, beneficial behavioural consequences and its 117 modulating effects on neurobiology and brain ultrastructure (van Praag et al. 2000; 118 Nithianantharajah and Hannan 2006). For example, EC improves learning and memory 119 (Simpson and Kelly 2011), reduces anxiety (Reichmann et al. 2016), promotes stress 120 resilience (Crofton et al. 2015) and ameliorates disease phenotypes in animal models of 121 neuropsychiatric disease (Pang and Hannan 2013). In contrast, there is also evidence that 122 increased EC could have negative effects on laboratory animals. Specifically, it has been 123 shown that EC induces agonistic encounters and increases aggressive behaviour in male 124 mice, leading to increased anxiety-like behaviour, higher corticosterone levels and eventually 125 to physical and psychological injuries (Cabrera-Muñoz etal., 2022; McQuaid et al., 2018; 126 Sowndharya & Rajan, 2024). EC has also been associated with non-adaptive behaviours in 127 rodents as well as with enhanced alcohol intake (Berardo et al. 2016; González-Pardo et al. 128 2019; Suárez et al. 2020; Corredor et al. 2022). In addition, it has been found that EC can 129 lead to chronic stress (Moncek et al. 2004; Domínguez-Oliva et al. 2025) and behavioural 130 despair (Guven et al. 2022). 131 In a study investigating the effects of EC on stress-induced neuronal activation, we have 132 previously shown that colitis modulates the effects of EC on stress-induced neural activity, 133 indicating an interaction between EC and gut-brain axis signalling (Reichmann et al., 2013). 134 In addition, we and others found that EC can enhance peripheral inflammation and increase 135 immune system activity, suggesting that EC might have negative effects in animal models of 136 inflammatory diseases (Brod et al., 2017; Reichmann et al., 2013). In this study, we therefore 137 set out to systematically investigate, if EC exerts positive or negative effects on MGB axis 138 signalling in an animal model of UC. We investigated how EC affects gut-brain axis signalling 139 in the course of DSS colitis, and if EC is able to modify colitis-induced changes to behaviour 140 and neurobiology. To gain mechanistic insights, we characterized colitis and EC effects on all 141 levels of the MGB axis by assessing inflammatory processes, intestinal dysbiosis and 142 metabolic shifts. 143

Enhanced environmental complexity (EC) exacerbates DSS-induced colonic 145 

inflammation 146 To analyse if EC modulates MGB axis signalling in the course of colitis, we first investigated 147 the effects of DSS treatment after 9 weeks of EC housing on disease activity and colonic 148 inflammation in male and female mice (Fig. 1A; Suppl. Fig. 1). We found that female mice 149 kept in standard environment (SE) and treated with DSS for 7 days lost weight at the end of 150 the treatment period (day 8-10) compared with baseline (day 1), which partially recovered 151 after stopping DSS treatment (day 11-12; Fig. 1B). Interestingly, however, this body-weight 152 loss was much more pronounced in female DSS-treated EC mice than in SE-housed controls 153 (time x group interaction: F (21, 279) = 25.550; P<0.001), and these animals did not regain any 154 of the lost weight after stopping DSS (Fig. 1B). This finding was not the result of increased 155 DSS intake in the EC group, given that the amount of liquid consumed during the treatment 156 period was similar between the two colitis groups (Suppl. Fig. 2A). DSS treatment also 157 significantly shortened the colon of female mice (DSS main effect: F (1, 33) = 43.050; P<0.001; 158 Fig. 1C), and while female EC/DSS animals had on average the shortest colons (5.890 ± 159 0.211 cm), this failed to reach statistical significance compared to SE/DSS mice (6.440 ± 160 0.197 cm; P = 0.184). As expected, DSS treatment increased the disease activity index (DAI; 161 main effect DSS: F (1, 34) = 81.100; P<0.001) compared to non-treated mice, but female 162 EC/DSS mice had higher DAI scores than SE/DSS mice both at the end of DSS treatment 163 (day 8; DSS x housing interaction: F (1, 33) = 6.750; P=0.014; Fig. 1D) and five days later (day 164 12; DSS x housing interaction: F (1, 34) = 8.264; P=0.007; Suppl. Fig. 2B). axis 182 Previous studies found that EC is able to alter the activity of the immune system (Brod et al. 183 2017; Xiao et al. 2019). Therefore, we investigated whether the more severe disease course 184 of female DSS-treated mice housed in EC is a consequence of enhanced colonic immune 185 cell infiltration (flow cytometry gating strategy in Suppl. Fig. 3). We found that innate immune 186 cells including monocytes, macrophages, eosinophils and neutrophils are more abundant in 187 the colon of DSS-treated mice compared to non-treated controls independent of housing 188 conditions (Fig. 2A-D; DSS main effect: F (1, 35) = 61.130; P<0.001 for monocytes; F (1, 35) =189 13.950; P<0.001 for macrophages; F (1, 35) = 21.750; P<0.001 for eosinophils; F (1, 35) = 25.350; 190 P<0.001 for neutrophils). In addition, we detected higher numbers of T helper, cytotoxic T 191 cells and natural killer (NK) cells in DSS-treated mice (Fig. 2E-G; DSS main effect: F (1, 15) =192 9.898; P=0.007 for T helper cells; F (1, 15) = 10.900; P=0.005 for cytotoxic T cells; F (1, 35) =193 6.727; P=0.014 for NK cells), but no groups difference in NK T cell numbers (Fig. 2H). EC 194 had no effect on any of these cell populations in healthy animals and it also did not modify 195 the DSS-induced colonic cell infiltration. EC heightens colitis-induced metabolite alterations across the gut-brain axis 228 Given that female EC/DSS mice did not show changes in the number of circulating or tissue-229 resident immune cells compared to SE/DSS mice, we next investigated effects on 230 inflammatory mediators and metabolites. To do this, we first examined circulating 231 corticosterone levels to assess the stress levels of the animals. We found that both colitis 232 (DSS main effect: F (1, 35) = 18.730; P<0.001) and EC (EC main effect: F (1, 35) = 16.690; 233 P<0.001) increased corticosterone (Fig. 5A), but no treatment X housing interaction, 234 suggesting that both interventions increase stress levels independent of each other. 235 However, planned comparisons revealed that mice in the EC/DSS group had the highest 236 corticosterone levels (80.893 ± 14.804), which were significantly higher than in the SE/DSS 237 group (37.310 ± 11.825; P=0.001; Fig. 5A). This indicates a synergistic negative impact of 238 both interventions on the stress axis. In addition to that, we also examined pro-inflammatory 239 cytokine levels in the blood circulation, as potential mediators of the negative consequences 240 of EC during colitis (Fig. 5B-F). Next, we explored whether EC and/or colitis affect metabolic profiles along the gut-brain axis 251 of female mice by performing plasma, brain tissue, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and brain 252 interstitial fluid (ISF) metabolomics. After applying analytical-quality criteria, the detected 253 metabolite numbers were 330 in plasma, 356 in brain tissue, 275 in CSF, and 286 in ISF 254 samples. Detailed analysis revealed that EC and colitis induced numerous significant 255 metabolite alterations in all sample types investigated (Suppl. Table 1). Based on absolute 256 numbers, we found the largest metabolic changes in the CSF of healthy mice kept in EC 257 (EC/CO). Compared to SE-housed controls (SE/CO), 111 metabolites were more abundant in 258 the EC/CO group (Fig. 6A; Suppl. Table 2), suggesting that EC strongly increases the 259 secretion of various metabolites from the brain into the CSF and/or the reabsorption of 260 metabolites from the CSF. Even more metabolite changes (123 significantly different 261 metabolites) were detected when comparing EC/CO with EC/DSS. The vast majority (121) of 262 those metabolites were again more abundant in the EC/CO group (Fig. 6B; Suppl. Table 2) 263 suggesting that the CSF metabolome of healthy EC mice is very different from the 264 metabolome of all other investigated groups. Given that the CSF metabolome of the EC/DSS 265 group is much more similar to the SE/CO group than the EC/CO group, it appears that colitis 266 disrupts the ability of EC to alter CSF metabolite dynamics. Supporting this view, we found a 267 high overlap of 98 metabolites altered both in the EC/CO vs. SE/CO comparison and also the 268 EC/CO vs. EC/DSS comparison (Suppl. Fig.5A). Similar findings were made in the plasma of 269 the EC/CO group (Suppl. Table 3), where the vast majority of significantly different 270 metabolites had again higher concentrations compared to SE/CO (45 out of 50 significantly 271 different metabolites) and EC/DSS (53 out of 63 metabolites). Most of the metabolites altered 272 between EC/CO and SE/CO belong to the class of amino acids and derivatives or to energy 273 metabolism metabolites (Fig. 6C; Suppl. In the current manuscript our aim was to study the effects of EC, an environmental 494 factor that is considered beneficial for laboratory mice (van Praag et al. 2000; 495 Nithianantharajah and Hannan 2006), in a murine model of ulcerative colitis. For this, we 496 characterized the effects of EC on all levels of the gut-brain axis including the gastrointestinal 497 microbiota, gastrointestinal tissue, systemic circulation, brain tissue, brain interstitial fluid as 498 well as cerebrospinal fluid. Against expectations, we found that EC had a sex-specific 499 negative impact on experimental colitis. Female mice with colitis kept in EC had a more 500 severe disease course and signs of enhanced peripheral inflammation compared to mice 501 kept in SE. These differences were accompanied by metabolite alterations along the gut-502 brain axis, compartment-specific changes in immune cell compositions, behavioural 503 alterations as well as changes to the gastrointestinal microbiome. 504 It is widely accepted that EC is a meaningful refinement procedure, often used as part of the 505 3Rs in animal research (Kulpa-Eddy et al. 2005).
