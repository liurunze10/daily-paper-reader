Abstract 23 

The gut microbiota influences colorectal cancer (CRC) progression, primarily through the 24 

secretion of small molecule metabolites. While numerous microbial products are known to drive 25 

CRC, endogenous protective mechanisms remain largely uncharacterized. Utilizing a folate 26 

metabolomics platform, we demonstrate that the healthy gut microbiota produces folinic acid (FA), 27 

a known chemotherapeutic adjuvant also known as leucovorin. This microbially derived folinic 28 

acid is progressively depleted in mouse models of colitis-associated CRC and in human clinical 29 

metagenomic cohorts with advancing disease severity. Mechanistically, folinic acid acts as a 30 

signaling molecule that directly binds and inhibits the intracellular protease calpain-2. This 31 

interaction stabilizes epithelial E-cadherin protein expression and suppresses CRC epithelial-to-32 

mesenchymal transition driving metastasis. Genetically manipulating gut microbial production of 33 

FA is sufficient to modulate CRC in vivo , even in the presence of chronic inflammation. This study 34 

reframes folinic acid from a chemotherapeutic enhancer to an endogenous microbial metabolite 35 

that actively suppresses CRC progression. 36

Main 37 

Leucovorin, or folinic acid, is a naturally occurring folate metabolite widely used in chemotherapy 38 

regimens designed to treat advanced stages of colorectal cancer (CRC) 1,2 . A combinatorial 39 

treatment regimen of folinic acid and 5-flurouracil, often with other cytotoxic agents, is the current 40 

standard of care for neoadjuvant, adjuvant, and palliative therapy for CRC 3. Folinic acid is known 41 

to enhance cytotoxic effects of 5-flurouracil by forming a ternary complex that stabilizes and 42 

inhibits thymidylate synthase, an enzyme crucial for DNA synthesis and repair 4.43 

Pharmacologically, folinic acid is versatile as it is used both to enhance 5-flurouracil cytotoxicity 44 

and to counteract the cytotoxic effects of antifolate chemotherapeutics such as methotrexate 5.45 

Both applications of folinic acid require participation in the one-carbon metabolism cycle. Unlike 46 

folic acid, folinic acid is naturally occurring and biologically active as it does not require 47 

dihydrofolate reductase for conversion into tetrahydrofolate for entry into the folate cycle 6.48 

However, recent evidence suggests that folinic acid can also function as a signaling molecule in 49 

cells, potentiating the cytotoxicity of non-fluoropyrimidine agents such as the proteasome inhibitor 50 

bortezomib through the upregulation of apoptotic signaling 7. This suggests a multifaceted anti-51 

cancer role for folinic acid, with yet unknown therapeutic potential that extends beyond the classic 52 

ternary complex with thymidylate synthase. Uncovering these signaling pathways may 53 

revolutionize therapy for CRC, which remains the second leading cause of cancer mortality 54 

worldwide despite advancements in screening and diagnosis 8.55 

56 

As an essential component of the colon, and thus of the CRC tumor microenvironment, the gut 57 

microbiota has been associated with CRC progression 9,10 . A healthy gut microbiome plays a 58 

critical role in the maintenance of gut homeostasis by supporting digestion, producing metabolites, 59 

and regulating the immune system 11 . When this microbial balance is disrupted, a dysbiotic 60 

microbiota can contribute to CRC progression by promoting chronic inflammation, increasing 61 

immune dysregulation, and altering metabolism 10 . It is widely known that CRC is characterized by 62

pathogenic bacteria 12,13 . Recent studies have highlighted that the direct influence of the gut 64 

microbiome on colorectal cancer is mediated predominantly by bacterial metabolites 14,15 . Gut 65 

microbes produce metabolites that can either protect against or promote cancer. Harmful 66 

metabolites such as secondary bile acids, sulfides, and other toxins can damage DNA, trigger 67 

inflammation, and promote tumor development 11 . In contrast, beneficial metabolites like the short-68 

chain fatty acids butyrate, acetate, and propionate promote anti-inflammatory and anti-cancer 69 

effects by supporting intestinal health and activating tumor-suppressing genes 16 . Whether gut 70 

microbial folate metabolites influence CRC development is unknown. 71 

72 

To investigate the host-microbe folate axis, we recently performed a systematic analysis of folate 73 

metabolites in 37 tissues from male and female mice under germ-free and conventional 74 

conditions 17 . We found that a healthy gut microbiota consumes folate, with the germ-free 75 

gastrointestinal tract showing the largest increase in folate availability compared to conventional 76 

among the 37 tissues analyzed. Considering that excess folate is shunted into intracellular 77 

storage as folinic acid 18 , we found that folinic acid accumulated in the lower gastrointestinal tracts 78 

of colonized mice and was absent in germ-free counterparts. These data identify the gut 79 

microbiota as the biosynthesis source of the intestinal folinic acid pool. Given the clinical 80 

importance of folinic acid in modulating CRC chemotherapy, its microbial origin represents a 81 

previously unrecognized endogenous defense mechanism against CRC progression. 82 

83 

In this study, we find that folinic acid production by the gut microbiome is reduced in CRC mouse 84 

models and human patients, particularly in patients with metastatic CRC. We show that microbial 85 

folinic acid enhances gut epithelial integrity and suppresses CRC progression. This protective 86 

efficacy of folinic acid is independent of its ability to mitigate gut inflammation, providing anti-87 

neoplastic benefits even in the presence of chronic colitis. Investigation into the molecular 88

Results 95 

Bacterial folinic acid production is decreased in murine and human colorectal cancer 96 

In the one-carbon metabolism cycle, folates serve as essential cofactors facilitating the transfer 97 

of one-carbon units for key anabolic processes, including DNA synthesis, repair, and methylation 19 

98 

(Figure 1a). Folinic acid (5-formyltetrahydrofolate) is not a one-carbon donor, rather it is a 99 

chemically stable reservoir of reduced folates produced from 5,10-methylenetetrahydrofolate via 100 

a side reaction catalyzed by the serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT) enzyme, which in 101 

bacteria is encoded by the glyA gene 20 (Figure 1a). Utilizing our established folate liquid 102 

chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) metabolomics platform 17 , we verified that intestinal 103 

folinic acid levels are dependent on the presence of a functioning microbiota. Folinic acid was 104 

detected in the gut of conventionally colonized mice but was virtually absent in germ-free mice 105 

(Figure 1b). These data establish the intestinal microbiota as the primary source of luminal folinic 106 

acid under folate-replete conditions. To evaluate the relevance of microbially-produced folinic acid 107 

in the context of colorectal cancer (CRC), we extended our analysis to the C57BL/6J-Il10 -/-

108 

(IL10KO) mouse model of colitis-associated CRC 21 . Strikingly, cecal abundance of folinic acid 109 

was ~10-fold lower in IL10KO mice compared to wild-type (WT), suggesting that microbiota in 110 

IL10KO mice are deficient in folinic acid production (Figure 1c). 111 

112 

To determine whether a loss of microbial folinic acid production is also a hallmark of human CRC, 113 

we assessed glyA gene abundance in CRC patient microbiota (Figure 1d). Metagenomic analysis 114

To investigate the bacterial drivers behind the observed depletion of fecal folinic acid in CRC, we 120 

performed microbiota 16S rRNA sequencing in fecal samples from IL10KO mice and WT controls. 121 

Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) cladogram analysis revealed an inflammatory 122 

and pro-CRC signature in IL10KO animals, characterized by an expansion of Christensenellaceae 123 

family, and a loss of homeostatic clades such as Lactobacillaceae, Bacilli, and several lineages 124 

within Proteobacteria (such as Burkholderiales , Alphaproteobacteria , and Betaproteobacteria )125 

(Figure 1g). Abundant anaerobic classes like Clostridia and Bacteroidia did not display uniform, 126 

class-wide shifts. To determine if these shifts corresponded to a loss of folinic acid biosynthetic 127 

capacity, we performed a BLAST analysis of the bacterial SHMT protein encoded by the glyA 128 

gene across the defined 119-member human commensal (hCom2) community 24 to identify the 129 

taxonomic distribution of glyA. This analysis revealed that glyA is conserved within specific 130 

obligate anaerobes clustering within groups of core homeostatic gut commensals, including 131 

dominant lactic acid bacteria and major carbohydrate-degrading Clostridiales lineages, which are 132 

depleted in CRC 12,13 (Figure 1h). The SHMT-positive Lactobacillaceae and Proteobacteria 133 

lineages that dominate the folinic acid-replete WT gut were also significantly depleted in the 134 

IL10KO mice (Figure 1g,h). Taken together, these data support a model wherein the fecal folinic 135 

acid deficiency observed in CRC is driven not by broad shifts in abundant bacterial taxa, rather 136 

by the targeted depletion of specific glyA -containing bacterial strains. 137

transition in human CRC cells 140

Folinic acid is considered a non-toxic folate vitamer and acts as a mucosal protective agent in 141 

clinical settings 25 . We confirmed that folinic acid does not, by itself, kill cancer cells, by incubating 142 

human colorectal adenocarcinoma epithelial HT29 cells with varying concentrations of folinic acid 143 

for 4 days in vitro (Figure 2a). While no overt toxic effects were observed in a cell viability assay, 144 

HT29 cells incubated with folinic acid exhibited a dramatic morphological restructuring of colonies. 145 

The vehicle-treated controls behaved like HT29 cells in culture; rather than arresting to form a 146 

structured epithelial monolayer upon confluence, these cells stacked into overlapping, 147 

mesenchymal-like disorganized multilayers, characteristic of undifferentiated parental HT29 148 

cells 26 (Figure 2b). However, HT29 cells treated with folinic acid appeared more epithelial and 149 

less mesenchymal in morphology. Volume/area quantification of each colony confirmed that folinic 150 

acid induced a monolayer-like re-structuring of cells compared to controls (Figure 2b). Based on 151 

this observation, we hypothesized that folinic acid prevents or reverses epithelial-to-mesenchymal 152 

transition (EMT) of CRC cells in vitro (Figure 2c). Loss of epithelial cell-to-cell adherens junction 153 

proteins, such as E-cadherin, is a hallmark of EMT in CRC 27 (Figure 2c) Click or tap here to enter 154 

text. . Consistently, E-cadherin levels were significantly increased in HT29 cells treated with folinic 155 

acid compared to the vehicle treated cells as measured via immunocytochemistry (Figure 2d) and 156 

western blot (Figure 2e) analysis. Thus, while folinic acid is not cytotoxic, it increases E-cadherin 157 

protein levels in CRC cells in vitro .158

migratory as determined by the significantly decreased number of colony forming units (CFUs) on 163 

the basolateral side (Figure 2g). Consistent with our previous results, folinic acid-treated cells in 164 

the transwells showed high E-cadherin levels (Figure 2h). Transmission electron microscopy 165

Folinic acid inhibits calpain-2 and reverses CRC epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition 171 

We next sought to identify the mechanism of how folinic acid reduces EMT in CRC cells. Using 172 

target-engagement proteomic analysis on HT29 cell lysates incubated with folinic acid, we 173 

identified calpain-2 (CAPN2) and its catalytic subunit (CAPNS) as potential binding targets (Figure 174 

3a). This interaction was further supported by in silico docking analysis, which showed that folinic 175 

acid binds to the catalytic subunit of CAPN2 (Figure 3b). Notably, the 5-formyl group of the bound 176 

folinic acid is oriented toward the catalytic cysteine in the CAPN2 active site, suggesting that this 177 

folate vitamer acts as a selective inhibitor of calpain protease activity. To biochemically 178 

characterize this ligand-protein interaction, we cloned, overexpressed, and purified human 179 

CAPNS for in vitro binding assays. Recombinant protein was incubated with equimolar 180 

concentrations of five distinct folate vitamers, and protein-bound folates were subsequently 181 

quantified via LC-MS to evaluate competitive binding affinity. Folinic acid (5-formyl-182 

tetrahydrofolate) exhibited the highest binding affinity to the CAPN2 catalytic subunit, followed by 183 

5-methyl-tetrahydrofolate (Figure 3c). This pattern indicates that modifications at the N5 position 184 

of the pterin ring are critical determinants of CAPN2 binding. To test whether folinic acid functions 185 

as a competitive inhibitor, we performed a ligand displacement assay using a known, potent 186 

covalent inhibitor of CAPN2 (CAPN2i, CAS No: 110115-07-6). Purified protein pre-incubated with 187 

folinic acid demonstrated dose-dependent displacement of the vitamer upon the addition of 188 

CAPN2i as measured by LC-MS (Figure 3d). These data confirm that folinic acid and CAPN2i 189 

competitively occupy the same catalytic binding pocket. 190

Previous studies have established that CAPN2 is overexpressed in CRC and inhibiting its activity 192 

suppresses tumor burden in vivo 29,30 . Mechanistically, CAPN2 is known to promote CRC 193 

progression by driving a pro-inflammatory gene signature, a process mediated by activation of 194 

the pro-inflammatory transcription factor NF-κB 29 . Given our discovery that folinic acid inhibits 195 

CAPN2 and suppresses EMT in vitro , we next sought to investigate whether CAPN2 natively 196 

promotes EMT in CRC cells and directly modulates expression of the epithelial marker E-197 

cadherin. To evaluate this, we utilized CRISPR-Cas9 genome editing to generate a CAPN2 198 

knockout (KO) HT29 cell line (Figure 3e) and performed transwell migration assays alongside E-199 

cadherin quantification. While the WT HT29 cells exhibited robust migratory capacity, CAPN2KO 200 

cells displayed a striking ~10-fold reduction in migration, suggesting that depletion of CAPN2 is 201 

sufficient to attenuate the EMT phenotype of CRC cells in vitro (Figure 3f). Consistently, 202 

CAPN2KO cells demonstrated markedly elevated E-cadherin expression compared to WT 203 

controls (Figure 3g). 204 

205 

Given that CAPN2 is frequently overexpressed in aggressive and chemotherapy-resistant CRC 206 

tumors 31,32 , we next investigated whether elevated CAPN2 expression is independently capable 207 

of driving EMT. To test this, we generated a CAPN2-overexpressing (OE) cell line by progressively 208 

treating HT29 cells with increasing doses of SN-38, the active metabolite of the chemotherapy 209 

drug irinotecan 29 (Figure 3h) Click or tap here to enter text. . These SN-38-resistant cells 210 

exhibited a marked upregulation of CAPN2, which coincided with a significant increase in cell 211 

migration and a concomitant decrease in E-cadherin expression (Figure 3h,i). These data indicate 212 

that elevated CAPN2 protein levels are a hallmark of chemoresistance-associated EMT in CRC. 213

Gut microbial synthesis of folinic acid improves colon epithelial and mucosal homeostasis 224 

in vivo 225 

To determine if microbial folinic acid promotes E-cadherin expression and intestinal homeostasis 226 

in vivo , we utilized the probiotic commensal Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 ( EcN ), which can 227 

naturally produce all folate derivatives 33 . Using Lambda Red recombination 34 , we deleted the glyA 228 

gene to generate a genetically engineered EcN strain ( EcN glyA KO) with a dampened capacity 229 

of producing folinic acid (Extended Data Figure 1). C57BL/6J mice were gavaged biweekly with 230 

10 9 CFUs of either wild-type (WT) EcN or EcN glyA KO for 12 weeks (Figure 4a). While both 231 

strains colonized at equivalent levels (Figure 4b), LC-MS analysis confirmed a significant 232 

reduction in fecal folinic acid production in the glyA KO group (Figure 4c). 233 

234 

After 14-weeks, animals were sacrificed and colon tissue was harvested for analysis. Strikingly, 235 

the EcN glyA KO colonized mouse colons appeared to exhibit signs of inflammation, characterized 236 

by visible thickening and increased irregularities along the length of the colon compared to the 237 

EcN WT group 35 (Figure 4d). This morphological change occurred with a slight but not significant 238 

reduction in colon length in EcN glyA KO gavaged mice compared to the EcN WT mice, which is 239 

indicative of intestinal inflammation and tissue damage 36 (Figure 4e). Consistent with these 240 

macroscopic changes, fecal levels of lipocalin-2 (LCN-2), a biomarker of intestinal inflammation 37 ,241 

showed a progressive and significant increase by week 11 in the glyA KO group (Figure 4f). 242

Discussion 327 

This study identifies a previously uncharacterized role for microbial folinic acid as an endogenous 328 

metabolite that actively suppresses colorectal cancer (CRC) progression. We show that folinic-329 

acid producing bacteria are depleted in mouse models of colitis-associated CRC and in human 330 

clinical metagenomic cohorts with advancing disease severity. Our in vitro assays using HT29 331 

cells show that treatment with folinic acid decreases metastasis, which is mediated through 332 

calpain-2 inhibition. Administration of a single microbe, wild-type EcN , which can produce folinic 333 

acid, improved epithelial and mucosal homeostasis and tumorigenesis in the colon compared to 334 

the EcN glyA KO strain. While EcN has been previously documented to improve CRC outcomes, 335 

our findings demonstrate that its protective efficacy is dramatically attenuated when its ability to 336 

produce folinic acid is genetically abolished via glyA deletion. 337 

338 

The clinical utility of folinic acid is well-established; owing to its chemical stability and 339 

pharmacokinetics, it is the folate of choice for adjuvant chemotherapy, where it stabilizes the 5-340 

fluorouracil (5-FU) bond with thymidylate synthase 41,42 . However, this heightened potency is often 341 

accompanied by severe gastrointestinal side effects, including inflammation, damage, drug 342 

resistance, and gut dysbiosis 43 . Because these outcomes typically occur in patients receiving 343 

highly cytotoxic co-therapies (e.g., fluoropyrimidine, methotrexate), it has historically been difficult 344 

to discern whether folinic acid is causative or correlative towards these outcomes. There have 345 

been no reports on the effects of folate production from the microbiome in CRC therapeutics and 346

Folate cannot be synthesized naturally by humans and must be acquired through diet, 359

progression by limiting microbial folinic acid reservoirs 49 . Moving forward, characterizing the 362 

precise bacterial consortia, such as Lactiplantibacillus , Lactococcus , Bifidobacterium , and 363 

Streptococcus capable of high-level folinic acid production, will be essential for developing next-364 

generation probiotic therapeutics. Future research should also investigate whether patient-365

improved chemotherapy response and long-term CRC remission. This study potentially offers a 367 

new approach into colorectal cancer therapy by altering the microbiome to produce beneficial 368 

metabolites, such as folinic acid, to maintain epithelial and mucosal integrity within the host. 369

Mass spectrometry data analysis 658 

Raw files were first converted to mzXML, and monoisotopic peaks were re-assigned using 659 

Monocle. Database searching included all human entries from Uniprot (downloaded in February 660 

2020). The database was concatenated with one composed of all protein sequences in the 661 

reversed order. Sequences of common contaminant proteins (e.g., trypsin, keratins, etc.) were 662 

appended as well. Searches were performed using the comet search algorithm. Searches were 663 

performed using a 50-ppm precursor ion tolerance and 0.02 Da product ion tolerance. TMTpro on 664 

lysine residues and peptide N termini (+304.2071 Da) and carbamidomethylation of cysteine 665 

residues (+57.0215 Da) were set as static modifications, while oxidation of methionine residues 666 

(+15.9949 Da) was set as a variable modification. 667 

668 

Peptide-spectrum matches (PSMs) were adjusted to a 1% false discovery rate (FDR). PSM 669 

filtering was performed using linear discriminant analysis (LDA) as described previously 55 while 670 

considering the following parameters: comet log expect, different sequence delta comet log 671 

expect (percent difference between the first hit and the next hit with a different peptide sequence), 672 

missed cleavages, peptide length, charge state, precursor mass accuracy, and fraction of ions 673 

matched. Each run was filtered separately. Protein-level FDR was subsequently estimated at a 674 

data set level. For each protein across all samples, the posterior probabilities reported by the LDA 675 

model for each peptide were multiplied to give a protein-level probability estimate. Using the 676 

Picked FDR method 56 , proteins were filtered to the target 1% FDR level. 677 

For reporter ion quantification, a 0.003 Da window around the theoretical m/z of each reporter ion 678 

was scanned, and the most intense m/z was used. Reporter ion intensities were adjusted to 679 

correct for the isotopic impurities of the different TMTpro reagents according to manufacturer 680
