Sandhu et al. Confidential 2

Abstract 37 

Recognition of infected macrophages by CD4+ T cells is essential to immune protection 38 

against Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) , the causative agent of tuberculosis (TB). However, not 39 

all infected macrophage subsets successfully elicit T cell activation. We recently discovered that 40 

M2-like macrophages fail to efficiently activate memory CD4+ T cells when infected with Mtb, yet 41 

successfully elicit T cell activation when loaded with peptides, g-irradiated bacteria, or Mtb whole 42 

cell lysate. Since the mechanisms underlying CD4+ T cell evasion by infected M2 but not M1-like 43 

macrophages remain underexplored, we sought to determine the genes and pathways unique to 44 

Mtb infection of M2-like cells, including alveolar macrophages. RNA sequencing of human 45 

macrophages infected with virulent Mtb identified enrichment of IL-10 and type I interferon (IFN) 46 

signaling genes, including IL10RA and HERC5 , respectively, in infected M2-like monocyte-47 

derived and alveolar macrophages. However, genes involved in MHC-II trafficking, such as 48 

AP1M2 , were higher in infected M1-like macrophages. In complementary experiments using 49 

fluorescence microscopy and flow cytometry, we observed impaired trafficking of newly 50 

synthesized MHC-II to the plasma membrane of Mtb-infected M2-like macrophages despite high 51 

total surface MHC-II levels. Neutralization of IL-10 or knockdown of HERC5 restored MHC-II 52 

trafficking to the cell surface among infected M2-like macrophages and significantly enhanced 53 

activation of memory CD4+ T cells in an MHC-II-dependent manner. These findings identify 54 

coordinated IL-10 and type I IFN signaling as key mechanisms that restrict MHC-II trafficking to 55 

the plasma membrane in Mtb-infected M2-like macrophages, thereby limiting antigen 56 

presentation and CD4+ T cell activation. We propose that host-directed therapies targeting these 57 

pathways in infected alveolar macrophages will facilitate T cell recognition for the prevention or 58 

treatment of active TB. 59

Introduction 82 

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a leading global health threat, with over 10.8 million incident 83 

cases and over 1.2 million deaths in 2024 [1]. CD4+ T cells are critical for protection against TB 84 

[2]. Protection is conferred by T cell antigen receptor (TCR) recognition of peptide-bound major 85 

histocompatibility complex (pMHC) on the surface of infected macrophages [3–5]. Successful T 86 

cell recognition depends on the processing of Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) proteins and 87 

presentation of peptides in the context of MHC molecules, dynamics that vary across macrophage 88 

subsets [6,7]. Pro-inflammatory (M1-like) macrophages more efficiently restrict bacterial growth, 89 

while alternatively activated/anti-inflammatory (M2-like) macrophages are more frequently 90 

infected and are permissive to Mtb growth [6,8]. We recently showed that M2-like macrophages 91 

fail to efficiently activate human CD4+ T cells upon Mtb infection, while M1- and M2-like 92 

macrophage subsets both activated T cells when treated with exogenous antigens in the form of 93 

peptides, g-irradiated bacteria, or Mtb whole cell lysate [9]. Together, these findings suggest that 94 

Mtb thrives within M2-like macrophages, evading T cell responses. However, the mechanisms by 95 

which Mtb leverages the environment of these antigen-presenting cells (APCs) to prevent T cell 96 

recognition remain unknown. 97 

Several lines of evidence indicate that T cells inefficiently recognize Mtb-infected lung-98 

resident alveolar macrophages (AMs). In the mouse model of TB, AMs are the only cells found to 99 

harbor bacilli for nearly two weeks after aerosol Mtb infection, at which point monocyte-derived 100 

macrophages (MDMs), dendritic cells (DCs), and neutrophils are recruited and become infected 101 

[10–13]. T cell responses in the lungs are similarly delayed by up to two weeks after aerosol 102 

infection [14,15]. Memory T cell responses are also delayed [4,16,17]. In humans, conversion of 103 

the tuberculin skin test or interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA), indicating Mtb-specific T cell 104 

responses, is delayed by up to 8-12 weeks after exposure to a household contact with TB [18,19]. 105 

Differences between murine and human macrophage responses to Mtb infection may account for 106

In this study, we aimed to identify the key pathways responsible for subversion of the CD4+ 136

Mtb-specific CD4+ T cells. This study highlights the roles of type I IFN and IL-10 signaling in 145 

impaired antigen presentation to CD4+ T cells through reduced MHC-II trafficking. 146

While M2-like MDMs do not efficiently activate memory CD4+ T cells upon Mtb infection, 210 

we previously found that they successfully elicit CD4+ T cell responses when treated with Mtb 211 

lysate or g-irradiated H37Rv (Irrad. Mtb) [9]. Therefore, we next sought to investigate the genes 212 

and pathways unique to Mtb infection of M2-like MDMs that could account for the reduced CD4+ 213 

T cell activation in response to infection. We performed bulk RNA sequencing on both M1 and 214 

M2-like MDMs 24 h after infection with Mtb (Mtb-infected), or after treatment with either g-215 

irradiated H37Rv (Irrad. Mtb) or media only (non-infected). Consistent with previous reports, we 216

Mtb infection of M2-like macrophages impairs MHC-II trafficking 254 

Since both HERC5 and IL-10R signaling have been shown to impact MHC-II trafficking, 255 

we next focused on MHC-II localization in Mtb-infected macrophages [35,54]. ISGylation by 256 

HERC5 was previously shown to negatively regulate endosomal sorting complexes required for 257 

transport (ESCRT) components such as ALIX and CHMP [55], which play key roles in 258 

multivesicular body (MVB) biogenesis [54,56], and MHC-II trafficking [56]. AP1M2 and CHMP4B ,259 

members of the AP-1 complex involved in MHC-II trafficking and intra-luminal vesicle formation 260 

[57,58], were found to be upregulated by infected M1 but not M2-like MDMs (Extended Data 2) .261 

Importantly, the ESCRT pathway has been shown to facilitate antigen presentation to CD4+ T 262 

cells by Mtb-infected macrophages [59]. Paradoxically, M2-like macrophages exhibit high surface 263 

MHC-II expression compared to M1-like macrophages despite not efficiently activating CD4+ T 264 

cells upon infection with Mtb [9]. Therefore, we reasoned that MHC-II trafficking dynamics may 265 

be more informative than static surface staining. 266 

To test the hypothesis that MHC-II trafficking to the plasma membrane differs between 267 

Mtb-infected M1 and M2-like macrophages, we performed an MHC-II blockade and re-staining 268 

assay with blocking and fluorescence-conjugated antibodies to compare Mtb-infected M1 and M2-269 

like MDMs (Fig. 4A) . We blocked HLA-DR using saturating concentrations of unconjugated anti-270 

HLA-DR blocking mAb (clone L243) prior to infection with GFP-expressing H37Rv (Mtb-GFP). At 271 

24 h post infection, macrophages were then re-stained with a PE-conjugated version of the same 272 

anti-HLA-DR mAb clone (PE-L243), and also with a BV421-conjugated alternative anti-HLA-DR 273 

mAb clone, LN3 (BV421-LN3), which binds a distinct HLA-DR epitope (Fig. 4A) . Fluorescence 274 

microscopy revealed differences in anti-HLA-DR re-staining between M1 and M2-like MDMs. 275 

Whereas both subsets stained positively for the alternative anti-HLA-DR mAb clone (BV421-LN3), 276 

significantly fewer M2-like infected macrophages showed surface re-staining using the same 277 

clone (L243) used for HLA-DR blockade 24 h earlier (Figs. 4B, 4C, and Supplemental Fig. 2A) .278

To determine whether the impaired surface receptor trafficking in infected M2-like 281

Macrophages were blocked with a saturating concentration of anti-HLA-A/B/C mAb (clone 283

data indicate that MHC-II but not MHC-I trafficking is impaired in Mtb-infected M2-like 290 

macrophages. 291 

We next used cycloheximide (CHX), a protein synthesis inhibitor, to determine the extent 292 

to which de novo MHC-II synthesis accounts for increased MHC-II trafficking to the cell surface in 293 

infected M1-like macrophages, as MHC-II surface levels depend both on new synthesis and 294 

recycling of existing molecules. After washout of Mtb-GFP, CHX was maintained in culture with 295 

infected M1 or M2-like MDMs. At 24 h post-infection, CHX treatment significantly reduced HLA-296 

DR (L243) MFI in M1-like MDMs to levels comparable to those in M2-like MDMs (Fig. 4D) . By 297 

contrast, M2-like macrophages showed no significant change in PE-L243 or BV421-LN3 MFI with 298 

CHX treatment (Figs. 4D, 4E) . Interestingly, M1-like macrophages also showed slightly increased 299 

HLA-DR (BV421-LN3) MFI (Fig. 4E) , which may reflect differences in the binding kinetics of the 300 

L243 and LN3 clones. Taken together, these data indicate that MHC-II trafficking to the plasma 301 

membrane is impaired in infected M2-like macrophages and that newly trafficked HLA-DR in 302 

infected M1-like macrophages arrives at the cell surface primarily through new synthesis. 303

Discussion 369 

Macrophages, the cellular niche for Mtb infection, can restrict bacterial growth or provide 370 

an environment permissive to Mtb persistence. In this study, we expand on our previous work 371 

demonstrating the differential capacity of macrophage subsets to activate CD4+ T cells upon Mtb 372 

infection. We find that compared to M1-like MDMs, M2-like macrophages exhibit greater type I 373 

IFN and IL-10 signaling in response to Mtb infection. Similar to M2-like MDMs, AMs expressed 374 

key type I IFN genes including HERC5 and RSAD2, high levels of IL10RA, and failed to efficiently 375 

activate memory CD4+ T cells. We further show that MHC-II trafficking to the plasma membrane 376 

is uniquely impaired in infected M2-like MDMs, and is restored with IL-10 neutralization or HERC5 377 

knockdown, which leads to augmented memory CD4+ T cell activation. Together, these data link 378 

increased IL-10 signaling and HERC5 expression in Mtb-infected M2-like macrophages to 379 

impaired antigen presentation and dampened CD4+ T cell activation. 380 

Constitutive IL-10 signaling in the lung is likely critical for maintaining gas exchange, but 381 

could be exploited by Mtb during infection of macrophages expressing IL-10R [9,35,37,60–62]. 382 

To examine the link between IL-10 neutralization and enhanced T cell activation, we focused on 383 

antigen presentation. We found MHC-II trafficking to the plasma membrane to be impaired among 384 

Mtb-infected M2-like macrophages in the presence of IL-10 signaling. Compared to M1-like 385 

macrophages, M2-like MDMs secreted high levels of IL-10 at baseline and after Mtb infection. 386 

Non-infected M2-like MDMs showed expression of IL10RA compared to M1-like MDMs. Infected 387 

M2-like MDMs showed the greatest expression, followed by infected AMs from BAL samples. 388 

These results indicate that AMs and M2-like MDMs demonstrate increased sensitivity to IL-10 389 

signaling relative to M1-like MDMs, and that IL-10 signaling can facilitate a state of restricted 390 

MHC-II exchange between the cell surface and the phagosome or other intracellular 391 

compartments during infection. The preferential expression of type I IFN genes in infected M2-392 

like macrophages, previously associated with phagosomal membrane damage and permeability 393

Limitations of the study 471 

To our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate a direct link between type I IFN 472 

signaling and impaired MHC-II trafficking during Mtb infection of human macrophages. 473 

Nevertheless, several limitations should be noted. First, although Mtb-infected M2-like MDMs 474 

represent many aspects of infected macrophages in the lungs, our model does not fully 475 

recapitulate the spectrum of diverse macrophage phenotypes found in vivo. Our ex vivo 476 

macrophage infection model represents extreme but useful M1 and M2 polarized phenotypes. 477 

Aside from limited macrophage phenotypes, we were similarly limited in our ability to examine the 478 

roles of type I IFN pathway genes. We focused on HERC5 based on its strong and consistent 479 

expression in infected M2-like macrophages, but other type I IFN signaling genes likely also 480 

contribute to reduced antigen presentation to CD4+ T cells. An obvious constraint of our study is 481 

the small sample size. We were limited by the scarce availability of human BAL samples for 482 

research, particularly from individuals with LTBI, which hampers the applicability of our findings 483 

across populations. Donor-to-donor variability likely contributed to variation in the magnitude of 484 

responses to IL-10 neutralization and HERC5 knockdown in both MHC-II trafficking and T cell 485 

activation experiments. While we observed consistent CD4+ T cell activation with either 486 

intervention, it is possible that with a larger sample size, greater MHC-II trafficking and CD4+ T 487 

cell activation responses could have been observed with HERC5 knockdown, or in combination 488 

with IL-10 neutralization. Finally, since our experiments focused exclusively on infected 489 

macrophage and CD4+ T cell interactions, they do not capture the full complexity of cellular 490 

interactions likely occurring in vivo . How interactions with other cell types influence infected 491 

macrophages and T cells remains an open question. Despite these limitations, this work provides 492
