Abstract 34 

Mycobacterium tuberculosis, the causative agent of tuberculosis (TB), has 35 

evolved strategies to evade innate immunity and establish persistent infection. 36 

However, the mechanisms by which M. tuberculosis reprograms human 37 

macrophage metabolism remain incompletely defined. Tuberculous pleural 38 

effusion (TB -PE), a common extrapulmonary manifestation that frequently 39 

coexists with pulmonary TB, offers a unique, clinically relevant immunometabolic 40 

window into the TB microenvironment. Here, using patient -derived TB -PE 41 

samples, we demonstrate that this microenvironment induces a metabolic state 42 

in human macrophages that compromises their antimicrobial function. Lipidomic 43 

analysis identified an enrichment of the specialized pro -resolving mediator 44 

Resolvin D5 (RvD5), which signals through GPR32 to suppress macrophage 45 

microbicidal activity. The acellular fraction of TB -PE was sufficient to induce RvD5 46 

secretion by monocytes, correlating with increased expression of RvD5 47 

biosynthetic enzymes in pleural monocytes from TB patients. Mechanistically, 48 

RvD5 -GPR32 signaling inhibited glycolysis without promoting oxidative 49 

phosphorylation, reducing HIF -1α activity and impairing intracellular M. 50 

tuberculosis control. HIF -1α stabilization restored antimicrobial function. These 51 

findings uncover the RvD5 -GPR32 -HIF -1α axis as a mechanism of metabolic 52 

immune suppression and a potential target for host -directed TB therapy. 53

Introduction 55 

The success of  M. tuberculosis  (Mtb) as a pathogen lies, in part, in its efficient 56 

adaptation to the intracellular environment of human macrophages, which 57 

involves regulating the host’s metabolic pathways to facilitate immune evasion. 58 

Macrophages are essential immune cells that can serve as a niche for 59 

intracellular mycobacterial persistence but can also limit infection by activating 60 

their microbicidal properties. In fact, innate immunity appears sufficient to clear 61 

Mtb infections in certain patients, conferring resistance 1. This activation of 62 

macrophages towards the microbicidal program (called the M1 activation 63 

program) depends on a profound metabolic change that promotes glycolysis to 64 

the detriment of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation 2,3 .65 

Tuberculous pleural effusion (TB -PE) is characterized by an accumulation of fluid 66 

in the pleural cavity of infected patients, marked by elevated levels of proteins, 67 

lipids, and specific leukocyte populations. Recent studies have begun to 68 

characterize the metabolites present in these samples, revealing differential 69 

abundance of amino acids, citric acid cycle intermediates, and free fatty acids 70 

compared with malignant pleural effusions 4. TB -PE is typically a serous fluid and 71 

contains very few viable mycobacteria, indicating a paucibacillary infection. The 72 

development of TB -PE is likely due to a low -burden mycobacterial infection in the 73 

pleural space, which typically arises from initial parenchymal lesions. This 74 

infection triggers an immune response that not only increases the production of 75 

pleural fluid but also impairs its drainage 5.76 

TB -PE is the second most prevalent form of extrapulmonary TB, after TB 77 

lymphadenitis, accounting for 15 -25% of TB cases worldwide. In 78 

immunocompromised populations, it may represent as much as 50% of all 79 

cases 6. Compared to samples obtained from blood or bronchoalveolar lavage 80 

(BAL) of TB patients, TB -PE provides a more accurate reflection of the 81 

microenvironment encountered during infection. It captures the specific cellular 82 

composition and environmental factors at the site of infection, which is 83 

anatomically related to the lungs. In fact, there is a significant association 84 

between pleural TB and pulmonary TB as coexisting parenchymal disease has 85 

been identified in 48% 7 and 86% 8 of computed tomography scans conducted on 86 

patients with pleural TB. Furthermore, TB -PE samples better reflect the 87 

physiological concentrations of metabolites and compounds because they are not 88

While it has been suggested that type -1 immunity is dominantly present in TB -91 

PE 9, infection control within the pleural cavity is considered insufficient, as 92 

patients frequently experience recurrent episodes of TB if left untreated 10 . This 93 

raises the possibility of active mechanisms that impede the development of a 94 

long -term protective immune response. In this regard, we demonstrated that the 95 

acellular fraction of TB -PE induces the differentiation of monocytes into 96 

immunosuppressive macrophages 11 –13 , converting them into lipid -laden or foamy 97 

macrophages, thereby affecting their effector functions against bacilli 14 . We have 98 

also demonstrated that microbicidal M1 macrophages exposed to a lipid fraction 99 

isolated from TB -PE, enriched in metabolites derived from polyunsaturated fatty 100 

acids (PUFA), are unable to carry out the metabolic reprogramming associated 101 

with their pro -inflammatory profile, showing reduced glycolytic activity, as well as 102 

increased mitochondrial respiration and reduced microbicidal activity 15 ; however, 103 

the mechanism of action as well as the specific metabolites involved in this effect 104 

remain unclear, limiting the potential to develop targeted therapies aimed at 105 

enhancing anti -TB immune response. 106 

In response to infection, both inflammatory and pro -resolving processes are 107 

deployed in TB -PE. Among the mechanisms aimed at limiting the inflammatory 108 

process and activating tissue repair and healing are those catalyzed by a family 109 

of lipids, recently termed specialized pro -resolving mediators (SPMs) 16 . SPMs 110 

derive from PUFA such as linoleic acid (C18: Δ2, n -6), arachidonic acid (C20: Δ4, 111 

n-6), eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, 20:5, n -3), and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, 112 

22:6, n-3), which are produced through enzymatic reactions catalyzed by 113 

lipoxygenases (LOX) and/or cyclooxygenases (COX). SPMs are composed of 114 

DHA -derived D -series protectins and resolvins, EPA -derived E -series resolvins, 115 

and arachidonic acid -derived lipoxins. 116 

By binding to specific receptors, resolvins modulate various inflammatory 117 

processes, including neutrophil migration, macrophage phagocytosis, and the 118 

expression of pro -inflammatory mediators, thereby mitigating inflammatory 119 

pathologies 17 . The G protein -coupled receptor 32 (GPR32) has been shown to 120 

bind to resolvins RvD1, RvD3, and RvD5 18 –20 . Although direct evidence 121 

supporting the RvD5 -GPR32 axis in lung infections remains limited, 122

mediates this impairment between RvD5 and its receptor GPR32, expressed in 134

Results 138 

Enrichment in specialized resolution lipid mediators correlates with 139 

impaired macrophage glycolysis in tuberculous pleural effusions. 140 

We previously found that PUFA metabolites in TB -PE inhibit the glycolytic activity 141 

of M1 macrophages 15 . To explore whether this inhibition was specific to pleural 142 

TB lipids, we compared the ability of the PUFA extracted from the acellular 143 

fraction of TB -PE and non -TB patients to inhibit glycolysis in M1 macrophages 144 

(activated by IFNγ and LPS), by assessing lactate release from these cells. The 145 

lipid fractions isolated from TB -PE showed greater inhibition of lactate release by 146 

M1 macrophages than PE from non -tuberculous patients with other pathologies, 147 

such as heart failure, pneumonia, and cancer ( Figure 1A ). To further investigate 148 

the candidate components responsible for this inhibition, a targeted lipidomic 149 

analysis was conducted on lipids isolated from TB and non -TB patients (n=8 and 150 

3, respectively). Among the 29 screened PUFA metabolites, four exhibited higher 151 

levels in TB -PE compared to non -TB PE ( Figure 1B -C). Notably, these molecules 152 

were specialized pro -resolving lipid mediators (SPMs) derived from omega -3 fatty 153 

acids, i.e., 14 ‑HDoHE, RvD5, Protectin Dx (PDx), and 18 -HEPE ( Figure 1B -C). 154 

Importantly,  the abundance of these SPMs was significantly correlated with the 155 

decline in glycolysis induced by each PE in monocyte -derived M1 macrophages 156 

from healthy donors ( Figure 1D ).  Taken together,  our data indicate that SPMs 157 

are highly abundant in TB -PE, and their levels correlate with TB -PE's capacity to 158 

suppress M1 macrophage glycolytic activity. Moreover, RvD5 was significantly 159 

enriched in TB -PE compared to non -TB PE, with a log ₂ fold change of 15.07, a 160 

−log(p -value) of 3.09, and a multiple testing -corrected q -value of 0.02. By 161 

contrast, 18 -HEPE and 14 -HDoHE showed only marginal statistical significance 162 

(q = 0.05)  (Figure 1C and E ). 163

Local Synthesis of RvD5 by Monocytes and Expression of its Receptor 175 

GPR32 on Macrophages in Tuberculous Pleuritis. 176 

Our next objective was to investigate the potential origins of S PM s in the TB -PE. 177 

To address this, we analyzed the expression of key enzymes involved in SPM 178 

biosynthesis, namely arachidonic acid 5 -lipoxygenase (5 -LO, ALOX5), along with 179 

arachidonic acid 12 - and 15 -lipoxygenating paralogues (ALOX12, ALOX15, and 180 

ALOX15B) 22 , in peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) and pleural fluid 181 

mononuclear cells (PFMC) from TB patients. This analysis utilized publicly 182 

available single -cell RNA -Seq datasets (accession numbers HRA000910 and 183 

HRA00036). According to the literature 23 , lymphocytes (mainly T cells) dominate 184 

leukocyte infiltrates, while monocytic populations are underrepresented in TB -PE 185 

compared to blood ( Figure 2A) . While monocytes from both compartments 186 

showed minimal expression of ALOX12, ALOX15, and ALOX15B, that of ALOX5 187 

was significantly upregulated in pleural CD14 ⁺ cells compared to circulating 188 

monocytes (Figure 2B, S2A ). In CD14 ⁺ monocytes specifically, ALOX5 189 

expression increased ~4.4 -fold in PFMC versus PBMC (predicted means: ~0.33 190 

to ~1.48), with no significant changes observed in other cell types ( Figure 2C, 191 

S2B ). This selective upregulation suggests that, within the context of pleural TB, 192 

pleural CD14 ⁺ cells are the primary mononuclear cell population responsible for 193 

local specialized pro -resolving mediators. 194 

195 

We also examined RvD5 levels in monocytes from blood of healthy donors 196 

exposed to the acellular fraction of TB -PE, compared with unexposed monocytes, 197 

to replicate the pleural environment encountered by newly recruited monocytes. 198 

After exposure, the monocytes were extensively washed, and intracellular RvD5 199 

levels were measured. RvD5 was significantly detected in extracts from 200 

monocytes treated with the acellular fraction of TB -PE ( Figure 2D ). Together, 201 

these findings indicate that monocytes produce RvD5 in response to 202 

environmental factors in the pleural cavity of patients with pleural TB. 203

Building on these results, we next explore the potential involvement of the RvD5 -205 

GPR32 axis in pleural TB pathogenesis. Previous studies have shown that 206 

GPR32 is highly expressed on macrophages and its activation induces 207 

macrophage polarization from a pro -inflammatory to a pro -resolutive 208 

phenotype 24 . We first confirmed the presence of the RvD5 receptor, GPR32, on 209 

cells within the pleural cavity. Expression was significantly higher in pleural 210 

CD14 + cells than in their circulating counterparts, a pattern consistent in both TB 211 

and non -TB patients. Furthermore, GPR32 expression in pleural CD14 + cells was 212 

clearly increased in TB patients compared with non -TB patients  (Figure 2E -F) .213 

This demonstrates the receptor’s expression in cells locally exposed to RvD5. 214 

215 

The RvD5 -GPR32 axis mediates the inhibition of macrophage glycolysis by 216 

tuberculous pleural effusions 217 

To determine whether RvD5 was responsible for the inhibition of macrophage 218 

glycolysis triggered by TB -PE, we conducted functional assays using 219 

commercially available RvD5. As previously shown 25 , compared to non -activated 220 

macrophages (M0), M1 macrophages typically exhibited high glycolytic activity 221 

by increasing lactate release ( Figure 3A ). To determine the contribution of RvD5 222 

to the observed effect, we treated M1 macrophages with 17 and 33 nM RvD5, 223 

concentrations chosen to mirror those found in 20% TB -PE. Under these 224 

conditions, which reflect the physiological stoichiometry of the effusion, RvD5 225 

alone significantly reduced lactate production, matching the inhibition achieved 226 

by TB -PE 20% v/v  (Figure 3A and S3A).  Notably, RvD5 treatment did not affect 227 

cell viability ( Figure S3B ). Given the pivotal role of HIF1α in the TB -PE -induced 228 

impairment of M1 macrophage functions 15 , we explored whether RvD5 influenced 229 

the upregulation of HIF1α in M1 cells. Our findings revealed that RvD5 reduced 230 

HIF1α expression in M1 macrophages ( Figure 3B ). Similarly, RvD5 suppressed 231 

lactate release when M1 macrophages (activated by IFNγ) were challenged with 232 

irradiated  M. tuberculosis  (Figure 3C ). 233 

234

investigated whether the inhibitory effects of exogenous RvD5 are mediated 236 

through this receptor. First, in line with the literature 26 , we confirmed GPR32 237 

expression in M1 macrophages ( Figure 3D ). Next, we observed that blocking 238

Activation of the RvD5 -GPR32 axis reduces glycolysis without affecting 249 

mitochondrial bioenergetics in M1 macrophages 250 

To investigate if the TB -associated microenvironment affects the metabolic profile 251 

of pleural monocyte/macrophages, we compared the  ex vivo  metabolic profile of 252 

CD14 + cells from TB -PE with that of paired circulating monocytes. This was 253 

achieved by monitoring changes in protein translation upon treatment with 254 

oligomycin and/or 2 -deoxy -D-glucose using the SCENITH assay 27 . Our findings 255 

indicated a reduced glycolytic capacity in cells residing in the TB pleural cavity 256 

compared to blood cells ( Figure 4A ). Interestingly, pleural macrophages from TB 257 

patients undergoing antibiotic treatment exhibited a significant increase in 258 

glycolysis compared to those from untreated patients ( Figure 4A ). Our previous 259 

work established that circulating monocytes from TB patients have higher 260 

glycolytic metabolism than those from healthy individuals 28 . Here, we extended 261 

these observations to circulating monocytes from pleural TB patients, in whom 262 

glycolytic activity showed a decreasing trend upon antibiotic treatment  (Figure 263 

4A ). 264 

265

metabolic pathways to the cellular energy metabolism of M1 macrophages using 267 

SCENITH. According to the expected glycolytic profile of M1 activation 268 

program 29 , M1 macrophages exhibited a lower reliance on OXPHOS alongside 269 

an increase in the glycolytic capacity compared to unstimulated cells (M0) 270 

(Figure 4B ). However, this metabolic shift in M1 macrophages was impaired 271 

upon addition of exogenous RvD5  (Figure 4B and Figure S3 and S4) . Of note, 272

To examine the effect of RvD5 on the OXPHOS metabolic state in macrophages, 279 

we performed the Seahorse XF Cell Mito Stress Test in M0, M1, and RvD5 -280

Analysis of respiratory parameters indicates substantial reductions in ATP -linked 291 

OCR and maximal respiratory capacity in M1 macrophages compared to M0 292 

cells. While RvD5 treatment did not reverse these M1 -induced deficits, it caused 293 

a further, specific reduction in maximal respiratory capacity ( Figure 4D ). 294

To further evaluate intracellular  ATP production, we measured the basal ECAR 296 

and OCR to calculate ATP production rates from glycolysis (GlycoATP) and 297 

mitochondrial OXPHOS (MitoATP). M1 macrophages exhibited higher ATP 298 

production rates from glycolysis compared to RvD5 -treated M1 macrophages 299 

(Figure S6A, C -D), with no differences in ATP production from mitochondrial 300 

respiration ( Figure S6A -B and D ). Consistent with SCENITH findings, the 301 

relative contribution of MitoATP to total ATP production increased, while the 302 

contribution of GlycoATP decreased in RvD5 -treated M1 macrophages 303 

compared to untreated M1 cells ( Figure 4E ).  Furthermore, in line with these 304 

results, M1 macrophages showed lower mitochondrial mass than control cells, 305 

but this parameter remained unchanged upon RvD5 treatment  (Figure S7A -B) .306

Finally, we assessed whether specific inhibition of GPR32 could restore M1 313 

metabolism in RvD5 -treated macrophages. Our results revealed that blocking 314 

GPR32 led to a notable shift in RvD5 -M1 cells, driving them towards heightened 315 

glycolysis, as evidenced by an increase in basal ECAR ( Figure 4F and Figure 316 

S8A and C ). Notably, key respiratory parameters, including basal OCR and 317 

maximal respiratory capacity, were significantly diminished in RvD5 -M1 318 

macrophages following GPR32 inhibition (Figure 4G ), suggesting a319 

compensatory reduction in OXPHOS to accommodate the increased glycolysis. 320 

These findings were further corroborated by ATP production analysis, which 321 

yielded a significant increase in GlycoATP levels and a corresponding decrease 322 

in MitoATP levels upon GPR32 inhibition ( Figure S8B -D). Additionally, the 323 

relative contribution of GlycoATP to total ATP production increased, while that of 324 

MitoATP decreased, compared with cells treated with the isotype control ( Figure 325 

4H ). This metabolic reprogramming highlights a pronounced shift towards 326 

glycolytic reliance at the expense of the OXPHOS activity. 327 

328 

Collectively, these results underscore the critical role of the RvD5 -GPR32 axis in 329 

suppressing glycolytic activity in M1 macrophages, maintaining a hypometabolic 330 

state without increasing OXPHOS flux. 331

Then, we examined the capacity of M1 macrophages to engulf viable Mtb. There 346 

was no significant change in the initial bacterial uptake between RvD5 -treated M1 347 

macrophages and untreated cells ( Figure 5A ). However, RvD5 treatment lowers 348 

IL -1β secretion by M1 macrophages in response to Mtb infection and impaired 349 

strongly their microbicidal activity ( Figure 5B -C) . Additionally, while Mtb ‑infected 350 

macrophages displayed a higher LC3 ‑II/LC3 ‑I ratio compared with uninfected 351 

cells, this induction was reduced when M1 cells were exposed to RvD5 ( Figure 352 

5D ). Consistent with this observation, punctate LC3 labeling was predominantly 353 

seen in Mtb ‑infected macrophages relative to RvD5 ‑treated, Mtb ‑infected 354 

macrophages (Figure 5E) . Together, these results demonstrate that RvD5 355 

prevents the induction of autophagy in Mtb -infected macrophages, thereby 356 

impairing  a key mechanism through  which macrophages control bacterial 357

359 

Consistent with the role of autophagy -dependent mycobacterial degradation in 360 

enhancing antigen presentation to T cells 34,35 , we observed reduced CD4 T cell 361 

activation in antigen -presenting cell (APC) -dependent responses against 362 

irradiated  Mtb  (iMtb) when RvD5 was added to the cocultures ( Figure S10A –D). 363 

Notably, this effect was absent in APC -independent T cell activation assays 364 

(Figure S10E –G), providing additional evidence that RvD5 specifically 365 

compromises macrophage function. Interestingly, rapamycin treatment reversed 366 

the RvD5 -mediated inhibition of T cell activation, indicating that pharmacological 367 

restoration of autophagy is sufficient to improve antigen presentation ( Figure 368 

S10H -I). Together, these data directly link the effect of RvD5 on autophagy to 369 

impaired T cell responses. 370

reprograms the proinflammatory profile of M1 macrophages toward a more pro -373 

resolving, immunomodulatory phenotype. 374

HIF1α stabilization restores M1 macrophage features inhibited by the 376 

GPR32 -RvD5 axis 377 

To investigate whether the inhibition of HIF1α mediates the inhibition of the 378 

microbicidal activity of M1 macrophages induced by the RvD5 -GPR32 axis, we 379 

stabilized HIF1α expression by using Dimethyloxalylglycine (DMOG). 380 

Remarkably, HIF1α stabilization in RvD5 -treated M1 macrophages restored their 381 

impaired lactate and IL -1β release ( Figure 6A -B), as well as their microbicidal 382 

activity, evidenced by reduced bacillary loads and a higher localization of Mtb in 383 

compartments marked by the late endosomal marker, LAMP2 ( Figure 6C -D) .384 

Conversely, DMOG treatment diminished the pro -resolution features induced by 385 

RvD5, including enhanced efferocytic capacity, upregulated MerTK expression, 386 

and increased IL -10 production ( Figure S11 ).387 

388 

Furthermore, blocking GPR32 in the presence of RvD5 significantly improved 389 

control of intracellular bacterial growth ( Figure 6E ), providing direct evidence that 390 

RvD5 signals through GPR32 to impair microbicidal activity. 391 

392 

Together, these findings demonstrate that the RvD5 -GPR32 -HIF1α axis 393 

suppresses macrophage microbicidal functions and promotes susceptibility to 394 

Mtb . Importantly, these effects can be reversed by pharmacological stabilization 395 

of HIF1α. 396

Discussion 397 

Pleural TB presents unique challenges for healthcare providers, particularly 398 

concerning its treatment. Effective host defense against TB involves infiltration of 399 

peripheral blood cells into the pleural space, leading to accumulation of immune 400 

cells and soluble factors, including lipid mediators, in tuberculous pleural fluid. 401 

SPMs play a critical role in limiting inflammation and preventing collateral damage 402 

to healthy tissues. However, it is essential to carefully regulate the potential 403 

adverse effects of SPMs on the microbicidal functions of inflammatory 404 

macrophages to achieve a balanced approach that supports adequate pathogen 405 

clearance while minimizing excessive inflammation. While previous studies have 406 

highlighted the beneficial roles of SPMs in enhancing the microbicidal activities 407 

of macrophages and neutrophils 21,36 –38 , our study is the first to establish the 408 

involvement of the  RvD5 -GPR32 -HIF1α  axis  in TB. We demonstrate that RvD5, 409 

present in pleural TB, activates GPR32, thereby inhibiting HIF1α  -dependent 410 

microbicidal activity in macrophages. 411 

412 

Our study centers on two pivotal findings. First, we identified the RvD5 -GPR32 413 

axis as a key mediator of macrophage glycolysis suppression in TB pleural 414 

effusions. We demonstrated that RvD5 is not only present in pleural effusions but 415 

can also be produced by monocytes exposed to the acellular fraction of these 416 

effusions. Notably, CD14 + cells in the tuberculous pleural cavity constitute the 417 

primary mononuclear cell population responsible for local production of SPMs, 418 

such as resolvins. The accumulation of RvD5 within the pleural cavity correlates 419 

with reduced glycolytic capacity in CD14 + cells compared to their circulating 420 

counterparts. This suggests that upon entering the pleural cavity, phagocytes 421 

undergo a metabolic shift characterized by attenuated glycolytic activity, 422 

influenced by the local microenvironment. In line with this, in vitro exposure of 423 

macrophages to RvD5 led to a similar reduction in glycolysis. In this work, we 424 

used M1 -polarized cells to model the  in vitro  effects of pleural TB, given that 425 

pleural effusions from patients with active TB contain high levels of IFN -γ– in fact, 426 

IFN γ has been proposed as a diagnostic marker for tuberculous pleural effusions 427 

compared to other pleural diseases 39,40 . We acknowledge that this represents a 428 

simplified view of the complex, mixed macrophage phenotypes present in the 429 

pleural cavity. However, key findings (such as GPR32 expression and metabolic 430

We show that pleural macrophages from TB patients undergoing antibiotic 435 

treatment exhibit a significant increase in glycolytic activity compared to those 436 

from untreated patients. This observation indicates the presence of active 437 

mechanisms within the pleural cavity that suppress long -term protective immune 438 

responses, likely modulated by SPMs. Such findings offer a potential explanation 439 

for the frequent relapses observed in untreated pleural TB patients, suggesting 440 

that while glycolysis in macrophages is locally suppressed during active disease, 441 

systemically, monocytes in TB patients maintain a dysregulated glycolytic profile, 442 

as we recently reported 28 . This dichotomy supports the notion of contrasting 443 

metabolic environments at local and systemic levels. Even though using drugs 444 

that alter metabolism in all cells might have deleterious implications for patients, 445 

identifying strategies to do so selectively in macrophages has important clinical 446 

implications. We propose that upon entering the pleural cavity, monocytes 447 

upregulate ALOX5 transcript levels, thereby promoting local biosynthesis of 448 

SPMs. These SPMs, in turn, may modulate the metabolism of inflammatory 449 

macrophages, shifting them toward reduced glycolysis. This immunosuppressive 450 

metabolic state, however, appears reversible with TB treatment, underscoring the 451 

dynamic interplay between local immune modulation and systemic immune 452 

dysregulation in pleural TB. 453

Several studies have identified associations between ALOX5 polymorphisms and 455 

increased susceptibility to TB 41,42  or the development of multisystem TB 43 . These 456 

studies primarily focus on the role of ALOX5 in synthesizing pro -inflammatory 457 

lipid mediators, such as leukotrienes. In contrast, our findings identify RvD5 as a 458 

novel and underappreciated player in this context. Supporting our observations, 459 

ALOX5 -deficient mice have been shown to exhibit increased resistance to Mtb 460 

infection 44 . Interestingly, ALOX5 expression has been implicated in both pro -461 

inflammatory and immunosuppressive outcomes across various pathological 462 

contexts. For example, in rheumatoid arthritis, increased ALOX5 expression has 463 

been associated with harmful inflammation by driving pyroptosis in CD4 + T464

effects may change depending on microenvironmental factors, such as lipid 472

GPR32 belongs to the most intensively studied drug target family, mainly 478

Controlling excessive inflammation in the pleural space is crucial to prevent tissue 482 

damage and maintain host -pathogen balance. While RvD5 has been shown to 483 

impair the microbicidal functions of macrophages, other SPMs prevalent in 484 

pleural TB, such as PDx and MaR1, do not exhibit these detrimental effects 21 .485 

This underscores an opportunity to selectively enhance SPMs that preserve 486 

microbicidal activity while minimizing tissue damage, thereby avoiding lipid 487 

mediators such as RvD5. For instance, in tuberculous meningitis, elevated 488 

concentrations of SPMs in cerebrospinal fluid have been associated with 489 

improved patient survival 49 . These results support the idea of targeted 490 

interventions to harness the benefits of SPMs while mitigating the risks. 491 

492

“protective” lipid mediators, often manipulated through dietary supplementation 494 

with omega -3 fatty acids (n -3 PUFAs, precursors of protective lipids) versus 495 

omega -6 fatty acids (n -6 PUFAs, mostly precursors of proinflammatory lipids). 496 

However, many studies lack a detailed examination of the mechanisms of action 497 

for individual lipids. The outcomes of such supplementation studies have been 498

504 

Although some reports propose that certain SPMs, such as RvD1 53  or MaR1 54 ,505 

can enhance cellular respiration in neutrophils and macrophages, our findings did 506 

not demonstrate that RvD5 increases absolute mitochondrial respiration in 507 

macrophages stimulated with LPS and IFNγ. This discrepancy may be due to the 508 

strong metabolic imprinting induced by LPS and IFNγ, which results in decreased 509 

OXPHOS that RvD5 cannot counteract. Alternatively, the effect of RvD5 may be 510 

limited to reducing glycolysis without activating compensatory energetic 511 

mechanisms. These insights further support the complex, context -dependent 512 

nature of SPM -mediated metabolic regulation, warranting more in -depth studies 513 

to fully understand their roles and therapeutic potential in TB. 514 

515 

Our second key finding reveals that activation of GPR32 by RvD5 inhibits HIF1α 516 

-dependent microbicidal activity in macrophages, shedding light on a critical 517 

pathway that modulates immune responses in TB. Previously, we demonstrated 518 

that lipids extracted from the acellular fraction of TB pleural effusions suppress 519 

HIF1α expression, leading to reduced glycolytic and microbicidal phenotypes in 520 

macrophages 15 . The present study goes beyond our previous results, as we now 521 

show that RvD5 is explicitly a central lipid mediator driving this effect through 522 

GPR32 binding. While GPR32 is the best -characterized receptor for RvD5, 523 

emerging evidence suggests that RvD5 may also interact with GPR101, which is 524 

also expressed in macrophages 53 . As GPR101 is constitutively active, any role 525 

for RvD5 would likely involve enhancing or fine -tuning its basal signaling rather 526 

than classical activation. The potential contribution of this alternative receptor to 527 

the observed metabolic effects was not explored in this study and remains an 528 

open avenue for future research. 529 

530 

Our results demonstrate that RvD5 impairs Mtb clearance, at least partially, by 531 

limiting the induction of autophagy  in infected macrophages. Importantly, this 532

RvD5 also diminished IL -1β levels in Mtb -infected macrophages. IL -1β is 536 

essential for host control of Mtb: genetic mouse models lacking IL -1 signaling 537 

exhibit high mortality and increased bacterial burdens 55 , and clinical blockade of 538 

IL -1 with anakinra increases TB reactivation risk 56 . Critically for our study,  Il1b 539 

transcription is regulated by HIF -1α -dependent glycolytic reprogramming upon 540 

Mtb infection 57,58 , and IL -1β itself induces autophagy leading to mycobacterial 541 

killing 57 . Thus, the RvD5 -mediated reduction of both HIF -1α and IL -1β may 542 

cooperatively impair autophagy and bacterial clearance. Of note, it has been 543 

previously shown that  Mtb  can inhibit IFNγ -induced autophagy via an ALOX5 -544 

dependent pathway involving Mir155 -Mir31 -WNT -SHH signaling 59 . However, the 545 

role of RvD5 was not investigated in that study. Interestingly, unlike RvD5, other 546 

resolvins such as RvD1 promote autophagy in various contexts 60,61 . These 547 

findings emphasize the functional specificity of different SPMs and caution 548 

against generalizing biological functions across the resolvin family. 549 

550 

In conclusion, our study provides valuable insights into the pathophysiology of 551 

pleural TB by uncovering RvD5 as a critical lipid mediator that modulates 552 

macrophage metabolism and function through GPR32. This work underlines the 553 

importance of understanding the specific mechanisms by which lipid mediators 554 

influence immune cell metabolism and TB severity. These results pave the way 555 

for the development of targeted therapeutic strategies that aim to balance 556 

immune regulation, preserve microbicidal activity, and mitigate the detrimental 557 

effects of excessive inflammation in TB. 558
