Abstract: During an immune response, metabolism changes dramatically. Metabolites are 22 

oxidized to power immune cell functions, serve as building blocks for proliferation, and act as 23 

effectors to regulate pathogen or host cells. Though metabolic changes in cultured cells have 24 

been studied extensively, metabolism changes in vivo are less understood. Here, we measured 25 

metabolomic changes across six mouse tissues in three models of immune activation: CpG-26 

DNA cytokine storm, lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus infection, and polyI:C viral mimetic 27 

injection; and carried out metabolomics in cultured macrophages activated with different stimuli. 28 

We found most metabolomic changes were exclusive to either inflamed tissues or cultured 29 

macrophages, although itaconate was strongly induced in both contexts. We then 30 

mechanistically dissected the role of the soluble sialic acid N-glycolylneuraminic acid, which is 31 

highly induced in inflamed tissues yet only modestly in cultured macrophages. This metabolite 32 

rises in tissues in different models of inflammation, and the analogous human metabolite, N-33 

acetylneuraminic acid, is increased in human patients experiencing inflammation. We found that 34

Introduction: 41 

An immune response in the body starts when pathogens or cellular damage trigger 42 

receptors on immune and tissue stromal cells 1. These signals start an amplifying cascade that 43 

leads to proliferation and activation of immune cells including macrophages, neutrophils, and T 44 

cells. These immune cells can migrate through a tissue, engulf dying cells, directly kill 45 

pathogens, kill infected or damaged host cells, and produce inflammatory mediators including 46 

cytokine proteins and reactive oxygen species 1.47 

Metabolic changes underlie the immune response. Cells consume nutrients to make 48 

ATP, then use ATP to produce proteins, proliferate, and migrate; metabolites like itaconate and 49 

reactive oxygen species serve as direct effectors of the immune response; and sickness 50 

behaviors suppress feeding and movement and thereby alter whole-body metabolism 2–7 .51 

While metabolic changes caused by activation of immune cells, like T cells 2,5,6,8 or 52 

macrophages 9–14 , have been carefully studied in tissue culture, how metabolite levels change in 53 

immune responses in vivo is less well characterized. For example, when a mouse or human 54 

mounts an immune response, do the same metabolites change as when immune cells are 55 

stimulated in tissue culture? Does a given inflammatory stimulus induce specific metabolite 56 

changes, or do all immune stimuli converge on similar metabolite changes? Further, is there a 57 

functional role for the metabolites changed in inflamed tissues? 58 

Here, we set out to catalog metabolite changes in the blood, liver, spleen, lung, kidney 59 

and brain in response to different inflammatory stimuli in mice. We then measured metabolomic 60 

changes in cultured stimulated macrophages, to allow direct comparison of in vivo and in vitro 61 

immunometabolic changes. We found that in mice, most metabolite changes were unique to 62 

particular inflammatory stimuli and were also not found in stimulated macrophages in culture. In 63 

contrast, stimulated macrophages changed a shared set of metabolites no matter the stimulus. 64 

We identified one previously-uninvestigated immunometabolite, the soluble sialic acid N-65 

glycolylneuraminic acid, that increased dramatically during in vivo inflammation yet only slightly 66 

in stimulated macrophages. We then characterized this metabolite’s production pathway, cell 67 

type of origin, and function in inflammation. We found that N-glycolylneuraminic acid is 68

Results: 74 

A tissue inflammation metabolomic atlas 75 

Metabolite levels change during an immune response. Immune cell proliferation, 76 

migration, cytotoxicity, and production of protein effectors requires increases in metabolic fluxes 77 

including glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and nucleotide synthesis 2,6,12,15 . In addition, certain 78 

metabolites are produced by activated immune cells as direct effectors. Key examples include 79 

itaconate 16–20 and nitric oxide 21,22 : these metabolites are both produced by myeloid cells and 80 

exert antimicrobial effects as well as regulatory effects on host cells. However, in most cases 81 

such functional immunometabolites were identified using cultured immune cells; careful studies 82 

of metabolomic changes during immune responses in animals may reveal immune-induced 83 

metabolic changes that do not occur in tissue culture. 84 

Here, we set out to identify how immune challenges in mice change tissue metabolite 85 

levels and whether altered metabolites influence the outcome of the immune response. First, we 86 

gave three different immune stimuli to cohorts of C57BL/6 mice ( Figure 1A-C ): a model of 87 

cytokine storm induced by repeated injection of CpG DNA 23,24 , viral infection with the chronic 88 

Clone 13 strain of the mouse virus lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) 25 , and injection of 89 

the viral RNA mimetic polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (PolyI:C) 26 . We then used high-resolution 90 

liquid-chromatography mass-spectrometry (LC-MS) metabolomics to identify metabolic 91 

changes, detecting and annotating around 700 features per dataset based on mass-to-charge 92 

ratio and retention time. 93 

These datasets comprise a tissue atlas of metabolomic changes in inflammation. Across 94 

the six tissue types measured (liver, spleen, blood serum, lung, kidney and brain), we found the 95 

greatest number of significant changes occurred in the liver for each stimulation ( Figure 1A, 96 

Figure S1A-E, Supplementary Tables ). LCMV infection led to the most metabolite changes in 97 

the liver ( Figure 1D ), perhaps because ongoing viral replication continually produces pathogen-98 

associated molecular patterns. LCMV altered 107 metabolites in the liver increase or decrease 99 

of at least 3-fold and p-value less than 0.05, compared to 22 altered metabolites during CpG-100 

induced cytokine storm and 34 after PolyI:C injection. The majority of changes (66-90%) were 101 

metabolite increases in each model, rather than decreases ( Supplementary Tables) .102

A small set of liver metabolites were changed by two or more stimuli ( Figure 1D-E ). 106 

Itaconate was the one metabolite robustly increased across all three in vivo immune stimuli. 107 

Other shared changes across two out of three conditions included the pyrimidine nucleoside 108 

cytidine and the amino acid aspartate, a building block for pyrimidines ( Figure 1A-E, Figure 109 

S1F ). Both LCMV infection and CpG cytokine storm dramatically raised the level of a metabolite 110 

annotated as N-glycolylneuraminic acid ( Figure 1A-E , increased 6.5-fold in the liver in LCMV 111 

and over twenty-fold in CpG cytokine storm). N-glycolylneuraminic acid is a sialic acid; it is best-112 

studied in its role in protein glycosylation 27 , while the soluble, non-protein-conjugated form has 113 

been little-studied. 114

Macrophage activation in vitro does not replicate most metabolic changes observed in 116 

inflamed tissues 117 

Most of the metabolites most altered in inflamed livers, including N-glycolylneuraminic 118 

acid, had not previously been studied in their connection with immune response. We 119 

hypothesized that perhaps these understudied metabolites were changed in tissues yet not 120 

observed in cultured immune cells. To this end, we measured metabolomic changes induced by 121 

stimulation of mouse bone-marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM). Macrophages carry a wide 122 

variety of sensors for pathogen- and damage-associated molecular patterns 1, and are a key 123 

model system for studying metabolic change in response to immune stimulus. Indeed, itaconate 124 

induction and its role in inflammation were first observed in cultured macrophages 16,17 .125 

We stimulated mouse bone-marrow-derived macrophages with either lipopolysaccharide 126 

(LPC), CpG DNA, or PolyI:C ( Figure 2A ), mimicking stimuli present during bacterial and viral 127 

infection 28 , and measured metabolomic changes after 24 hours. At this timepoint, we detected 128 

robust changes in both metabolite levels and gene expression ( Figures S2A-B ). In contrast to 129 

our findings in mouse tissue inflammation, in macrophages different stimuli changed similar 130 

metabolites: 73-94% altered metabolites were changed in multiple stimuli in macrophages, 131 

versus 32% or less in livers (Figure 2B-E ). Metabolites increased by two or more stimuli 132 

included itaconate; the nucleotide triphosphates ATP and UTP; the glycolytic intermediates 133 

fructose-1-6-bisphosphate and dihydroxyacetonephosphate; and the urea cycle intermediate 134 

argininosuccinate ( Figure 2E-F ). Enrichment analysis identified many shared altered metabolic 135

Inflammation increases soluble sialic acid in mice and humans 148 

We decided to investigate the regulation and functional role of one of the metabolites 149 

induced in tissue inflammation, the soluble sialic acid N-glycolylneuraminic acid (Neu5Gc, 150 

Figure 1 ). First, we validated the identity of this compound by confirming that its mass-to-charge 151 

ratio, retention time, and tandem-MS fragmentation pattern matched that of a purchased N-152 

glycolylneuraminic acid standard ( Figure 3A ). 153 

Next, we asked whether inflammation induced N-glycolylneuraminic acid (Neu5Gc) in 154 

tissues beyond the liver. We found that in the CpG cytokine storm model, Neu5Gc was most 155 

highly induced in liver, yet was also increased in the blood serum, kidney, spleen, brain and lung 156 

(Figure 3B ). Using an external standard curve we found that Neu5Gc reached extremely high 157 

levels in the liver, around 3 millimolar, higher than the level of any amino acid in the blood 30 .158 

LCMV infection also raised N-glycolylneuraminic acid in mouse liver and spleen ( Figure 3C, 159 

Figure S3A ). Examining the third model of inflammation we tested, PolyI:C injection, we 160 

discovered that even though N-glycolylneuraminic acid was not initially observed as a highly 161 

altered metabolite after 24 hours ( Figure 1C-E ), 48 hours after PolyI:C injection N-162 

glycolylneuraminic acid was significantly elevated in the liver and other tissues ( Figure 3D ). An 163 

additional inflammatory model, topical application of the TLR7 agonist imiquimod which mimics 164 

psoriasis 31 , also induced Neu5Gc in skin, liver and spleen ( Figure S3B ). Therefore, every 165 

inflammatory model we have investigated in mice induced the soluble sialic acid Neu5Gc. 166 

In contrast, bone-marrow-derived macrophages stimulated with various TLR ligands only 167 

showed a modest induction of this metabolite, between 1.3- and 2.7-fold ( Figure 3E; Figure 168 

S3C ). This relatively modest increase of Neu5Gc in macrophages, in contrast to the dramatic 169

We next investigated whether soluble sialic acid also increases in human patients 173 

experiencing inflammation. N-glycolylneuraminic acid is the most abundant sialic acid in mice 174 

and most other mammals, yet this form of sialic acid does not exist in humans, due to a loss of 175

3F ). In humans, the most abundant sialic acid is instead N-acetylneuraminic acid ( Figure 3F ). 177 

Publicly available datasets revealed that N-acetylneuraminic acid is elevated in the blood serum 178 

in patients with a range of inflammatory conditions, including patients in septic shock 32 (Figure 179 

3G ), with COVID-19 infection 33 (Figure 3H ), or with liver failure 34 (Figure 3I ). Therefore, soluble 180 

sialic acid is an evolutionarily-conserved metabolite elevated in inflammation. 181

Soluble sialic acid is produced from sialylated proteins by CD11b+ myeloid cells in the 183 

liver 184 

Since we found that soluble sialic acid is increased in a variety of inflammatory states in 185 

both mice and humans, we asked how this molecule is produced. There has been extensive 186 

study of the synthesis of protein-bound sialic acids 27,35 , but the soluble form is much less 187 

studied. Protein-bound sialic acids are produced from a pathway including the intermediates 188 

UDP-N-acetyl-glucosamine and CMP-sialic acid ( Figure 4A ), then sialyltransferase enzymes 189 

add the sialic acid moiety from CMP-sialic acid onto a protein-conjugated glycan chain as the 190 

terminal sugar 27 . Soluble Neu5Gc is produced if neuraminidase enzymes (Neu1-4) cleave sialic 191 

acid off a glycosylated protein 36 . However, the vast production of Neu5Gc we observed in 192 

inflammation, over 3mM in the liver during CpG cytokine storm ( Figure 3B ), required that we 193 

confirm that this conventional pathway was used and not some previously-unknown route. 194 

To confirm that the described pathway is used to produce Neu5Gc in mouse tissues, we 195 

first used heavy-isotope-labeled nutrient intravenous infusion (schematic Figure 4B ). The 196 

upstream precursor of sialic acids, UDP-N-acetyl-glucosamine (UDP-GlcNAc), can be produced 197 

from glucose or from glucosamine 37 (Figure 4A ). Therefore, if we infuse carbon-13-glucose or -198 

glucosamine, any metabolite made from UDP-GlcNAc would therefore have similar or less 199 

carbon-13 labeling as UDP-GlcNAc ( Figure 4B ). Consistent with the previously-described 200 

pathway, Neu5Gc in the liver of healthy mice indeed displayed carbon-13 labeling from 201 

intravenously-infused carbon-13-glucosamine as well as glucose ( Figure 4C-D, Figure S4A-B ). 202 

The labeling was of a reduced magnitude compared to the precursors UDP-HexNAc and CMP-203

We next wondered how N-glycolylneuraminic acid level is increased during 207 

inflammation. Two non-mutually-exclusive possibilities are that more sialylated protein is 208 

produced during inflammation, or that there is increased neuraminidase activity during 209 

inflammation. To address these possibilities, we measured the level of protein-conjugated as 210 

well as free N-glycolylneuraminic acid in livers of mice after 10-day induction of CpG cytokine 211 

storm ( Figure 4E-F ). Although there is a trend toward increased protein-bound sialic acid in 212 

livers of CpG-injected mice, with the mean value increased two- to four-fold ( Figure 4E, Figure 213 

S4C ), the increase in free Neu5Gc in CpG-treated mice is around twenty-fold (p=0.0008, Figure 214 

4F ). Therefore, increased neuraminidase activity is the major contributor to the increase in liver 215 

Neu5Gc in inflammation. 216 

To further confirm that Neu5Gc in inflammation was produced by cleavage from 217 

sialylated protein, we treated mice with the sialyltransferase inhibitor P-3F AX -Neu5Ac along with 218 

CpG cytokine storm induction. P-3F AX -Neu5Ac is expected to prevent sialic acid addition to 219 

glycosylated proteins 38 , and our data confirmed that this inhibitor indeed lowered liver protein-220 

bound sialic acid ( Figure 4E ). If soluble Neu5Gc were produced by cleavage from sialylated 221 

proteins, reducing the sialylated protein pool with sialyltransferase inhibitor should reduce 222 

soluble Neu5Gc. This was indeed the case: treating mice with the sialyltransferase inhibitor 223 

during CpG cytokine storm reducing liver soluble Neu5Gc close to the level in uninflamed mice 224 

(Figure 4F ). Therefore, soluble N-glycolylneuraminic acid is produced via sialyltransferase 225 

conjugation to proteins followed by neuraminidase cleavage. 226

hepatocytes, we asked what is the cellular source of Neu5Gc in the liver. We observed in 228 

publicly available data 39 that Neuraminidase-1 ( Neu1 ) RNA is induced in splenic macrophages 229

polyI:C ( Figure S4B )24 , its stimulation of Neu1 RNA levels suggests that myeloid cells might be 231 

responsible for the increased cleavage and release of Neu5Gc during inflammation. Consistent 232 

with this hypothesis, macrophages upregulate neuraminidase activity during differentiation 40 and 233 

activation 41 .234

N-glycolylneuraminic acid does not affect pathology in cytokine storm or viral infection 251 

The metabolite itaconate is highly induced in myeloid cells during inflammation or infection, 252 

and exerts important antimicrobial and immune-regulatory roles 18,19,29 . We asked whether 253 

Neu5Gc, also highly induced in myeloid cells during inflammation, also plays a functional role. 254 

To address this question, we took advantage of the sialyltransferase inhibitor P-3F AX -Neu5Ac, 255 

which blocked the induction of soluble Neu5Gc during CpG-induced inflammation ( Figure 3E-F, 256 

Figure S5A ). In fact, we found that this drug reduced Neu5Gc with moderate specificity, only 257 

reducing three other metabolites more than 3-fold ( Figure 5A ). 258 

However, reducing Neu5Gc levels did not seem to alter liver pathology in CpG-induced 259 

cytokine storm. Using blinded histopathological assessment, increased foci of extramedullary 260 

hematopoiesis, increased prominence of cells lining the sinusoids, and increased cellular 261 

infiltrates within the sinusoids were identified in livers with CpG administration compared to 262 

control animals ( Figure 5B-C, Figure S5B ). However, daily treatment with sialyltransferase 263 

inhibitor neither worsened nor improved these phenotypes. While CpG administration induced a 264 

number of RNAs in the liver including Tnf , Ifng, and Acod1 (the enzyme that produces itaconate) 265 

(Figure 5D , Figure S5C ), there were no changes in RNA level comparing livers of mice 266 

receiving CpG alone versus CpG with sialyltransferase inhibitor ( Figure 5E ). Therefore, 267 

blocking Neu5Gc induction did not alter gene expression or pathology during CpG cytokine 268 

storm. 269

Discussion 289 

Here, we provide an atlas of metabolomic changes in inflammation in mouse models and 290 

in tissue culture macrophages. We identified metabolites increased during immune responses in 291 

vivo , including N-glycolylneuraminic acid, aspartate, and thymidine. We then characterized the 292 

production route, cellular source, and function of one of these novel immunometabolites, the 293 

soluble sialic acid N-glycolylneuraminic acid (Neu5Gc). We showed that this metabolite is 294 

induced in mice by all in vivo inflammatory stimuli tested. The analogous human metabolite, N-295 

acetylneuraminic acid, was also induced by inflammation including sepsis, COVID-19 infection, 296 

or liver failure. We showed that Neu5Gc is produced by cleavage and release of protein-bound 297 

sialic acid by CD11b+ myeloid cells. However, we could find no effect of Neu5Gc production on 298 

pathology, gene expression, viral infection, or T cell phenotype. Our experiments demonstrate 299 

that metabolomic changes in mice or human patients during immune activation are distinct from 300 

changes induced in tissue culture macrophage activation. 301 

It was surprising to us that most metabolite changes in inflamed tissues were distinct 302 

from changes in activated macrophages. A notable exception was itaconate, which increased in 303 

inflamed tissues and macrophages ( Figure 1-2 ); as expected, itaconate was produced in vivo 304 

by myeloid cells ( Figure S4G ). Why were other metabolite changes not conserved between 305

dominant producers of specific metabolites; for example our previous work suggested that 307 

thymidine may be produced by T cells during inflammation 45 . Note however that even in healthy 308 

tissues, myeloid cells are by far the most abundant immune cell type 46 , and their numbers rise 309 

during inflammation; therefore these cells may well be dominant contributors to metabolic 310 

changes in inflamed tissues in many cases. Second, there may be signals or metabolic 311 

substrates present in vivo that are not replicated in tissue culture. This second possibility may 312 

be the explanation for the stronger induction of Neu5Gc in vivo , since myeloid cells produce it in 313 

the inflamed liver ( Figure 4H-I ) but not in culture when activated with the same stimulus ( Figure 314 

3E ). 315 

Identifying which cell type is responsible for production of any metabolite in vivo is 316 

technically challenging, since tissues in mice and humans are a mix of different cell types. Here, 317 

our data supports a role for CD11b+ myeloid cells in the production of Neu5Gc soluble sialic 318 

acid ( Figure 4, Figure S4 ). (Note that several cell types, including macrophages and 319 

neutrophils, express the CD11b+ marker 47 ; so far we don’t know which of these cell subsets 320 

may be the dominant Neu5Gc producer.) However, a caveat of our cell isolation experiments is 321 

that tissue digestion and cell purification can alter metabolite levels, since the cells live and 322 

metabolize in an altered nutrient milieu during the isolation procedure 15,48,49 . The definitive way 323 

to test this hypothesis would be to generate a mouse conditionally deficient for Neu5Gc 324 

production in myeloid cells- for example LysM-cre Neu1 fl/fl 50 - and test whether Neu5Gc is no 325 

longer induced when the mouse is exposed to an inflammatory stimulus. Another approach 326 

would be to use imaging mass spectrometry to colocalize tissue Neu5Gc to myeloid cells 51 ,327 

although currently the spatial resolution of this technique precludes single-cell resolution. 328 

Why would this metabolite Neu5Gc be so highly induced- up to 3mM in CpG cytokine 329 

storm liver-yet play no functional role? Possibly Neu5Gc does play a role in a context not 330 

examined here: for example, in the resolution of inflammation or wound healing. Alternatively, it 331 

is possible that neuraminidase activity itself, rather than the metabolite it produces, can play a 332 

role in inflammation, perhaps via its role in removing sialic acid from glycosylated proteins. 333 

Sialylated glycoproteins send immunosuppressive signals via the family of Siglec receptors 52 ;334 

neuraminidase removal of the terminal sialic acid from glycoproteins could help unleash the 335 

immune response. Our experiments using P-3F AX -Neu5Ac blocked the addition of sialic acid 336 

onto glycoproteins; yet perhaps Neu1 cleavage off mature glycoproteins has a distinct effect on 337 

the immune response. 338
