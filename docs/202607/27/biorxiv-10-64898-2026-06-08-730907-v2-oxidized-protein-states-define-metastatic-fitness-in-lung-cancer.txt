Abstract 
38 
Reactive oxygen species (ROS) are a pervasive feature of human cancers, yet the protein 
39 
targets through which ROS-regulated cell states shape tumor biology remains poorly 
40 
understood. Here, using cysteine chemical proteomics, we define signatures of protein 
41 
states under distinct cellular ROS environments that capture protein oxidation and 
42 
conformational changes. Quantifying these signatures in primary lung tumors and brain 
43 
metastases revealed a surprising enrichment of oxidative states in metastasis. To 
44 
determine how these states support fitness, we performed genome-wide CRISPR screens 
45 
and identified the mitochondrial Complex I subunit NDUFA10 as a key oxidation-
46 
dependent vulnerability. Oxidation of NDUFA10•Cys253 supports Complex I function

Introduction 
55 
Reactive oxygen species (ROS) imbalance is widespread across many cancers, yet their role in 
56 
tumor biology remains unresolved (1-5). ROS have long been viewed as damaging agents that 
57 
promote genomic instability and tumor initiation (3, 6-10), but excessive ROS can also impose a 
58 
bottleneck to continued tumor growth and metastatic progression (11). Indeed, experimental 
59 
and clinical studies have produced seemingly contradictory conclusions, with ROS reported to 
60 
both promote and suppress tumor phenotypes depending on context (12, 13). Despite this 
61 
duality, it remains unclear how ROS levels correlate with tumor phenotypes such as 
62 
aggressiveness or metastatic potential. This question is particularly consequential in lung 
63 
cancer, where ROS play a key role in cancer development (3, 14, 15) and metastasis which is 
64 
responsible for ~90% of cancer-related deaths (13, 16-18).  
65 
A major challenge in understanding ROS biology in tumors is the lack of framework to 
66 
define and compare cellular redox states in human disease. Most studies infer ROS indirectly 
67 
through transcriptional activation of antioxidant pathways, measurements of redox metabolites, 
68 
or genomic scars associated with oxidative stress (2, 3, 19, 20). However, these approaches 
69 
are confounded by pleiotropic functions unrelated to the direct effects of ROS on proteins and 
70 
incompletely capture how these reactive metabolites impact the functional state of the tumor cell. 
71 
In contrast to genetic or transcriptional signatures, which robustly define tumor subtypes, 
72 
vulnerabilities, and prognosis (21-23), there is currently no comparable framework for defining 
73 
ROS-associated cellular states in human cancers. As a result, ROS are typically treated as a 
74 
nonspecific stress rather than a regulatory signal with defined biological outputs including 
75 
determinants of cellular state.  
76 
Proteins represent a direct molecular interface through which ROS can alter cellular 
77 
functions (24-28). Within the proteome, cysteine residues are uniquely suited to mediate redox 
78 
regulation due to their chemical reactivity and capacity to undergo reversible oxidation (24, 26,

81

82

83

85

89

90 
Here, we sought to determine the oxidized and reduced protein states that lung cancers 
91

92

93

94

95 
accessibility using chemical proteomics. By integrating changes in cysteine oxidation and 
96 
protein conformation, this approach allowed us to derive defined oxidized and reduced protein-
97 
state signatures.

100

101

102

103 
progression. To identify such functions, we combined ROS-state profiling with genome-wide

Defining ROS-associated protein conformational states in lung cancer  
118 
ROS levels across stages of lung cancer progression have been inferred through transcriptional 
119 
programs such as NRF2 and NF-κB activation, or through genomic scars induced by oxidative 
120 
stress (17, 49-51). However, how ROS impacts global protein states across different stages of 
121 
lung cancer remains unknown. Because proteins are direct effectors of cellular function, we 
122 
reasoned that defining tumor states at the level of protein oxidation and conformation, could 
123 
provide a mechanistic framework linking ROS environments to cancer phenotypes.  
124 
To directly address this question, we profiled proteome-wide cysteine accessibility using 
125 
chemical proteomics in human lung cancer specimens. Cysteine accessibility is shaped both by 
126 
direct oxidation (e.g., sulfinylation) (31, 35, 52) and changes in protein conformation that alter 
127 
solvent exposure (53). Coordinated cysteine accessibility changes across the proteome can be

Disruption of mitochondrial Complex I mediates sensitivity to a reduced cell state  
267 
Our analysis of patient lung tumors revealed that brain metastases are enriched for oxidized 
268 
protein states, suggesting that the corresponding environment is not merely a byproduct of 
269 
oxidative stress, but may instead be selectively maintained to support tumor fitness. We 
270 
therefore hypothesized that metastatic cells may become dependent on proteins whose function 
271 
requires oxidation-dependent conformational states. To identify pathways and genes required 
272 
for fitness under oxidized conditions, we sought genes whose loss mediates cellular sensitivity 
273 
when ROS levels are lowered.

Having identified Complex I as a key dependency of cancer cells under reducing environments, 
305 
we next sought to determine the molecular mechanism linking ROS states to Complex I function.  
306 
We reasoned that proteins requiring oxidation-dependent conformations for activity should 
307 
satisfy two criteria: 1) they should undergo ROS-associated cysteine accessibility changes in 
308 
our proteomic atlas, and 2) their function should become limiting when cells are forced into 
309 
reduced states.  
310 
To identify such candidates, we integrated our chemical proteomic analysis of ROS cell 
311 
states with CRISPR loss-of-function screening to nominate high-priority cysteine targets that 
312 
may need oxidation for function (Fig. 3H, Methods). Focusing on the ETC given the results of 
313 
our CRISPR screen, this analysis highlighted 17 candidates, with the Complex I cysteine 
314 
NDUFA10•C253 emerging as one of the top-ranked hits (Fig. 3H and 4A). NDUFA10 is required 
315 
for Complex I assembly (75), and consistent with this role, depletion of NDUFA10 disrupted 
316 
mitochondrial respiration and Complex I activity, and increased NADH levels, a phenotype 
317 
rescued by expression of an sgRNA-resistant NDUFA10 PAM mutant (Fig. 4B-E, fig. S6A-E). 
318 
To determine whether the redox state of Cys253 directly regulates NDUFA10 function, we 
319 
generated Cys–>Ser and Cys–>Asp mutants of reduced and higher-order oxidized (sulfinic 
320 
acid-like) cysteines, respectively (61, 76) (Fig. 4B). While Asp preserves the negative charge 
321 
associated with cysteine sulfinic acid, it differs in its geometry and coordination chemistry. 
322 
Therefore, we interpret our findings with this mutant as a mimetic for this type of ROS post-
323 
translational modification. NDUFA10•C253S failed to rescue the NADH/NAD+ imbalance and 
324 
Complex I defects caused by NDUFA10 loss (Fig. 4C-E, fig. S6D, E). In contrast, the 
325

Discussion 
463 
A longstanding challenge in cancer biology has been to understand how the tumor environment 
464 
shapes cellular behavior beyond what can be inferred from genetic or transcriptional states (16, 
465 
96). Redox biology exemplifies this gap: although regulation of oxidative stress has been 
466 
ubiquitously linked to tumor initiation, growth, and metastasis (2-4), the field has lacked a 
467 
systematic framework that connects variation in the ROS redox state to defined molecular 
468 
mechanisms and cancer phenotypes. In this study, we demonstrate that ROS-associated 
469 
protein states can be captured in human tumors at the level of cysteine accessibility, providing a 
470 
structural and mechanistic complement to genetic and transcriptomic descriptions of tumor cell 
471 
states. This work further supports the premise that protein oxidation can function as a regulatory 
472 
input that shapes tumor fitness, rather than merely a source of molecular damage.  
473 
Cysteine accessibility is directly influenced by the intracellular environment. Accordingly, 
474 
chemical proteomics allows us to understand the corresponding ensemble of protein states 
475 
under a given environmental condition. By defining signatures of tumor redox environments 
476 
through changes in cysteine accessibility, this work reframes redox biology through a structural 
477 
lens. This is particularly relevant in metastasis, where cancer cells encounter extreme 
478 
microenvironmental constraints and where redox sensitivity has been repeatedly observed but 
479 
poorly understood at the level of proteome (11, 13, 16). Thus, chemical proteomic signatures 
480 
provide a residue-level connection linking changes in the cellular environment to protein function

483

484

485

486

487

489

490

492

493

495

496 
High levels of oxidative stress are damaging to the electron transport chain (73, 97-99). 
497 
Our findings extend these observations by demonstrating that Complex I function depends on a 
498

499

500

501
