Abstract  
35 
While colorectal cancer (CRC) is thought to originate primarily from resident stem cells, diet heavily 
36 
influences intestinal stem cell homeostasis and disease onset. In this study, we demonstrate that a 
37 
Western-style diet (WSD) alters intestinal homeostasis by uncoupling canonical stemness from 
38 
tumorigenesis. WSD exposure suppresses canonical Lgr5⁺ stem cells while activating an alternative 
39 
pool of facultative c-Kit⁺ secretory cells. Although these reprogrammed stem-like cells exhibit genotoxic 
40 
stress, they remain proliferative under prolonged dietary exposure, suggesting increased susceptibility 
41 
to tumor-initiating mutations. We identify diet-induced microbial shifts and the expansion of 
42 
enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (ETBF) as the upstream driver. ETBF and its toxin fragilysin 
43 
autonomously trigger multipotency in c-Kit+ cells through Wnt/β-catenin and YAP signaling. 
44 
Importantly, these alterations are fully reversible upon dietary intervention. Together, our results 
45 
highlight a targetable dietary-microbial axis that shapes epithelial stemness and underscores the role 
46 
of facultative stem cells in diet-induced CRC onset.

Introduction 
48 
One of the main dogmas in cancer biology predicts that most malignancies arise from resident stem 
49 
cells. However, as we recently demonstrated in mouse models and validated in patient-derived colon 
50 
cancers, in the context of inflammation, intestinal adenomas rather originate from committed lineages. 
51 
This observation is of relevance for the so-called “bad luck cancer” debate, triggered by the TomasetÝ 
52 
& Vogelstein study1, according to which cancer risk is mainly determined by the rate at which stem cells 
53 
divide and accumulate spontaneously occurring somatic mutations. However, in their mathematical 
54 
model, stem cell division and environmental factors were regarded as two independent variables, 
55 
somehow implying that etiologic risk factors such as diet and inflammation in colon cancer do not affect 
56 
the rate at which stem cells divide2. This is  in contradiction with our current knowledge of the intestinal 
57 
epithelium as one of the most rapidly renewing tissues in mammals maintained, in homeostasis, by a 
58 
pool of cycling Leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein coupled receptor 5 expressing intestinal stem 
59 
cells (Lgr5⁺ ISCs), located at the base of the crypts3. However, in response to tissue damage, such as 
60 
chemical- or radiation-induced tissue injury, the intestinal epithelium exhibits remarkable plasticity, 
61 
whereby differentiated or lineage-committed cells can revert to a stem-like state to support intestinal 
62 
regeneration. This facultative stem cell activity has been described in multiple intestinal lineages, 
63 
including Paneth cells (PCs) in the small intestine (SI) and deep crypt secretory (DCS) cells in the colon, 
64 
particularly in the context of severe inflammation and/or tissue damage4-8. 
65 
While beneficial for the tissue’s regenerative response, the recruitment of lineage-committed intestinal 
66 
epithelial cells (IECs) into the stem cell pool may be deleterious, as their reprogramming can confer 
67 
tumor-initiating capacity, as shown in experimental mouse models of acute dextran sodium sulphate 
68 
(DSS)-driven) inflammation and in patients with a history of inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD)9-12. Of 
69 
note, we observed that colon cancer initiation from committed cells is not necessarily restricted to 
70 
patients suffering from IBD, but also holds true for a substantial proportion of sporadic, non-IBD cases12. 
71 
As such, it is plausible to hypothesize that increased exposure to environmental risk factors leading to 
72 
subclinical but persistent low-grade inflammation may exert comparable reprogramming effects.

From this perspective, Western-style diet (WSD), a main modifiable colon cancer risk factor, 
74 
characterized by high fat and low fibre content and strongly associated with colon cancer13, is shown 
75 
to activate myeloid cells and induce innate immune responses leading to a subclinical, though 
76 
persistent type of metabolic inflammation, termed metaflammation14,15. Here, we investigated 
77 
whether the WSD-driven inflammation alters the composition of the intestinal stem cell niche and 
78 
expands the pool of cell targets for tumor initiation, similar to what was observed upon acute 
79 
inflammation in mice (DSS-driven) and men (IBD)4,11.  
80 
We found that WSD rapidly suppresses canonical Lgr5⁺ ISCs while enhancing epithelial stemness 
81 
through activation of reserve stem cell programmes in small intestinal PCs and DCS cells of the colon. 
82 
The reprogrammed PCs and DCS cells undergo genotoxic and inflammatory stress, rendering them 
83 
vulnerable to somatic mutations and positioning them as potential drivers of tumorigenesis. The 
84 
inflammation-associated reprogramming is driven, at least in part, by alterations of the microbiome, 
85 
including expansion of enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis (B. fragilis).  
86 
Collectively, our findings reveal that dietary cues can reversibly rewire intestinal stem cell identity 
87 
through inflammation and microbiota-dependent mechanisms, providing a functional link among diet, 
88 
the gut microbiome, and epithelial plasticity.

NWD1 enhances colon tumor onset  
178 
It was previously reported that normal (i.e. not genetically modified) C57Bl/6 mice fed with NWD1 for 
179 
longer intervals (18-24 months) develop benign and malignant neoplasms in the colon25. To confirm 
180 
the NWD1-driven increased susceptibility to colonic tumors, Cdx2CreERT2; Apcfl/+ mice were fed for 1 
181 
months with the AIN76A and NWD1 diets before administering tamoxifen to induce monoallelic 
182 
deletion of the adenomatous polyposis coli (Apc) tumor suppressor gene in Cdx2-expressing epithelial 
183 
cells, i.e. in virtually all colonic lineages. Of note, in this model, tumor onset requires the acquisition of 
184 
a second somatic hit at the wild-type allele, leading to loss of Apc function in actively dividing cells. Six 
185 
months after tamoxifen-induced generation of the first somatic hit, only one mouse of the AIN76A-fed 
186 
group developed a single hyperplastic lesion, while multiple polypoid and hyperplastic lesions all mice 
187 
fed the NWD1 diet (Fig. 3F and Supplementary Fig. 3G). Notably, 9 months post induction mice 
188 
maintained on NWD1 developed invasive colorectal adenocarcinomas, while we detected no malignant 
189 
tumors in the AIN76A group (Fig. 3G and H). 
190 
These findings suggest that, despite the NWD1-mediated suppression of Lgr5+ ISCs, tumor formation 
191 
is enhanced, most likely through the expansion or activation of alternative diet-induced stem cell 
192 
populations, such as c-Kit⁺ deep crypt secretory (DCS) cells. Notably, early lesions of 6 months NWD1 
193 
fed mice contained Ki67⁺/c-Kit⁺ double-positive cells (Fig. 3I), indicating that DCS cells may contribute 
194 
to the increased tumor burden observed in NWD1-fed animals.

Discussion 
373 
In homeostasis, the intestinal epithelium is sustained by rapidly cycling Lgr5⁺ ISCs. Yet it exhibits 
374 
remarkable plasticity through recruitment of facultative stem cell populations upon inflammation and 
375 
injury3,40. While such regenerative programs have been mainly characterized in acute tissue damage, 
376 
whether chronic environmental exposures engage similar mechanisms is yet unclear. Here, we show 
377 
that a rodent diet mimicking the composition of Western-style diet in man is pro-inflammatory, 
378 
underlies specific microbiome alterations, and ultimately results in the rapid and reversible 
379 
reprogramming of the intestinal stem cell niche by suppressing canonical Lgr5⁺ ISCs while activating 
380 
alternative stem cell states derived from secretory lineages.  
381 
A central insight of our study is the uncoupling of canonical ISC activity from overall epithelial stemness 
382 
and tumorigenesis in the context of Western dietary habits.

388

389 
However, we believe that the trade-off of this adaptive response is a reason why Western-style dietary 
390 
habits represents the main etiologic risk factor for colon cancer. The novel stem-like cells originating 
391

392

393

394

395

396 
therefore support a model where pro-inflammatory nutrients expand the pool of stem-like cell targets 
397 
for mutations, thus providing a mechanistic link between diet and CRC risk. Accordingly, NWD1 
398

399

400

401

402

403 
antibacterial immune responses. Eradication of the microbiome by means of antibiotics largely 
404

405 
of epithelial changes. These observations are consistent with prior findings demonstrating that 
406

407 
diet reshapes epithelial homeostasis through inflammation and microbiome modification.

409 
as a key effector linking gut microbes to stem cell plasticity. ETBF and its toxin BFT, suppress Lgr5⁺ ISCs 
410

411

412

413

414

415 
findings suggest that ETBF contributes to colorectal cancer risk not only by promoting chronic 
416 
inflammation but also by directly regulating stem cell identity and epithelial plasticity. The ETBF-derived 
417 
toxin BFT is known to disrupt epithelial barrier integrity through cleavage of the E-cadherin 
418

419 
pathways in lineage-committed, mutation-prone PCs and DCS cells, ETBF expands the pool of tumor-
420

421 
carcinogenesis without directly inducing a distinct bacterial mutational signature48. 
422

423 
a normal diet rapidly rescues Lgr5⁺ ISC activity and suppresses c-Kit⁺-driven stemness, indicating that 
424 
epithelial reprogramming is dynamically maintained and dependent on continuous environmental 
425 
input. This aligns with emerging evidence according to which ISC identity is highly plastic and 
426 
responsive to niche-derived signals40, and suggests that dietary and microbiome-targeted interventions 
427 
may have therapeutic potential even after prolonged exposure. 
428

429

430

431

814

815

816
