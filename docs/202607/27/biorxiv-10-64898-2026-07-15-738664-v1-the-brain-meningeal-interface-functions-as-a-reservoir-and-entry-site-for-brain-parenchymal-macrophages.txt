SUMMARY 
27 
Microglia are regarded as self-maintaining brain parenchymal macrophages without contribution 
28 
from adult hematopoiesis. Nevertheless, peripheral macrophage engraftment into the brain has 
29 
been reported, but the biological variables governing central nervous system (CNS) macrophage 
30 
niche access remain unclear. We show that CNS macrophage engraftment is determined by the 
31 
balance between tissue-resident macrophage (TRM) self-renewal and the temporal alignment of 
32 
niche opening with the availability of engraftment-competent cells and proximity to the vacated 
33 
niche, rather than prolonged niche vacancy. Fate mapping revealed that monocytes entering the 
34 
subdural space undergo border-associated macrophage (BAM)-like differentiation, clonal 
35 
expansion, and transpial migration into the parenchyma, whereas mature BAMs directly 
36 
repopulate selectively vacated parenchymal niches. We further identify a parenchymal 
37 
macrophage population with a peripheral BAM-like transcriptional program in aged and 
38 
neurodegenerative human brains, challenging the dogma of MG-exclusivity. Together, these 
39 
findings establish a predictive framework for interpreting macrophage maintenance and 
40 
replacement and for developing macrophage-based therapies. 
41

INTRODUCTION 
46 
The CNS contains anatomically distinct macrophage niches populated by TRMs1–4. Among these, 
47 
microglia (MG) represent the sole macrophage population within the brain parenchyma, whereas 
48 
BAMs reside at CNS interfaces, including the leptomeninges (LM), perivascular (PV) spaces 
49 
(subsummarized as subdural BAMs; sdBAMs), the dura mater (duBAMs), and the choroid plexus 
50 
(CP; cpBAMs). One of the tenets of CNS immune privilege is the restricted accessibility of the 
51 
brain parenchyma to peripheral immune cells. Hence, MG and sdBAMs are maintained 
52 
predominantly through local self-renewal5,6 without contribution from circulating monocytes under 
53 
homeostasis7. While all CNS macrophage compartments are initially seeded by yolk sac–derived 
54 
progenitors2,8–10, only MG and sdBAMs maintain a predominantly embryonic ontogeny throughout 
55 
adulthood, whereas cpBAMs and duBAMs undergo partial replacement by bone marrow (BM) 
56 
derived monocytes, resulting in a mixed ontogeny2,9,11–13.  
57 
Macrophage depletion and repopulation paradigms have become central tools to interrogate 
58 
macrophage function and maintenance. However, distinct pharmacological and genetic depletion 
59 
strategies produce divergent repopulation outcomes, ranging from complete restoration through 
60 
residual MG self-renewal to extensive peripheral macrophage engraftment1,7,14–18. A unifying 
61 
framework defining the biological principles that govern peripheral macrophage access and CNS 
62 
niche population, and defines capable cellular sources is currently lacking. Addressing these 
63 
questions has become important as macrophage replacement therapy is transitioning from 
64 
preclinical proof-of-concept studies to clinical application19. Recent therapeutic success in patients 
65 
with CSF1R-associated leukoencephalopathy (ALSP) demonstrated that MG replacement could 
66 
alter disease progression in humans. However, these approaches rely on irradiation- and 
67 
chemotherapy-based conditioning to create permissive niches for donor-cell engraftment 14,20. As 
68 
the field reaches this translational inflection point, a mechanistic understanding of how CNS 
69 
macrophage niches are maintained, accessed, and reconstituted will be critical for the 
70 
development of safer and more effective replacement strategies.

Here, using complementary pharmacological and genetic macrophage depletion models in mice, 
72 
we systematically dissected the determinants governing CNS macrophage reconstitution and 
73 
defined physiological principles controlling peripheral macrophage engraftment into the CNS. We 
74 
identified the coincidence of niche opening and availability of engraftment-competent cells as 
75 
principal determinants governing competitive occupation of CNS macrophage niches and 
76 
identified BAMs as spatially privileged reservoirs capable of directly colonizing the MG niche 
77 
through transpial migration across CNS border interfaces. Finally, we provide evidence that these 
78 
principles extend to humans by identifying CD163+ brain-engrafting peripheral-like macrophages in 
79 
the aged and neurodegenerating human brain.

82

84

85

86

87

Exhaustion of MG self-renewal enables MDM brain engraftment. 
74 
Repeated cycles of pharmacological MG depletion have been reported to delay niche refilling48, 
75 
suggesting that the MG proliferative capacity may become limited over time. In a further study49, 
76

77

78 
We hypothesized that the delayed repopulation following iterative CSF1R-mediated depletion 
79

80 
Reanalysis of the published scRNAseq dataset49 (Fig. 3F-G, Fig. S3E for experimental outline) 
81

Discussion 
64 
The principles that determine when and how monocytes or peripheral macrophages gain access 
65 
to CNS compartments remain incompletely understood. Experimental macrophage depletion 
66 
paradigms have become important tools to investigate CNS macrophage maintenance and 
67 
function. However, apparently conflicting repopulation outcomes across macrophage depletion 
68 
strategies7,15–17 have complicated interpretation of these studies and contributed to the view that 
69 
prolonged 
niche 
availability 
dictates 
peripheral 
engraftment26. 
Here, 
we 
integrated 
70 
pharmacological and genetic macrophage depletion models to identify shared physiological 
71 
variables that govern repopulation outcomes across major CNS macrophage niches. We show 
72 
that these models are not contradictory; rather, they represent distinct perturbations of a common 
73 
biological framework centered on two key parameters and their temporal alignment: presence of 
74 
engraftment-competent cells and TRM self-renewal capacity. 
75 
Broad pharmacological CSF1Ri efficiently depletes MG but also transiently impairs CM 
76 
differentiation and engraftment, despite preserving their abundance. Rapidly self-renewing MG re-
77 
establish niche dominance and limit peripheral engraftment. In contrast, persistent impairment of 
78 
TRM self-renewal, through either irreversible genetic disruption of CSF1R signaling or iterative 
79 
depletion-repopulation cycles, exhausted yolk sac-derived TRMs (including MG, sd- and cpBAMs) 
80 
and shifted the competitive balance toward peripheral engraftment. Importantly, selective TRM 
81 
depletion demonstrated that MDM engraftment does not require prolonged niche vacancy if 
82 
engraftment-competent cells are preserved during transient niche opening. Despite partial 
83 
acquisition of canonical MG genes (P2y12, Tmem119), brain-engrafting monocytes failed to fully 
84 
adopt a bona fide MG identity (Sall127,58), potentially reflecting ontogenetically imprinted epigenetic 
85 
constraints26,54 or insufficient time within the CNS niche59. We show that brain-engrafting MDMs 
86 
are transcriptionally heterogeneous yet share a conserved core MDM transcriptional program, 
87 
which, consistent with recent reports54,60, includes acquisition of a BAM-like transcriptional profile. 
88 
We extended these findings by mapping the spatiotemporal acquisition of the BAM state by CNS-
89 
infiltrating monocytes, demonstrating that monocytes progressively differentiate through a BAM

Limitations of the study 
44 
While we demonstrate that monocyte-derived MHCIIhigh BAMs undergo transpial entry into the 
45 
brain parenchyma, selective genetic fate-mapping strategies for embryonic MHCIIlow BAMs are 
46 
currently unavailable. Consequently, because of the remarkable plasticity and rapid phenotypic 
47 
convergence of macrophages during niche adaptation, the exact relative contribution of MHCIIhigh 
48 
and MHCIIlow BAMs to microglial replacement cannot yet be definitively quantified. Furthermore, 
49 
whether similar permissive niche conditions and transpial recruitment of BAMs occur during 
50 
physiological aging or distinct pathological conditions, rather than acute experimental niche 
51 
perturbation, remains to be determined. 
52 
Finally, although our transcriptomic and histological analyses support the presence of peripheral 
53 
macrophages within the human brain, definitive lineage relationships cannot currently be 
54 
established in humans. Future studies integrating naturally occurring somatic lineage markers, 
55 
including mitochondrial DNA variants and clonal hematopoiesis associated mutations, with single-
56 
cell multiomic and spatial analyses may enable direct reconstruction of macrophage ontogeny and 
57 
replacement dynamics in humans.

14

15

Bone marrow chimeras 
18

Parabiosis 
33 
Parabiosis experiments were performed between UBCGFP mice and age- and sex-matched 
34 
(female) Sall1CreERT2/wt:Csf1rfl/fl or Sall1wt/wt:Csf1rfl/fl mice as previously described54. Briefly, mice 
35 
were anesthetized with a ketamine/xylazine (100mg/kg, 10 mg/kg) mixture and the corresponding 
36 
lateral sides were shaved and disinfected using alternating betadine and alcohol washes. A skin 
37 
incision extending from the olecranon to the knee joint was generated, and subcutaneous fascia 
38 
was carefully separated to create a free skin margin. Corresponding knee and elbow joints of 
39 
parabiont pairs were connected using absorbable 5-0 Vicryl sutures, followed by approximation 
40 
and closure of the dermis while excluding epidermal layers at the junction site. Animals received 
41 
postoperative analgesia with buprenorphine (Buprenorphine SR; 1.0 mg/kg) and antibiotic 
42 
supplementation in the drinking water. 
43 
 
44 
Calvarium bone-flap transplantation 
45 
Sall1Cret/wt:Csf1rfl/fl 
46 
7–10-week-old mice were anesthetized with a ketamine/xylazine mixture (80 mg/kg ketamine, 16 
47 
mg/kg Xylazine) and maintained at 37°C on a heating pad throughout the surgical procedure. Hair 
48 
over the head was removed using depilatory cream, and the surgical site was thoroughly 
49 
disinfected with Betadine. A midline skin incision was made to expose the parietal and interparietal 
50 
bones. A cranial window of approximately 4 × 6 mm was generated using an electric drill. During 
51 
drilling, the surgical area was carefully monitored and kept moist as needed. The cranial flap was 
52 
then gently removed by carefully detaching the underlying dura from the bone, while preserving 
53 
the integrity of the dura mater, brain parenchyma, and sinus structures. 
54 
Following craniotomy, a sex- and age-mismatched fluorescent donor mouse, typically UBC-GFP, 
55 
was euthanized. A donor calvarial flap of comparable size and from the corresponding skull region 
56 
was prepared. The donor dura was removed from the bone flap, and the flap was trimmed as 
57 
needed to match the recipient craniotomy site. The donor bone flap was then positioned over the 
58 
cranial window and fixed in place using dental cement. Care was taken to prevent cement leakage 
59 
between the fracture margins to allow proper bone apposition and healing. After the cement had 
60 
hardened and the transplanted flap was stably immobilized, the skin was re-approximated and 
61 
sutured. 
62 
Postoperative care included analgesic treatment 
without antibiotic administration. No 
63 
postoperative infections were observed. Following surgery, mice were housed individually during 
64 
the recovery period.

66 
immunofluorescence analysis, at least twenty 40-µm-thick sagittal brain sections within the 
67 
transplantation area were analyzed per mouse.
