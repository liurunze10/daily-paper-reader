Abstract 22 

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) released into the cytosol activates innate immune signaling and 23 

promotes inflammation, yet its role in macrophages following sterile tissue injury remains poorly 24 

understood. Here, we show that cardiac macrophages from both patients and mice with myocardial 25 

infarction (MI) exhibit increased mitochondrial biogenesis, mitochondrial content, membrane 26 

potential, and expression of mitochondrial nucleases that facilitate mtDNA release. Consistently, 27 

macrophage-specific silencing of genes regulating mitochondrial biogenesis or mtDNA processing 28 

attenuated adverse cardiac remodeling after MI. Unexpectedly, despite the role of mtDNA in 29 

activating the cGAS –STING pathway, myeloid deletion or macrophage-specific silencing 30 

of Sting or cGas exacerbated ventricular dilation, fibrosis, and contractile dysfunction following 31 

MI. Single-cell transcriptomic and cell communication analyses identified amyloid precursor 32 

protein (APP) as a key downstream effector of STING in cardiac macrophages. Macrophage-33 

specific in vivo App silencing rescued the detrimental effects of myeloid Sting deficiency, 34 

establishing APP as a critical mediator of adverse remodeling. Mechanistically, STING interacted 35 

with the transcriptional repressor MZF1, promoted its nuclear localization, facilitated its binding 36 

to the App promoter, and suppressed App transcription to restrain adverse cardiac remodeling. 37 

Together, our findings uncover an unexpected cardioprotective function of myeloid STING and 38 

identify the STING–MZF1–APP axis as a previously unrecognized mechanism governing cardiac 39 

repair after myocardial infarction. 40

Introduction 42 

The ability of macrophages to exert a wide range of functions ranging from tissue homeostasis to 43 

inflammatory response stems from their phenotypic plasticity and ability to adopt varied activation 44 

states based on extrinsic cues 1. Different macrophage polarization states are associated with unique 45 

metabolic profiles and shift in mitochondrial metabolism, structure, and function 2. In addition to 46 

modulating macrophage polarization and function, damaged mitochondria and elevated ROS 47 

levels result in the release of mtDNA in the cytosol, which acts as a damage associated molecular 48 

pattern (DAMP), mediating the type I interferon response via binding to cyclic GMP-AMP 49 

synthase (cGAS) and activating STimulator of Interferon Genes (STING) 3,4 .50 

51 

The cGAS-STING pathway is a crucial component of the innate immune system and serves to 52 

detect double stranded DNA (dsDNA) in the cytoplasm, independent of other DNA sensing 53 

pathways 4,5 . cGAS binds to dsDNA and generates cyclic guanosine monophosphate adenosine 54 

monophosphate (cGAMP), a second messenger that activates STING, which in turn initiates the 55 

transcription of type-1 interferons 3,4,6 . STING is expressed in a wide range of immune cell types 56 

- macrophages, dendritic cells, lymphocytes, and in non-immune cells like endothelial and 57 

epithelial cells 7,8,9 . At steady state, STING localizes to the ER. Upon cGAMP binding, STING 58 

forms a complex with TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1) that translocates to the perinuclear region 59 

and activates transcription factors IRF3 and NF-kB by phosphorylation, resulting in the stimulation 60 

of type I interferon gene expression and cytokine production 9.61

Pathological release of mtDNA into the cytosol via the mPTP and VDAC-dependent channels 66 

activates the cGAS-STING pathway and fuels inflammation 13,14 whereas mitophagy by 67

autoimmune disease, cancer, and diabetes 20 . STING agonists, when used in combination with 71

73 

In cardiovascular diseases, chronic activation of STING has been associated with NF-kB and 74 

NLRP3 inflammasome signaling, leading to the production of IL-1B in diabetic cardiomyopathy 75 

and following ischemia reperfusion injury 23-26 . Moreover, STING has been shown to aggravate 76 

cardiac remodeling after MI 27,28 and viral myocarditis 29 . Global knockdown or inhibition of 77 

STING improved cardiac function and ameliorated cardiac hypertrophy in a murine model of 78 

DCM and decreased inflammation and apoptosis in cultured cardiomyocytes 23,24 . In contrast to 79 

these studies revealing the deleterious functions of STING in cardiovascular study, a recent study 80 

reported a protective function of STING in a mouse model of transverse aortic constriction 30 .81 

These contrasting findings of STING functions in cardiovascular disease could be attributed to the 82 

lack of cell-specific deletion or targeting of this nucleic acid sensor. In support of this statement, 83 

STING manifests opposing functions in cancer depending on cell type by which it is expressed 84

The main goals of this study are two-fold: A) To understand how mitochondrial DNA synthesis in 88 

cardiac macrophages impacts STING and post-MI inflammation and B) To delineate the function 89 

of myeloid STING in cardiac remodeling following MI. We demonstrate that cardiac macrophages 90 

in the infarct heighten mitochondrial biogenesis and the expression of the proteins involved in this 91 

process, mtDNA release into the cytosol, and the expression of mitochondrial nucleases, which 92 

facilitate cleavage of mtDNA and its egress from mitochondria. Ablation of the genes driving 93 

mitochondrial biogenesis, such as Pgc1a and Polg , and mitochondrial nucleases, such as Fen1 and 94 

Apex2 , in macrophages subsided inflammation and cardiac fibrosis and remodeling after MI. These 95 

data indicate that the cGAS-STING pathway, which is activated in response to oxidized and 96 

fragmented mtDNA, is detrimental in post-MI healing of the heart. Surprisingly, genetic deletion 97 

and silencing of Sting and cGas in macrophages resulted in adverse cardiac remodeling and 98 

exaggerated fibrosis by inflating the production of APP after MI. Mechanistically, STING bound 99 

to the suppressor MZF1, facilitating its nuclear translocation. MZF1 bound to the App promoter, 100 

resulting in suppressed transcription of this gene. 101

Cardiac macrophages after MI display increased mitochondrial volume and membrane 104 

potential 105 

Oxidized and fragmented mitochondrial DNA exacerbates inflammation and contributes to disease 106 

pathogenesis 32-35 . However, how mitochondrial biogenesis affects inflammation after an acute 107 

sterile injury, such as MI, is not known. In this study, we used a mouse model of MI and cardiac 108 

specimens from patients with a history of MI and ischemic cardiomyopathy to characterize 109 

mitochondria in cardiac macrophages, which are crucial in propagating inflammation and tissue 110 

remodeling. We observed that mitochondrial volume as assessed by TOM20 immunostaining and 111 

oxidative DNA damage indicated by 8-OHdG staining were elevated in cardiac macrophages of 112 

the patients with MI compared to healthy controls (Figures 1A-1B ). Similarly, cardiac 113 

macrophages from mice with MI had elevated TOM20 and 8-OHdG immunostaining, indicating 114 

greater mitochondrial volume and increased oxidative DNA damage, respectively ( Figures 1C-115 

1D ). Cardiac macrophages but not monocytes in the infarcts exhibited greater mitochondrial 116 

volume ( Figure 1E ) and mitochondrial membrane potential ( Figure 1F ). With the exception of 117 

bone marrow neutrophils and splenic monocytes, mitochondrial volume and membrane potential 118 

were unaltered after MI in most leukocytes and myeloid cell populations in the bone marrow and 119 

spleen ( Figure S1A-S1B ). Increased mitochondrial volume is primarily attributed to augmented 120 

mitochondrial biogenesis associated with mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) synthesis. To examine if 121 

exaggerated inflammation, which is associated with post-MI sequelae, augments mtDNA 122 

synthesis, we treated bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) with oxidized LDL (oxLDL). 123 

This treatment significantly augmented mitochondrial DNA content, assessed by D-loop/Tert ratio 124 

(Figure 1G ). In line with this observation, oxLDL treatment upregulated the expression of several 125

Ischemic heart disease caused by myocardial infarction (MI) is among the leading causes of 312 

morbidity and mortality across the world 48 . Inflammatory pathways play a major role in healing, 313 

remodeling, and the progression of heart failure after MI 49,50 . Macrophages comprise one of the 314 

largest immune cell populations in the heart and play a wide range of roles from cardiac 315 

homeostasis to chronic inflammation and adverse remodeling in heart failure (HF) 51,52 via 316 

secretion of inflammatory cytokines and modulating ECM production 53-55 . Although STING has 317 

been shown to propagate inflammation, our data demonstrate that macrophage STING is beneficial 318 

after MI. Specifically, we demonstrate that STING suppresses APP production by translocating 319 

MZF1 into the nucleus, suppressing fibrosis and cardiac remodeling. 320 

321 

We also show that uncontrolled synthesis of mitochondrial DNA, which is known to stimulate the 322 

STING pathway, in infarct macrophages contributes to exacerbated inflammation and worse 323 

outcome after MI. Our data reveal that macrophages exposed to an inflammatory stimulus or 324 

cardiac macrophages in ischemic hearts have greater levels of oxidative DNA damage and 325 

augmented mtDNA release into the cytoplasm. This is consistent with an expanding body of 326 

literature on mitochondrial DNA as a proinflammatory stimulus and the role of mitochondrial 327 

dynamics in modulating macrophage function 2,24,33 . Damaged mtDNA is cleaved by 328 

mitochondrial nucleases, such as FEN1 and APEX2, expediting mtDNA release into the cytoplasm 329 

and fueling inflammation 56,57 . In line with these findings, our study shows attenuated inflammation 330 

and improved cardiac function when these nucleases were silenced in macrophages. It has been 331 

shown that mitochondrial DNA, once in the cytosol, stimulates cGAS-STING signaling and 332
