Abstract 19 

Background: Biochemical, biophysical and structural characterisation of isozymes from the 20 

ubiquitous family of iron - or manganese -dependent superoxide dismutases (SodFMs) requires 21 

the purification of high -quality preparations of recombinant enzymes. Determination of their key 22 

biochemical parameter, their catalytic metal -preference, requires the comparison of the catalytic 23 

turnover of samples loaded exclusively with iron versus samples loaded exclusively with 24 

manganese. Both of these aims are inhibited by the potential contamination of recombinant 25 

preparations of SodFMs, prepared by heterologous overexpression inside Escherichia coli cells, 26 

by even low levels of endogenous SodFMs from the host, both of which show very high turnover 27 

with either manganese ( E. coli MnSOD) or iron (FeSOD). To overcome this problem, we created 28 

a strain of E. coli lacking the endogenous SodFMs. Here, we characterised this E. coli BL21 (DE3) 29 

sodA sodB strain, determining the physiological effects of SodFM deletion and demonstrating 30 

its utility for producing recombinant SodFMs for in vitro characterisation and use. 31 

Results: Genomic analysis verified the targeted gene deletions, without off -target effects. Growth, 32 

expression, elemental analysis, and proteomic data confirmed a lack of physiological defects of 33 

the strain except for a known inability to grow on glucose, which is overcome by heterologous 34 

SodFM expression. We demonstrate the utility of the strain for the efficient production of diverse 35 

recombinant SodFMs, including highly divergent, understudied isozymes, including the ability to 36 

precisely control the metal -loading of the heterologously expressed protein. 37 

Conclusions: The E. coli strain described herein is a useful microbial cell factory for production 38 

of recombinant SodFMs, which should find widespread utility as expression host of choi ce, 39 

enabling more efficient production of protein for studies of the biochemical, biophysical and 40 

structural properties of this remarkable family of metalloenzymes. 41

Background 42 

Since t he first expression of a recombinant protein inside cells of Escherichia coli was reported in 43 

1977 [1] and t he first recombinant therapeutic , human insulin, in 1979 [2] , demand for recombinant 44 

protein production for academic study and for commercial, medical or industrial applications has 45 

grown dramatically. Although alternatives are available, E. coli remain s the cell factory of choice 46 

for producing recombinant proteins [3,4] . It offers several advantages for recombinant protein 47 

synthesis. It has been extensively studied, with well -characterised physiology and genetics and 48 

easy -to -use tools available [5 –10] . These include inducible expression vectors [6,7,11] and the 49 

host strain, BL21 (DE3) . This strain lacks Lon and OmpT proteases, limit ing degradation of foreign 50 

protein, and incorporates RNA polymerase from T7 bacteriophage in its genome [12] , enabl ing it 51 

to express transcripts from the strong T7 promoter [5] .52 

Derivatives of E. coli BL21 (DE3) ha ve been constructed to improve its production of 53 

proteins for specific applications. Strains were created that are optimised for production of 54 

proteins containing a poly -histidine tag to facilitate their purification . Th es e strains have mutations 55 

in five endogenous E. coli genes whose product s commonly contaminate protein preparations 56 

after purifi cation by immobilised metal affinity chromatography (IMAC), reducing their affinity for 57 

IMAC or enabling a pre -clearance step using chitin [13] . Other modified BL21 strains eliminate 58 

contamination of recombinant protein preparations by the chaperone DnaK [14] , improve 59 

expression of outer membrane proteins [15] , facilitate site -specific incorporation of non -natural or 60 

isotopically labelled amino acids [16,17] , produc e glycosylated eukaryotic proteins [18] , or 61 

improve production of specific metabolites such as N‑acetyl ‑D‑neuraminic acid (Neu5Ac) [19] ,62 

lycopene [8] , or phenylpyruvic acid [20] .63 

For the last decade, we have been studying the iron - or manganese -dependent 64 

superoxide dismutase (SOD) family of metallo enzymes (SodFM) [21 –27] . SODs are the primary 65 

cellular defence against superoxide radicals , key antioxidant enzymes that maintain redox 66

disease [28] . The SodFMs are the most common and most widely distributed class of SODs, 68 

present in diverse organisms including some obligate anaerobes [21] . SodFMs exhibit a spectrum 69 

of metal specificities, ranging from highly manganese -preferring to highly iron -preferring enzymes 70 

[21,29,30] . Between these extremes are isozymes that can catalyse their reaction with either 71

metalloenzymes [32] . Yet, all SodFMs share identical folds and remarkably similar active site 73

distinct catalytic metal -preferences [22,23,28,35] . The d etailed biochemical, biophysical and 75 

structural characterisation of SodFMs needed to answer such questions requires purified 76 

preparations of recombinant enzymes, free of contamination by the endogenous SodFMs that are 77 

expressed by the host cell. E. coli possesses two SodFMs, strongly manganese -preferring SodA 78 

and iron -preferring SodB [36 –38] , which are highly active enzymes with their cognate metal 79 

cofactor [21,29,30] . This makes even very low levels of their contamination in recombinant 80 

preparations of heterologous SodFMs highly problematic for quantifying the catalytic turnover of 81 

an isozyme with each metal, especially as highly metal -preferring isozymes can have very low 82 

catalytic efficiency when loaded with the ‘wrong’ metal cofactor [21,23,29,30] .83 

To overcome this bottleneck, we created a strain of E. coli BL21 (DE3) in which both genes 84 

encoding SodFMs were deleted from the genome [21] . The strain grows normally in aerobic 85 

culture when used for recombinant protein expression [21,39] . The BL21 (DE3) sodA sodB 86 

strain enables large -scale preparation of recombinant SodFMs, free of contamination by native 87 

E. coli SodFMs , and with specific metal -loading despite the BL21 parental strain being known to 88 

have defects in metal homeostasis [40] . Using this strain, we developed medium throughput in -89 

gel assays to assess the catalytic metal -preference of SodFM isozymes, which enabl ed a study 90 

of the evolution of the SodFM family [21] . Here, we have characterised this E. coli mutant strain, 91 

indicated its lack of physiological growth defects under standard culture conditions, and 92

The g enome of the BL21 -derived host strain show s no off -target mutations 230 

The E. coli BL21 strain is known to be vulnerable to genetic mutations, show ing an increased 231 

mutation rate relative to other Type B E. coli [47] . The Lambda Red method for genetic 232 

modification of the E. coli genome ha s also been suggested to be vulnerable to secondary 233 

mutations due to the use of low and high temperature selection steps [48,49] . Further, d elet ing 234 

genes encoding superoxide dismutase s, which are involved in cellular defence against 235 

exogenous oxidative stress , also make s the host cell vulnerable to secondary mutations [50] . For 236 

these reasons, we began our study of the BL21 sodA sodB strain by sequencing its genome to 237 

identify any mutations that were present in addition to the targeted deletions .238 

We used Illumina sequencing to compare the genome sequences of both the sodA sodB 239 

strain and the parental BL21 (DE3) strain from which it was made (herein ‘wild type’ , WT ) to the 240 

reference genome for E. coli BL21 (DE3) [51] . The WT strain showed no deviations from that of 241

superoxide -induced metabolic block [39,55] .264

Discussion 431 

We previously constructed a strain of E. coli BL21 for the purpose of creating a microbial cell 432 

factory for the efficient synthesis of recombinant heterologous superoxide dismutase enzymes 433 

free of contamination by its endogenous cytosolic SodFM isozymes . The native E. coli SodFMs 434 

exhibit very high metal -preference of their catalysis, with SodA being active only with manganese 435 

and SodB only with iron, each with very high catalytic turnover [30,65] . Therefore , even low levels 436 

of contamination by the E. coli SodFMs can contribute to the measured SOD activity of 437 

recombinant protein preparations, especially when biochemically characterising an isozyme with 438

isozyme when wrongly loaded with iron and vice versa ). Additionally, while the expression of the 440 

iron -dependent E. coli SOD, SodB, is largely constitutive, the manganese -dependent E. coli SOD, 441 

SodA, is induced under stress conditions [66] . Thus, it is inevitably unclear to what extent each of 442 

these isozymes will contribute through their contamination to a given extract, interfering not only 443 

with the measurement of catalytic activity but also to elemental analysis to determine the metal 444 

content of recombinant protein preparations of heterologously expressed SodFMs. Our E. coli 445 

BL21 sodA sodB strain was designed to overcome this contamination problem, enabl ing us to 446 

produce large quantities of the purified recombinant enzymes for detailed biochemical and 447 

biophysical characterisation [21,22] . It also create d a system for screening the activity of SodFMs 448 

in a higher throughput manner, avoiding the need for full purification of target isozymes in each 449 

metal form by assaying the recombinant enzyme direct from crude bacterial lysates, either in 450 

solution or in gel activity assays. The latter provided us with a tool that enabled us to survey a 451 

diverse set of 60 SodFM isozymes from across the entire phylogenetic tree, as well as more than 452 

100 different mutated variants of those isozymes, to determine the evolutionary history of this 453 

widely distributed metalloenzyme family [21] .454 

In this study, we sought to characterise this mutant strain of E. coli BL21 to identify any 455 

physiological defects which it might exhibit and to validate it as a new tool for use in the study of 456 

the remarkable SodFM family of metalloenzymes. Based on prior observations made with a 457 

mutated strain of Type A E. coli , strain K -12, we anticipated that we would observe physiological 458

sodA sodB strain failed to grow in medium supplemented with glucose [39] , a phenotype 460 

proposed to result from superoxide -mediated damage to Fe -S clusters in essential enzymes of 461 

carbon metabolism [67] . Remarkably, this phenotype was not observed in our BL21 strain , which 462 

grew well in either LB or M9 minimal medium supplemented with glucose. Notably, w e found no 463

Conclusions 496 

Here, we have characterised the physiology and utility of an E. coli BL21 sodA sodB strain for 497 

production of recombinant SodFM proteins for in vitro studies. Genomic analysis verified the 498 

targeted gene deletions, without significant off -target effects. Growth, expression, elemental 499 

analysis, and proteomic data confirmed a lack of physiological defects of the mutant strain , except 500 

for a known inability to grow on glucose, which we have demonstrated can be overcome by 501 

heterologous expression of a SodFM isozyme . We demonstrate the strain can efficient ly produc e502 

diverse recombinant SodFMs, including precise control of metal -loading of the heterologously 503 

expressed protein. The E. coli strain is thus a useful microbial cell factory for production of 504 

recombinant SodFMs, which should find widespread utility as expression host of choise, enabling 505 

improved production of protein for studies of the biochemical, biophysical and structural properties 506 

of this remarkable family of metalloenzymes. 507
