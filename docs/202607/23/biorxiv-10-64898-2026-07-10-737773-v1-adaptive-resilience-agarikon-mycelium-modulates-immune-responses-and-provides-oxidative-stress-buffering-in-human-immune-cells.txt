Abstract 8

Agarikon ( Fomitopsis officinalis , syn. Laricifomes officinalis ) is a fungus with millennia of 9

traditional use across many cultures with modern research supporting the antimicrobial, antiviral, 10 

anticancer, antioxidant , and immune -modulating properties of both mycelium and fruit body . Due to 11 

the slow growth and old -growth forest habitat of agarikon fruit bodies, its mycelium represents an 12 

easily cultivated immunomodulating and stress buffering preparation , underscored by recent clinical 13 

trials of a blend of agarikon and Trametes versicolor mycelium. We investigated the transcriptomic 14 

effect s of agarikon mycelium in human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) under both 15 

basal and LPS -stimulated conditions , alongside evaluations of antioxidant , iron chelating , and kinase 16 

binding activity . Two agarikon fractions were also assessed for effects on cell viability and 17 

proliferation and induction of select cytokine targets. Under basal conditions, agarikon mycelium 18 

selectively engaged the innate immune response through IL -1 and NF -κB axes , balanced by 19 

increases in anti -inflammatory mediators such as IL -1RA and decreases in toll -like receptor 20 

transcripts . Under LPS -stimulated conditions, this innate immune response was modified, w ith 21 

measured increases in immune effectors (including TLR5 ) observed in response to induced stress ,22 

along side accompanying transcript decreases in cytokine pathways that can overstimulat e the 23 

immune system. Overall, agarikon mycelium demonstrated a coordinated, context -dependent 24 

immune response profile, supporting its stress -buffering and immune -modulating potential and 25 

warranting continued clinical validation. 26 

1 Introduction 27 

Agarikon ( Fomitopsis officinalis , syn. Laricifomes officinalis ) is a medicinal mushroom with 28 

millennia of traditional use in many cultures , with preparations included in ancient pharmacopoeia s29 

such as Dioscorides’ De Materia Medica (1, 2, 3 ). Modern research has supported the antimicrobial, 30 

antiviral, anticancer, immunomodulatory , and antioxidant properties of individual components and 31 

whole extracts of agarikon mycelium ( 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12) and fruit body (4, 13, 14, 15) .32 

The antioxidant and anticancer effects of agarikon treatment are partially enacted by its distinct 33 

immunomodulatory capacities and mediated by the nuclear factor kappa -light -chain -enhancer of 34

Fungi can produce compounds unique to developmental stage , substrate, and extract type , which 37 

influence the immunological effects of fungal treatments ( 16, 17 ). For instance, two chlorinated 38 

coumarins have been isolated from agarikon mycelium, with structure -dependent activity against 39 

Mycobacterium tuberculosis , expanding the reported bioactive metabolite space of this species 40 

beyond its well -studied terpenoid fraction ( 6). Additionally, i nvestigations of the antiviral activity of 41 

agarikon have thus far centered on the action of mycelial fractions , in particular against pox viruses 42 

(12 ) and influenza A ( 18 ). A specific agarikon fruit body fraction was found to act as a toll -like 43 

receptor (TLR) agonist by activating NF -κB and the innate immune system via TLR2 and TLR4 ,44 

and bolstered the adaptive immune system by binding to and preventing the interaction of 45 

programmed cell death protein 1 ( PD -1) and its l igand 1 (PD -L1 ) and vascular endothelial growth 46 

factor (VEGF ) and its receptor ( VEGFR -2), which inhibits tumor angiogenesis ( 14, 15 ). A broader 47 

fraction of agarikon fruit body was also observed to engage this pathway by upregulating NF -κB 48 

inhibitor alpha (NFKBIA ), NF -κB subunit 1 (NF KB 1), and transcription factor p65 (RELA ) protein 49 

products in HepG2 cancer cells as part of its anticancer effect ( 13 ). However , s ome preclinical 50 

studies suggest that high concentration polysaccharide extracts from fruit bodies of certain species ,51 

including Ganoderma lucidum and Hericium erinaceus , may reduce viability (19 ) or induce stress 52 

responses (17 ) in human immune cells . Collectively, t hese findings suggest that immunological 53 

activity elicited by fungal -derived preparations is highly dependent on biological source and 54 

extraction method, necessitating functional evaluation across tissue types and solvent -based extracts 55 

to characterize context -dependent immune responses in vitro .56 

Because agarikon fruit bodies are slow growing and largely limited to old -growth forest habitats, 57 

contributing to its Endangered status on the IUCN Red List of Threatened Species, aseptically 58 

cultured mycelium has emerge d as a controlled and scalable method for accessing its bioactive 59 

potential without reliance on wild harvest ( 11, 20 ). Accordingly , one cryopreservation program ha s60 

established a repositor y containing at least 87 genetically distinct agarikon strains, supporting long -61 

term conservation of its genetic diversity (21 ). Recently, an annotated nuclear genome of agarikon 62 

ha s been made publicly available for the first time , providing foundational resources for 63 

understanding its genetic architecture and biosynthetic potential, and reflecting its status as a 64 

phylogenetically distinct polypore and sole member of the family Laricifomitaceae (22 ). Building on 65 

the genetic, ecological, and functional rationale for investigating agarikon mycelium, t his study 66 

evaluated its potential as a cytocompatible and effective immun e- and oxidative stress -modulating 67 

treatment in human cells to assess its suitability for medicinal use. 68

2.2 Cell Viability and Proliferation Testing 87 

2.2.1 MTT Evaluation of Cell Viability and Cytotoxicity 88 

Before culturing PBMCs for RNA extraction and additional assays, MTT assays (ATCC, 30 -1010K) 89 

were performed to evaluate cell viability (as based on metabolic activity) following treatment with 90 

agarikon extract preparations. MTT assays were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s 91 

kit instructions, but with a decrease in volume from 110 μl to 55 μl reactions to conduct the assay in 92 

a 384 -well plate, with a minimum of n = 4 replicate wells per treatment concentration. Standard 93 

human PBMCs (ATCC, PCS -800 -011) were thawed, centrifuged, and washed with 37 °C RPMI 94 

1640 media to remove DMSO used for cryopreservation purposes. Cells were incubated in 15 mL of 95 

warmed RPMI 1640 (10% FBS, heat inactivated, Sigma -Aldrich, F1051 -100ML) overnight in a T75 96 

flask at 37 °C in a 5% CO 2 atmosphere to recover from the freeze/thaw process. Cell counts were 97 

determined via hemocytometer after staining with 0.4% trypan blue diluted in PBS. Following a 24 98 

h growth phase within the flask, cells were transferred to a 96 -well plate (2 × 10⁶ cells per well). 99 

Treatments were introduced approximately 24 h after seeding, and plates were incubated for 18 -24 h 100 

post -treatment before addition of the MTT reagent and further incubating at 37 °C in a 5% CO 2101 

atmosphere for 4 -24 h to facilitate formazan crystal formation. Detergent was then added for 4 -24 h 102 

until crystals were completely solubilized. Absorbance readings were recorded at 570 and 660 nm, 103 

and sample values were calculated by subtracting values at 660 nm from values at 570 nm. Percent 104 

cell viability was evaluated relative to either the PBS or DMSO vehicle controls, according to each 105 

sample’s vehicle solvent. 106 

2.2. 2 Fluorometric Evaluation of Cell Proliferation 107 

Cells were also evaluated for cellular proliferation levels under both basal and LPS inflammatory 108 

conditions via fluorometric assessment (Invitrogen, C35006) to more thoroughly explore the 109 

cytocompatibility of agarikon mycelium carried in a variety of solvents in human cell culture. 110 

Standard human PBMCs were cultured and treated as described for MTT assay and then centrifuged 111 

at 100 x g for 6 min. Cell media was aspirated and frozen at –20 °C for use in ELISA quantification .112 

A working solution of 1X HBSS buffer containing CyQUANT NF dye reagent (1:500 dilution) was 113 

prepared in accordance with the nonadherent cell protocol provided . Cells were washed with PBS 114 

and aspirated before 100 μL of combined 1X HBSS buffer and dye reagent was added to each well .115 

Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 60 min before being read fluorometrically (485 nm excitation, 116 

528 emission), and cell proliferation values were calculated as percentages relative to the DMSO 117 

vehicle control. 118

2.3 PBMC Culture for mRNA -Sequencing 126 

mRNA -sequencing (RNA -Seq) was used to assess global gene expression changes in PBMCs 127 

following agarikon treatment under basal and LPS -stimulated conditions. After an initial 24 h 128 

incubation in 12 -well plates , PBMCs were treated and incubated for a further 24 h , including 100 129 

ng/mL LPS for inflammatory condition samples. For harvest, cells and media were aspirated, 130 

pipetted into sterile 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tubes, washed with PBS, and aspirated again before 131 

freezing cell pellets in liquid nitrogen prior to storage at –80 °C. 132 

Before starting RNA extraction, surfaces and tools were cleaned with RNaseZap (ThermoFisher, 133 

AM9780). Using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, 74104), 350 μL of RLT buffer was added to each 134 

microcentrifuge tube after flicking to loosen the cell pellet. Cells were lysed by vortexing for 1 min, 135 

and the supernatant was transferred to a new sterile 1.5 mL microcentrifuge tube. The 136 

manufacturer’s recommended extraction procedure for mammalian cells was followed, including the 137 

optional step of placing columns into new collection tubes and centrifuging for 1 min at 13,000 x g138 

immediately before elution. 30 μL of RNase -free water was used for elution, and columns were 139 

incubated at room temperature for 1 min before proceeding. RNA quality (A260/280 ratios) and 140 

concentrations of samples were determined spectrophotometrically prior to DNase digestion, where 141 

30 μL of eluted RNA was added to 3 μL of reaction buffer and 3 μL of DNase I (ThermoFisher, 142 

EN0521). Samples were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min before 3 μL of 50 mM EDTA was added, 143 

then incubated at 65 °C for 10 min. All samples were stored at –80 °C and shipped to Novogene 144 

Corporation Inc. (Sacramento, California) on dry ice for RNA -Seq. 145 

2.4 RNA -Seq and Analysis 146 

Extracted samples proceeded to sequencing if they had a total RNA quantity of ≥100 ng and a 147 

spectrophotometric A260/280 ratio of 1.95 or greater. Novogene’s mRNA -Seq service was used, 148 

performed on an Illumina NovaSeq X -Plus platform with a paired -end read length of 150 bp. At 149 

least 20 million read pairs per sample were generated and aligned to the reference genome Homo 150 

sapiens GRCh38 ( hg38) ; RNA -Seq data are summarized in Supplementary Tables S1 and S2. 151 

NovoMagic (Novogene’s RNA -Seq platform) was used to generate differentially expressed gene 152 

(DEG) lists and dot plots summarizing KEGG and Reactome pathways. DESeq2 analysis, applying 153 

the Benjamini -Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction with an adjusted p-value ( padj) 154 

cutoff of ≤ 0.05, was used to identify significant DEG and pathway results (24). PCAGO was used 155 

to assess variability via principal component analysis (PCA), RStudio 202 6.05 .0 (R 4 .6.0 ) was used 156 

to create bar and line graphs, volcano plots, and heatmaps, and KEGG Mapper – Color was used to 157 

generate and color code KEGG plots. 158

Investigation of the mitogen -activated protein kinase (MAPK ) binding activity of agarikon 170

reported as percent control values, where lower values indicate stronger kinase binding interactions 177

2.7 DPPH Assay s179 

DPPH radical scavenging assay s were employed to assess general antioxidant potential of agarikon 180 

and provide functional corroboration of the stress - and redox -related pathways explored via 181 

transcript omics . A 100 μg/mL DPPH reagent (Sigma -Aldrich, 300267) stock was prepared in 9 9%182 

ethanol and calibrated by adding 60 μL of DPPH stock to 40 μL of sterile water in a single well of a 183 

384 -well plate and ensuring absorbance values fell between 1.0 and 1.1 when read 184 

spectrophotometrically at 517 nm. DPPH reagent was included as a maximal signal control, and 185 

agarikon treatment was serially diluted in sterile water or 99% ethanol before being added to a 384 -186 

well plate at a volume of 75 μL per well. Background was evaluated at 517 nm before adding 25 μL 187 

DPPH stock to each well and reading at 517 nm every 5 min for 60 min, shaking between read 188 

points. Final absorbance values were obtained by subtracting the average background absorbance at 189 

60 min from all other wells, and percent radical scavenging activity was calculated by subtracting 190 

sample absorbance from the maximal signal control, dividing by the maximal signal control, and 191 

multiplying by 100, where the maximal signal control was defined as the absorbance of DPPH 192 

reagent alone at 60 min. 193

4.2 Immune Modulation of Agarikon Treatment After LPS Stimulation 467 

Overall, the general pattern of immune engagement alongside simultaneous anti -inflammatory 468 

action was also observed under inflammatory conditions, though with additional (and appropriate) 469 

stimulation of immune effectors in response to LPS challenge. First, pathway results repeatedly 470 

implicate cytokine and interleukin signaling, and key messengers ( IL 1A , FC = 2.56; IL1 B, FC = 471 

2.01; IL8 , FC = 3.14; TLR5 , FC = 4.28) are significantly upregulated, indicating an appropriate 472 

response to LPS stimulation, where robust but regulated activation of these pathways is required for 473

1α protein response , where the EtOH agarikon fraction resulted in small ( <50 pg/mL) but significant 475 

increases in IL -1α, while the EtOAc fraction resulted in similarly significant decreases in IL -1α 476 

protein levels (Figure 5B). Overall, the pg/mL levels of IL -1α protein induced under inflammatory 477 

conditions are two orders of magnitude lower than the amount of IL -1RA induced under basal 478 

conditions, which aligns with the 5- to 100 -fold greater concentrations of IL -1RA required to 479 

antagonize IL -1α activity (61, 62). 480 

Cytokine transcript upregulation is paired with the downregulation of three suppressor of cytokine 481 

signaling transcripts ( SOCS1 , FC = -1.53; SOCS2 , FC = -2.18, SOCS3 , FC = -1.65), key r egulators 482

inflammatory conditions, with reduced TLR and increased NF -κB subunit transcript expression at 494

immunomodulatory response. 496

4. 3 Translational Relevance of Agarikon Immunomodulation 542 

The transcriptomic immunomodulatory profile showed a clear overlap with antiviral -associated 543 

pathways, supporting a potential mechanistic basis for the well -established antiviral activity of 544 

agarikon observed in functional and clinical contexts (9, 10, 12, 18). Agarikon treatment engaged 545 

key mediators known to support innate immune activation and viral clearance , such as IL8 and IL1 B546 

(Figure s 3G and 7A, 7B ), while also inducing significant virus -related pathway results (Figure 3A,547 

3B, 3C, 3D ), notably including influenza A ( 96, 97, 98). These system -level insights align with 548 

functional antiviral profiling of agarikon mycelial extract against influenza A (H3N2) infection 549 

(Figure 6), which demonstrated SI values exceeding 300 in some assays and consistently higher 550 

activity than ribavirin alongside low cytot ox icity . Influenza -related antiviral activity of agarikon has 551 

also been reported in independent studies, including a prior investigation of the antiviral activity of 552 

aqueous extracts of agarikon mycelium against H5N1 and H3N3, which produced significant 553 

reduc tions in viral titers at concentrations that remained non -cytotoxic ( 18). Furthermore, the strain 554 

of agarikon mycelium employed in the current study has been evaluated clinically in combination 555 

with T. versicolor mycelium in multiple contexts , using unfermented rice as a placebo control. One 556 

clinical trial (9) investigating the use of this fungal preparation as a COVID -19 treatment has 557 

establish ed that this agarikon and T. versicolor mycelium blend is a safe and well -tolerated 558 

intervention associated with reduced number and severity of COVID -19 symptom s, with supportive 559 

preclinical findings demonstrating significant reductions in viral entry in mammalian epithelial cells 560 

(Vero -TMPRSS2 ). Agarikon and T. versicolor mycelium also show promise as a safe COVID -19 561 

vaccine adjunct, where the blend was associated with reduced vaccine -related side effects without 562 

compromising SARS -CoV -2 antibody responses , and potential indications of increased antibody 563 

levels for at least six months in patients without prior COVID -19 exposure ( 8). 564

5 Conclusion 584 

Despite millennia of traditional use and modern clinical application, agariko n treatment remains 585 

incompletely characterized with respect to mechanistic immune outcomes. Building on findings 586 

reported for the fruit body, this work highlights coordinated immun oregulatory effects elicited by 587 

agarikon mycelium, providing context for preclinical studies and clinical investigations of individual 588 

and blended preparations including agarikon and supporting hypotheses regarding its specifics of 589 

action , such as IL -1 axis modulation. While mycelial and fruit body preparations seem to produce 590 

both overlapping and distinct biological effects, this study further supports the immune -buffering 591 

capacity of mycelium -based treatment and highlights overlap with fruit body findings, including 592 

engagement of NF -κB signaling. Given the endangered status of agarikon, mycelial cultivation 593 

offers a scalable and sustainable approach to accessing its bioactive potential, underscoring the 594 

importance of continued research on this material. Overall , these findings support a role for agarikon 595 

in promoting adaptive resilience under stress ed conditions, characterized by coordinated 596 

immunomodulatory activity and maintenance of redox balance. 597
