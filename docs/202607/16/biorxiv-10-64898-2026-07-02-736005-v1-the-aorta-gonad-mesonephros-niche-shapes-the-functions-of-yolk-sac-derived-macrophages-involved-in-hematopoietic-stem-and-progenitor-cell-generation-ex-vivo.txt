Abstract 19 

Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) generated from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) offer a 20 

promising patient-specific alternative to allogeneic transplantation, yet current differentiation 21 

protocols fail to fully recapitulate in vivo HSC maturation. During mouse development, yolk sac 22 

(YS)-derived macrophages populate the aorta-gonad-mesonephros (AGM) region at the time of 23 

HSC emergence, but the mechanisms by which they support ex vivo hematopoietic stem and 24 

progenitor cell (HSPC) generation remain poorly defined. Bulk RNA sequencing revealed that 25 

mature AGM CD206 ⁺ macrophages upregulate pro-inflammatory cytokines and the adhesion 26 

molecule F4/80. Using F4/80 knockout embryos, we identify a previously unreported, niche-27 

specific role for F4/80 in restraining the frequency and colony-forming activity of HSPC subsets 28

Introduction 47 

Hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) are vital components of the hematopoietic system, 48 

responsible for the production of all blood cell types throughout the lifetime on an individual. 49 

HSCs can be used for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, a curative therapy for various 50 

blood disorders. 51 

HSCs are unique in their ability to self-renew, differentiate, and reconstitute the entire blood 52 

system in a recipient after transplantation 1.53

For over six decades, allogeneic HSC transplantation has been employed to treat a range of 54 

malignant and non-malignant blood diseases. However, several challenges limit the widespread 55 

application of this therapeutic approach. Two major obstacles are the variability in patient 56 

response to treatment, and the scarcity of human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-matched donors, 57 

particularly for individuals from ethnically diverse backgrounds 2, 3 . The latter can lead to serious 58 

consequences such as graft-versus-host disease, a life-threatening complication in which T cells 59 

transplanted together with the HSCs recognize the recipient’s body as non-self and mount an 60 

immune response against it 4. The possibility of mitigating transplantation-related side effects by 61 

expanding patient-derived functional HSCs in vitro is hindered by the difficulty of maintaining 62 

and expanding HSCs outside a living organism 5, 6 . As a result, many patients face significant 63 

barriers in accessing curative treatments for blood disorders, highlighting the need for innovative 64 

solutions to address these challenges. 65 

66 

An alternative approach is to generate HSCs from induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) in a 67 

patient-specific manner 2. Years of research have significantly advanced our knowledge on the 68 

topic, with several scientific breakthroughs moving the field from suboptimal early attempts to 69 

highly promising iPSC differentiation protocols 7-9 . Recent work from by Ng et al. described a 70 

method for generating repopulating iPSC-derived HSCs with high-level multipotent 71 

engraftment 9. However, they reported a 20-fold lower frequency of primary engraftment 72 

compared to mobilized CD34 + cells, and low levels of secondary engraftment 9, suggesting that 73 

some aspects of in vivo HSC generation and maturation remain difficult to recapitulate in vitro .74 

75 

HSCs are generated during embryonic development in both humans and mice 10-13 . They are 76 

produced in the aorta-gonad-mesonephros (AGM) region of the embryo during the third 77 

definitive wave of developmental hematopoiesis, occurring at embryonic day (E)10.5 in mouse 78 

and Carnegie stages (CS)13-17 in humans, through a trans-differentiation process called 79

It is made The copyright holder for this preprint (which this version posted July 3, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.02.736005 doi: bioRxiv preprint

endothelial-to-hematopoietic transition (EHT) 14-17 . The first two waves preceding HSC 80 

generation occur in the extra embryonic yolk sac (YS) at E7.5 and E8.5 in mice, and CS6-CS7 81 

and CS8-CS10 in humans 13, 18 . Moreover, an additional wave of fetal-restricted hematopoietic 82 

stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) has recently been described, emerging from vitelline and 83 

umbilical arteries between E8.5 and E9.5 in the mouse embryo 19 .84 

85 

Both waves of YS hematopoiesis see the production of various progenitors, such as early and 86 

late erythroid-myeloid-progenitors (EMPs) which give rise to differentiated cells including 87 

macrophages 20-23 .88 

89 

YS-derived macrophages have well-established functions in tissue specification and remodeling 90 

during development 12, 24-26 Moreover, we and others previously demonstrated that YS-derived 91 

macrophages are present in the AGM of mouse and human embryos at the time of HSC 92 

generation 27, 28 . However, the mechanism through which macrophages contribute to HSPC 93 

generation and/or maturation remains an open question. Understanding which macrophage-94 

related factors can promote HSPC generation and/or maturation is key to informing human 95 

iPSC-based differentiation protocols and to more fully recapitulating HSC generation in vitro. 96 

Here we demonstrate that macrophages expressing the mannose receptors in the mouse AGM 97 

at E10.5 have specific HSPC-promoting functions in an in vitro model of endothelial-to-98 

hematopoietic transition. These macrophages, while YS-derived, remodel their transcriptome 99 

upon entry into the AGM and acquire niche-specific functions not shared by macrophages 100 

present in the YS at the same developmental stage. Transcriptomic analyses revealed that AGM 101 

macrophages express a combination of pro-inflammatory genes and metalloproteinases that 102 

may mediate their niche-specific functions. Furthermore, we describe a previously unreported 103 

role for F4/80 in controlling the frequency of specific HSPC subsets in the mouse AGM. 104

Material and Methods 106 

Timed matings 107 

Wild type C57BL/6 from the University of Edinburgh’s animal facility, as well as MacGreen 108 

(B6N.Cg-Tg(Csf1r-EGFP)) 29 , and F4/80 knock out (KO) 30 mice were used for timed mating 109 

experiments. Embryos were generated from MacGreen males crossed with wild type females, 110 

and F4/80 KO males crossed with F4/80 KO females. Embryos were staged accurately by 111 

counting somite pairs (sp), with 30-34sp corresponding to embryonic day (E) 10 and 35-39sp 112 

corresponding to E10.5. The day of vaginal plug detection was designated as E0.5, and plugged 113 

females were sacrificed at E10.5 for embryo collection. All mice were housed and bred at the 114 

University of Edinburgh's animal facilities in accordance with Home Office regulations, and all 115 

animal experiments were performed under the authority of a UK Home Office Project License 116 

and were approved by the University of Edinburgh's Animal Welfare and Ethical Review Body 117 

(AWERB). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Animals (Scientific 118 

Procedures) Act 1986 and the EU Directive 2010/63/EU on the protection of animals used for 119 

scientific purposes. For lineage-tracing experiments using the Cdh5-CreER T2 system, pregnant 120 

females received a single intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection of 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT; 37.5 121 

mg/kg body weight) dissolved in corn oil at either E7.5 or E8.5. Embryos were then harvested at 122 

E10.5 for analysis. Cdh5-CreER T2 and Rosa26 LSL-tdTomato mice were maintained at the San 123 

Raffaele Scientific Institute under standard housing conditions with ad libitum access to food 124

Immunostaining 155 

E10.5 embryos were fixed in 2% paraformaldehyde (ThermoFisher Scientific) in PBS solution 156 

for 30 minutes at 4°C in the dark. This was followed by an overnight incubation in 20% sucrose 157 

(Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS solution at 4°C in the dark. The embryos were then embedded in 158 

Cryomatrix (Epredia) and frozen at -80°C, with the yolk sac positioned on the side but still 159 

attached to the embryo. Frozen embryos were sectioned into 10 μm thick sections using a 160 

cryostat (Bright) and collected on Superfrost Plus slides (ThermoFisher Scientific). After 161 

thawing, the sections were treated with absolute acetone for 10 minutes and allowed to air dry. 162 

The sections were then washed three times with 0.05% Tween 20 (Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS by 163 

gentle shaking for 5 minutes. The sections were subsequently incubated with primary antibodies 164 

(Supplementary Table 1) in PBS-block buffer (PBS + 0.05% Tween 20 + 1% bovine serum 165 

albumin (Sigma-Aldrich)) for 1 hour at room temperature in a humid chamber. This was followed 166 

by three washes with PBS-Tween 20 before incubation with secondary antibodies 167 

(Supplementary Table 1) in PBS-block for 30 minutes at room temperature. However, for the 168 

F4/80 antibody, which was directly conjugated to the BV421 fluorophore, an overnight 169 

incubation at -20°C in the humid chamber was performed, omitting the secondary antibody step. 170 

After washing, the slides were mounted in Slowfade Diamond mountant (ThermoFisher 171 

Scientific) and the coverslips were sealed with clear nail polish and allowed to dry. The slides 172 

were stored at 4°C until visualization under a confocal microscope. 173 

To control for non-specific binding, single antibody-stained and secondary antibody-only stained 174 

sections were included in each experiment. Immunostained slides were visualized using a Leica 175 

SP8 microscope. 176

RNA sequencing processing 193 

Fastq files of 51 bp single-end reads were quality controlled using FASTQC. Reads were then 194 

aligned to the GENCODE M14 32 mouse reference genome (mm10) using STAR aligner (version 195 

2.3) for Linux Ubuntu, with default options, and ran over 8 threads per sample. For improved 196 

accuracy, the exon junction coordinates from the reference annotation were used. Resulting 197 

BAM files from different lanes were merged into one sample. Gene quantification on the merged 198 

samples was performed using the function “featureCounts” from the subRead package 33 .199

AGM E10.5 CD206 + macrophages and CD206 - cells are yolk sac-derived. 313 

It has been previously reported that macrophages in the mouse embryos are yolk sac (YS)-314 

derived 28, 40 . However, the possibility of de novo macrophage generation in the AGM has never 315 

been formally excluded. To address this, we employed a well-validated pulse-chase approach in 316 

which Cdh5-CreER T2 mice were crossed with Rosa26 LSL-tdTomato mice to generate embryos in 317 

which tdTomato labelling was induced by administering 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) to 318 

pregnant dams at E7.5 and E8.5 19, 41, 42 . At these time points, YS progenitors derived from 319 

Cdh5 ⁺ hemogenic endothelium are preferentially labelled. Pulsed embryos were then collected 320 

at E10.5 to assess tdTomato expression in AGM CD206 ⁺ Mac and CD206 ⁻ cells. Between 96– 321 

100% of cells in both populations were tdTomato ⁺ following pulse labelling at E7.5, with more 322 

variable tdTomato expression (36–100%) when pulse-labelled at E8.5 (Supplementary Fig. 3A– 323 

B). Normalization against tdTomato expression in YS erythro-myeloid progenitors (EMPs), used 324 

as a control to account for variability in labelling efficiency across experiments, consistently 325 

showed equivalent labelling levels in AGM CD206 ⁺ macrophages, CD206 ⁻ cells, and YS EMPs 326

Discussion 472 

In this study, we employed a combination of in vivo and in vitro approaches to identify the 473 

mediators of AGM macrophage functions that facilitate HSPC generation in vitro . We first 474 

established that CD206 expression is a hallmark of terminally differentiated macrophages in the 475 

mouse AGM, defined by the absence of in vitro progenitor potential — a capacity that is retained 476 

by CD45 ⁺CD11b ⁺F4/80 ⁺cKit ⁻CD206 ⁻ cells. CD206 ⁺ Mac (CD45 ⁺CD11b ⁺F4/80 ⁺cKit ⁻CD206 ⁺)477 

enhance HSPC generation when co-cultured with endothelial cells in vitro . CD206, also known 478 

as the mannose receptor, is a carbohydrate-binding endocytic receptor with diverse functions 479 

including clearance of endogenous and exogenous molecules, collagen internalization, 480 

modulation of cellular activation, and promotion of antigen presentation 51 . CD206 is typically 481 

expressed by mature monocytes, macrophages, and certain subsets of vascular and lymphatic 482 

> .CC-BY 4.0 International license available under a was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made The copyright holder for this preprint (which this version posted July 3, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.02.736005 doi: bioRxiv preprint

endothelial cells 52 . Historically, mannose receptor expression has been associated with 483 

alternatively activated macrophages exhibiting predominantly anti-inflammatory and pro-tumoral 484 

functions 53, 54 . However, this association has been recently challenged by studies demonstrating 485 

a role for CD206 ⁺ Mac in recruiting anti-tumoral T cells 55 , pointing to a more nuanced role for 486 

CD206 ⁺ Mac in cancer biology. A similar complexity appears to apply to AGM macrophages, in 487 

which CD206 is expressed on the surface of macrophages that also express pro-inflammatory 488 

cytokines. As previously discussed 28 , in the embryonic AGM — an environment not exposed to 489 

extracellular pathogens — a pro-inflammatory milieu has been reported to be necessary for 490 

correct HSC generation in vertebrates 56, 57 . Although we consider CD206 to be a marker of 491 

macrophage maturity in the AGM rather than a molecule directly involved in HSC generation, 492 

additional experiments are needed to determine whether AGM CD206 ⁺ Mac actively contribute 493 

to extracellular matrix remodeling through collagen internalization. That said, the viability of 494 

CD206 KO mice, with no reported defects in embryonic HSC generation, argues against a direct 495 

functional role for CD206 in embryonic HSPC generation 58, 59 .496 

We also observed that CD206 ⁺ Mac are characterized by high F4/80 expression. F4/80 is one of 497 

the most specific cell-surface markers for murine macrophages 39, 60 . It belongs to the epidermal 498 

growth factor–seven transmembrane receptor family and is thought to mediate cell adhesion; 499 

however, its precise function(s) remain to be fully elucidated 61 . F4/80 knockout mouse models 500 

have not revealed a requirement for F4/80 in normal macrophage development or function 62 .501 

Nevertheless, F4/80 has been shown to be required for the induction of antigen-specific CD8 ⁺502 

regulatory T cells in models of peripheral tolerance in adult mice, a process dependent on direct 503 

cellular interaction between macrophages and NKT cells 30 . This demonstrates that F4/80 can 504 

mediate cell-to-cell interactions in specific tissue contexts. Having previously observed direct 505 

interactions between macrophages and hematopoietic cells in the mouse AGM at E10.5 28 , we 506 

used the F4/80 KO mouse model 30 to investigate whether F4/80 plays a previously 507 

unrecognized role in mediating macrophage–HSPC interactions in the mouse embryo. Notably, 508

in the AGM. Since CD31 ⁺F4/80 ⁺ endothelial cells have been reported in both embryos and adult 511 

mice 63, 64 , but our aim is to investigate the effect of the absence of macrophage-expressed 512 

F4/80, flow cytometry analyses were performed on F4/80 ⁻ cells to exclude any confounding 513 

factors. While endothelial cell frequency was reduced in the absence of F4/80, the frequency of 514 

cKit ⁺CD31 ⁻ cells was increased. cKit is a receptor tyrosine kinase whose expression is a 515 

canonical marker of HSPCs 65 . An increased frequency of cKit ⁺ cells in the AGM of F4/80 KO 516 

embryos could indicate a role for F4/80 in HSPC generation or proliferation. However, we 517 

cannot exclude the possibility that other non-hematopoietic cell types within the AGM also 518 

express cKit. The AGM encompasses not only the aorta — the site of HSPC generation — but 519 

also the gonad and mesonephros, which give rise to the gonads and kidneys later in 520 

development. Although no studies have specifically examined cKit expression in the gonad– 521 

mesonephros region of the AGM at E10.5, cKit expression has been documented during 522 

gametogenesis in primordial germ cells, spermatogonia, primordial and growing oocytes, and in 523 

the developing kidney 66, 67 . Therefore, the overall increase in cKit ⁺ cell frequency observed in 524 

F4/80 KO embryos may not be restricted to the hematopoietic compartment. Nevertheless, an 525 

expansion of early hematopoietic progenitors expressing CD41 lo , CD43, and CD45 is evident in 526 

F4/80 KO embryos at E10.5 and it is reflected by increased progenitor output from AGM cells 527 

isolated from these embryos. This may result from defective interactions between macrophages 528 

and HSPCs, leading to an expansion of specific HSPC subsets — suggesting that macrophages 529 

contribute to regulating the number of HSPCs generated in the AGM at E10.5. A similar 530 

regulatory role has been reported in the caudal hematopoietic tissue of zebrafish (the functional 531 

equivalent of the mouse fetal liver), where macrophages influence the number of HSC clones 532 

generated in the embryo that subsequently contribute to adult hematopoiesis 68 . In zebrafish, 533

Limitations 583 

> .CC-BY 4.0 International license available under a was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in perpetuity. It is made The copyright holder for this preprint (which this version posted July 3, 2026. ;https://doi.org/10.64898/2026.07.02.736005 doi: bioRxiv preprint

While the transcriptomic differences between embryonic macrophage subsets are 584 

representative of the in vivo situation in the mouse embryo, the functional assays were 585 

performed in an in vitro 2D model of endothelial-to-hematopoietic transition. This was 586 

specifically designed to inform iPSC protocols for in vitro HSC/HSPC generation; however, the 587 

results may not fully recapitulate what occurs in vivo . In particular, the compensatory 588 

mechanisms that may operate in a living embryo are absent in our model. We previously 589 

reported a decrease in functional HSCs in the absence of macrophages 28 and here we 590 

confirmed impaired HSPC generation in vitro . However, Perçin et al. recently reported no deficit 591 

in AGM HSC numbers in an in vivo model of embryonic macrophage depletion, suggesting that 592 

compensatory mechanisms operate in vivo that are not recapitulated in our system 74 .593 

A further limitation of this study is the absence of transplantation data. With the current 594 

experimental setup, it is not possible to determine whether the loss of macrophages and/or 595 

F4/80 also impairs the generation of functional HSCs. Future experiments using human iPSCs 596 

will be necessary to evaluate the effects of macrophages on HSC generation and/or maturation, 597 

and to assess the translational relevance of our findings. Human iPSC experiments will also be 598 

critical for determining whether supplementation with macrophage-related factors — such as 599 

CCL2, CXCL16, and TNFα — during differentiation is beneficial for HSC generation, particularly 600 

given previous reports that an in vitro inflammatory environment may favour HSC differentiation 601 

over self-renewal 75 . It should be noted, however, that this effect was observed in fetal bovine 602 

serum-containing cultures 75 , and may therefore not directly apply to defined, serum-free iPSC 603 

differentiation systems. In conclusion, our data reveal a previously unreported role for F4/80 in 604 

regulating HSPC frequency in the E10.5 AGM, and define a gene signature for CD206 ⁺ Mac that 605 

may be used to inform iPSC protocols for in vitro HSC differentiation. Validation in human 606 

cellular models will nonetheless be essential to establish the translational relevance of these 607 

findings. 608
