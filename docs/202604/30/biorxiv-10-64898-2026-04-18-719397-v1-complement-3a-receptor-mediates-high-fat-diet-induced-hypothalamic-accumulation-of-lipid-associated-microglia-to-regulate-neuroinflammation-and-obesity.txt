Abstract 21 

Microglia, the resident macrophages of the central nervous system, are recognized for their heterogeneity and 22 

integral role in brain function and diseases. In the context of high fat diet (HFD) feeding and obesity, microglia 23 

become overactive, acquiring a prevailing lipid associated microglial phenotype (also known as LAM). Yet, how 24 

microgliosis is induced and regulated remains unclear. Here we report a key role for the Complement 3a 25 

Receptor (C3aR), on HFD-induced hypothalamic gliosis and weight gain in mice. HFD consumption leads to 26 

elevated microglial expression of C3aR, which parallels widespread accumulation of reactive microglia, 27 

selectively in the hypothalamus. Conditional microglial C3aR deletion protects mice from HFD-induced 28 

hypothalamic reactive microgliosis. C3aR deletion or pharmacological antagonism opposes HFD-induced weight 29 

gain in male but not female mice. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that C3aR is essential for lipid-induced lipid 30 

droplet formation, and acquisition of a LAM molecular signature. In summary, we uncovered a previously 31 

unknown role for C3aR in the acquisition of a LAM signature driving diet-induced gliosis, identifying this receptor 32 

as a new viable therapeutic candidate for conditions associated with hypothalamic neuroinflammation. 33

Introduction 1

Ingestion of high fat diet (HFD) is associated with many inflammation-associated health complications including 2

obesity, type II diabetes, various types of cancers, neurodegenerative disorders, and cardiovascular diseases, 3

which are collectively the leading causes of mortality in the United States and worldwide[1–5]. Hence, there is a 4

need to increase our knowledge on downstream mechanisms informing the development of more effective and 5

targeted therapies[6–9]. HFD feeding and obesity are associated with a state of chronic, low-grade inflammation 6

in many organs, including the brain[10–12]. The hypothalamus, recognized as the homeostatic and autonomic 7

control center, is a primary site of maladaptive neuroinflammation associated with obesity or ingestion of diets 8

rich in fat, ultimately compromising hypothalamic function and leading to metabolic diseases[10–15] and 9

neurodegeneration[16–18] with prolonged exposure. 10 

The brain parenchyma hosts various non-neuronal cell types that are tightly integrated in functional circuits with 11 

neuronal populations. The prominent non-neuronal cells in the CNS are astrocytes, microglia, and 12 

oligodendrocytes[19,20], with a smaller population of resident immune cells also being present[21]. These non-13 

neural parenchymal cells have gathered growing attention for their still underappreciated roles in health[22–28]. 14 

In particular, microglia, the tissue resident macrophages of the CNS, are sensitive to a wide array of nutrients 15 

and signals, and capable of transmitting paracrine messengers to nearby neurons and astrocytes to directly 16 

regulate brain function and energy homeostasis[24,28,29]. Under HFD and obesity, microglia rapidly expand, 17 

particularly in the hypothalamus, which can cause neuronal dysfunction and physiological 18 

derangements[10,15,30–32]. If this proinflammatory cascade is persistent, it may become maladaptive, 19 

contributing to neurodegeneration, and increasing the risk of developing diseases such as ischemic injury, and 20 

Alzheimer’s or Parkinson’s diseases[10,33–36]. 21 

While substantial progress has been made in understanding the role of neuroinflammation on disease 22 

pathogenesis[14,33,35,36], limited knowledge exists on the molecular drivers of gliosis, besides the established 23 

role of fatty acid activation of Toll like receptor 4, and associated proinflammatory response[10–12]. Innate 24 

immunity, more specifically the complement system, is likely to play a pivotal role in neuroinflammation and brain 25 

diseases due to its early activation in response to various insults[37,38]. Yet, the link between the complement 26 

system, gliosis, and neuroinflammation is incompletely understood. Here we report a previously unknown key 27 

role for the Complement 3a Receptor (C3aR, encoded by the C3ar1 gene) on diet-induced hypothalamic gliosis 28 

and neuroinflammation. C3aR is a G-protein coupled receptor showing prominent expression in tissue-resident 29 

macrophages[39,40] and is the target of two endogenous ligands, the C3-derived anaphylatoxin C3a, and the 30 

neural VGF-derived (non-acronymic) peptide TLQP-21[40–45]. The role of C3aR on brain function and 31 

neurodegeneration in the context of Alzheimer’s disease is starting to emerge[42,43], but its role in diet-induced 32 

neuroinflammation remains unclear. Here we show that C3aR is exclusively expressed by microglia in the CNS, 33 

and that consumption of a HFD increases C3aR expression paralleling hypothalamic microgliosis, astrogliosis, 34 

and microglial activation, independent of obesity – changes that can be ameliorated by microglial-specific 35 

conditional deletion of C3aR. Mechanistically, we found C3aR is a novel marker of lipid associated microglia 36

Methods 4

Mouse experiments 5

All experiments and procedures were conducted with the approval of the University of Minnesota Institutional 6

Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC, protocol # 2301-40694A). The tdTomato-C3aR1 lox/lox reporter knock-7

in mouse model[39] was obtained in order to localize C3aR expression in the central nervous system. These 8

reporter mice, along with wild-type mice lacking the tdTomato reporter sequence, were used for comparison in 9

the initial characterization of the CNS C3aR. For experiments involving targeted conditional deletion of C3aR in 10 

microglia, we first crossed CX3CR1 creERT2/creERT2 mice (JAX, #:021160) with tdTomato-C3aR1 lox/lox mice to 11 

generate heterogeneous progeny that were then backcrossed to tdTomato-C3aR1 lox/lox mice to generate 12 

secondary progeny with necessary experimental genotypes. Following extensive genotyping of the secondary 13 

progeny, only those that were homozygous for loxP alleles were used for experiments as they were either or 14 

positive for the Cre transgene, resulting in experimental animals with WT (tdTom-C3aR1 lox/lox ;CX3CR1 wt/wt ) or 15 

KO (tdTom-C3aR1 lox/lox ;CX3CR1 creERT2/wt ) genotypes once given Tamoxifen (TAM). As this is an inducible system, 16 

TAM was dissolved in sterile corn oil (catalog #T5648; Sigma - Aldrich) at a concentration of 20mg/ml and 17 

administered via intraperitoneal (IP) injections to these experimental animals (aged 3-4mo) at a dosage of 18 

100mg/kg for five days while housed in their home cages, alternating sides of injection sites each day. 19 

Long term in vivo experiments for extensive metabolic characterization involving these C3aR1 lox/lox -CreER- or 20 

C3aR1 lox/lox -CreER+ animals placed on either a standard diet (Research Diets: SD, D12450B, n=10-21 

12/group/sex) or one of two high fat diets (Research Diets: 45% HFD, D12541 n=10-12/group/sex; 60% HFD, 22 

D124192, n=5/group/sex) began nine days after the final TAM injection. Animals were individually housed in 23 

static cages and put on a 6AM-6PM light/dark cycle three days to acclimate prior to starting their respective diets. 24 

Body weights and food intake were measured twice a week and before starting diets. Body composition was 25 

recorded via EchoMRI (EchoMRI LLC, Houston, TX, USA) at baseline prior to starting respective diets and 26 

measured biweekly until the end of the experiment. Energy expenditure was measured via indirect calorimetry 27 

using the Oxymax Comprehensive Lab Animal Monitoring System (CLAMS; Columbus Instruments) and 28 

analyzed using CalR, an online tool for processing metabolic data[46]. Glucose tolerance tests (GTT) were 29 

performed following six hours of fasting and measured immediately and up to two hours after intraperitoneal 30 

injection of D-glucose (2g/kg). Blood glucose was measured using Accucheck Aviva glucometers (Roche 31 

Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN). 32 

Intranasal delivery experiments (performed completely TAM naïve) were performed as previously described[47], 33 

with animals being acclimated prior to commencement of their respective diets and treatment of either sterile 34 

saline or DArg10_Aib20[48] (in sterile saline with pH adjusted to 7.4). During the treatment period, animals 35 

received 12ul of volume intranasally (3μl bolus in each nostril, two-minute rest, and repeat) daily. 36

Cy5-TLQP-21 In Vivo Imaging 5

Animal care and experimental protocols for in vivo imaging were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and 6

Use Committee at Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. Seven days prior to imaging, C57BL/6 (Jackson 7

Labs, Bar Harbor, ME, USA) were switched from a standard to an imaging diet [LabDiet 5V75 PicoLab Verified 8

75/IF (WF Fisher & Sons, Somerville, NJ, USA)] to eliminate interfering autofluorescence. One day prior to 9

imaging, fur on the head and upper abdomen was trimmed, and residual hair was removed by depilatory cream. 10 

Fluorescent images were captured using an In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS) Spectrum and LivingImage software 11 

(Perkin Elmer, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Mice were anesthetized and placed inside the IVIS imaging chamber with 12 

2% O2 and 2.5% isoflurane delivered via nose cone. Following capture of baseline images, lightly-anesthetized 13 

mice were intranasally-administered sterile saline or N-terminal fluorescent Cyanine 5 (Cy5) conjugated TLQP-14 

21 [5 μg/μL; Genscript (Piscataway, NJ, USA)] in saline, with total volumes of 6 μL (30 μg) or 12 μL (60 μg) 15 

delivered via pipet gun. Images were captured under anesthesia using excitation λ of 675nm and emission λ of 16 

720 nm ( Supplemental Figure 8 ). All images were analyzed for captured radiance (radiant efficiency), and 17 

statistical analysis was carried out using Graphpad Prism (Graphpad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). 18

Immunofluorescence 10 

Tissue was washed 3 times for 5 minutes each in PBS (6 washes for free-floating brain sections stored in 11 

cryoprotectant). Only brain tissue and not peripheral tissues were permeabilized using 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS 12 

for 15 minutes. Blocking was performed to prevent any nonspecific binding using 5% Normal Donkey Serum 13

were briefly rinsed with deionized water and coverslipped using ProLong Diamond antifade mounting medium 18

Immunofluorescence Quantification 24 

For quantification of hypothalamic microglial densities, sections spanning the thickness of the hypothalamus 25 

were collected, stained, and sampled [Bregma positions = -0.58mm, -0.94mm, -1.22mm, and -1.70]. 20x stitched 26 

images of the hypothalamus were collected at each slice/bregma position, with total Iba1+ cell count (CellCounter 27

were averaged across the four sections per animal. Quantification of the tdTomato fluorescent reporter was 29 

performed similarly to that of Iba1+ cells. For astrocyte density, images were converted to grayscale and 30 

thresholded to minimize any background or non-specific signal from being quantified. The density of astrocytes 31 

was calculated using the measurement function to determine percent area occupied by GFAP+ pixels. 32 

Iba1/CD68 and Iba1/Trem2-double positive cells were manually quantified by overlaying the individual image 33 

channels in FIJI/ImageJ and using the CellCounter plugin, again with 4 stitched 20x FOVs per animal. 34

Sholl Analysis for Microglial Morphology 4

Z-stack images at 1μm step size were collected at 60x magnification utilizing the optical sectioning to achieve 5

highest resolution images necessary for Sholl analysis. The Iba1 stacks were converted to a maximum intensity 6

projection using Keyence BZ-X810 Analyzer and then imported into ImageJ/FIJI for Sholl analysis. Microglial 7

ramification was quantified using a publicly available plugin (neuroanatomy > sholl > legacy: “sholl analysis from 8

image” [set radius = 0-45 μm, step = 0.5 μm][50]). Three FOVs were collected and processed from each animal 9

(males and females), containing around 5-10 cells/FOV and every cell captured was sampled for analysis. 10 

Values from all the microglia per mouse were averaged at each 1μm radial step size from the soma and then 11 

AUC was calculated. One-way ANOVA was performed on the AUC values averages across the groups; each 12 

point represents the total average of all the individual cells from a given animal subject. Experimenters were 13 

blinded to the genotype/sex/diet identifications when processing the images and performing Sholl analysis. 14

Isolation of primary microglia and immunocytochemistry for tdTom-C3aR1 model validation 2

Brains of postnatal day P0-3 tdTom-C3aR1 lox/lox or tdTom -/- were collected from decapitated pups and the 3

meninges were fully removed. Brains were collected in increments of 3-4 in 6-well plates on ice, each well 4

containing cold Hibernate A media (Catalog number: A1247501; ThermoFisher Scientific). Tissue was 5

homogenized in media using spring scissors, transferred to labeled, sterile 15mL Falcon tubes and then 6

centrifuged at 300g for 5 minutes. The pellets were resuspended in the culture media, filtered through a 70μM 7

nylon cell strainer (Ref: 352350; Falcon) into a clean tube and then seeded in poly-L-lysine pre-coated T75 8

flasks. Culture media consisted of Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% fetal bovine 9

serum (FBS, Invitrogen) and 0.5% penicillin/streptomycin. Mixed cultures were maintained at 37°C and 5% CO 2.10 

Media was changed the day after dissection to remove dead cells, and then changed every 3 days afterwards. 11 

After around 12-14 days, a dense astrocyte monolayer has formed at the bottom of the plate and microglia are 12 

loosely attached to said astrocytes. For microglial collection, flasks were agitated at 180rpm for 30 minutes and 13 

media was collected and changed for later, repeated collections. Isolated microglia were then plated for 14 

immunocytochemistry or calcium influx assays. 15 

For immunocytochemistry, isolated primary microglia (either from tdTom -/- or tdTom +/+ animals) were plated to 16 

poly-L-lysine pre-coated 8-well slide plates (Part #: 80826, ibidi) and then fixed with 4% PFA in PBS for 15 17 

minutes. Cells were washed with cold PBS and then blocked with 5% Normal Donkey Serum (Jackson 18 

ImmunoResearch; 017-000-121) in PBS for 1 hour. Cells were then incubated with a microglial primary antibody 19 

(rabbit anti-Iba1, 1:1000, FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals) for 1 hour, washed 3x in PBS, and then incubated with a 20 

secondary antibody (donkey anti-rabbit 488; Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories) for 1 hour, followed by a 21 

final wash series, counterstaining with DAPI, and then imaging. 22 

The calcium influx assay was conducted as previously described 44,45 . Primary microglia lifted from the astrocyte 23 

monolayer were plated to 8-well slide plates and loaded with calcium indicator, 2.5 μM Fluo-4 AM (Invitrogen) 24 

for 30 minutes. Cells were washed for 30 minutes with 1x Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (Gibco) and then 25 

replaced with clean HBSS. The slides were placed on a live cell environmental chamber in a Nikon Ti-E 26 

Deconvolution Microscope System with a New Lambda coat anti-reflective coated 20x objective lens. Images of 27 

individual wells were captured every second for a total of two minutes. For each recording, the respective 28 

individual wells were treated with 10μM TLQP-21 in HBSS just to observe calcium influx. Respective fluorescent 29 

images of each well/FOV were captured for tdTom (TxRed) and Fluo4 (GFP). Nikon Elements software was 30 

used for the measurement and analysis of the assays. 31

Selective expression of C3aR by microglia in the central nervous system 17

loxP sites for Cre-mediated excision (tdTom-C3aR1 lox/lox )[39]. After initial observation of diffuse tdTom 22 

fluorescence in the brain, including in the hypothalamus ( Fig.S1A-B ) and qPCR confirmation that the fusion of 23 

tdTom and insertion of the loxP-flanked cassette does not grossly affect receptor expression ( Fig.S1C ), we 24 

utilized cell type markers for microglia, neurons, and astrocytes (anti-Iba1, anti-NeuN, and anti-GFAP 25 

respectively) to assign the tdTomato signal to the different cell types. Microglia were the only major cell type in 26

Discussion 9

Ingestion of diet rich in fat is an established trigger for hypothalamic neuroinflammation[10,14,85–88], leading to 10 

increased risk for significant health complications[36,89], yet the mechanism underlying this process are still 11 

unclear. Building on the characterization of its unique expression in microglia, we identified a key role for the 12 

C3aR in HFD-induced hypothalamic gliosis, and a sex-specific effect on obesity. Mechanistically, we link dietary 13 

lipids to the acquisition of a LAM signature via C3aR. Recent studies implicate C3aR signaling in microglial 14 

activation in the context of neurodegeneration, and obesity-related conditions[43,45,48,65]. For example a recent 15 

study showed that C3aR signaling adversely affects the ability of microglia to clear amyloid-β plaques, leading 16 

to worsened neuropathology and cognitive outcomes[42,43,65,90,91]. Consistently, deletion of C3aR exerts a 17 

protective effect that restores normal microglial function and ameliorates associated cognitive deficits in AD 18 

mouse models through improved clearance of amyloid-β plaques[43,65,90]. These studies identified a central 19 

role for C3aR in microglia function and dysfunction in neurodegeneration, yet its role in diet-induced 20 

neuroinflammation remains poorly characterized. 21 

22 

An essential role for C3aR in HFD-induced hypothalamic gliosis 23 

Diet-induced hypothalamic gliosis can occur rapidly, often within days and can eventually affect other regions of 24 

the brain as well over extended periods of time[15,30,92,93]. Upon HFD exposure, we observed a strong 25 

increase in the expression of C3aR paralleling hypothalamic microgliosis, which was independent of sex and 26 

body weight gain. The notion that hypothalamic inflammation and gliosis are primarily driven by HFD 27 

consumption rather than body weight gain is consistent with previous data[14,30,85,94]. Conditional deletion or 28 

pharmacological antagonism of microglial C3aR normalized HFD-induced hypothalamic gliosis, suggesting that 29 

microglial C3aR is a major contributor in diet-induced expansion of glial cells, but also that the differentiation of 30 

BM-derived monocytes (which are C3aR negative) into Iba1+ macrophages is minimal under the present 31 

experimental condition, suggesting that local proliferation is the primary source of additional 32 

microglia/macrophages in this context[95]. We showed that microglial C3aR not only affects hypothalamic 33 

microglial density but also morphology, which is indicative of pro-inflammatory activation[74–76]. In response to 34 

HFD and many other pathogenic conditions, microglia acquire a proinflammatory state characterized by a 35

retracted processes and arborizations. Similarly, HFD resulted in increased CD68 staining as a proxy of 3

phagocytic activity[73,75,85]. C3aR deletion reverted the diet-induced amoeboid-like structure and normalized 4

CD68 staining in mice fed HFD irrespective of obesity and sex. The excess of circulating nutrients or lipid-driven 5

metabolic signaling, as well as elevated C3a production from activated astrocytes [96], which together promotes 6

microglial expansion and activation [97–100] may account for the HFD-induced microgliosis. 7

8

C3aR exerts a sex dependent role in HFD-induced obesity. 9

C3aR, and its endogenous ligands has been implicated previously in obesity[101–105]. However, a mechanistic 10 

understanding of how C3aR activation by microglia exerts a weight modulating effect is lacking. Here we showed 11 

that in the context of HFD consumption, the sex-independent hypothalamic microgliosis was paralleled by 12 

significant obesity in a sex-specific manner. When mice were fed a 45% HFD, only males significantly gained 13 

weight, while a 60% HFD was necessary for both sexes to become obese. Notably, C3aR deletion protected 14 

male but not female mice from 45% HFD-induced obesity. The relative obesity resistance in females was possibly 15 

masking a protective metabolic effect of C3aR deletion, and further studies are needed to elucidate the 16 

mechanism of this sex-dependent vulnerability, especially since neither food intake measured during the entire 17 

study or energy expenditure assessed after 10 weeks on the diet were affected by gene deletion. Nevertheless 18 

to extend this finding, we treated 45% HFD-fed male mice with daily intranasal administration of the synthetic 19 

C3aR functional antagonist, DArg10_Aib20[48]. Once bound to C3aR, DArg10_Aib20 is biased towards β-20 

arrestin signaling showing otherwise minimal agonist effect, leading to internalization of the receptor limiting 21 

agonist mediated activation[48]. DArg10_Aib20 protected against diet-induced weight gain and hypothalamic 22 

microgliosis, an effect that recapitulates the beneficial effect of conditional C3aR deletion. This result highlights 23 

the potential of developing CNS-restricted selective C3aR antagonists[44,48,106] as a therapeutic strategy to 24 

limit hypothalamic inflammation that is associated with a beneficial metabolic effect (at least in male mice), having 25 

the potential to exert protective effects on other conditions also associated with brain gliosis such as cognitive 26 

impairments, psychiatric and neurodegenerative disease[36,107–109]. 27 

28 

C3aR is a new LAM marker, necessary for lipid-induced lipid droplet formation 29 

Mechanistically, we established for the first time a role for C3aR as a marker, and a potential key regulator of 30 

the LAM phenotype. There is a growing appreciation of the role that LAM and lipid droplet accumulation plays in 31 

microglia in metabolic and neurological diseases, as well as in aging[110–112]. APOE [113,114] and 32 

Trem2 [81,82,110] , but also PLIN1/2 , HSL , and Fabp4 are among the most common LAM markers[62,63]. Here 33 

we established C3aR as a novel marker of LAM using both snRNAseq profiling and functional validation in vivo 34 

and in vitro . Not only can C3aR be considered as a novel LAM marker, but it appears to be a determinant of the 35

C3aR by DArg10_Aib20[44], blocked the accumulation of lipid droplets, demonstrating that this receptor plays 8

LAM markers like Trem2 and Cd68 . Further sequencing studies in the context of diet-induced obesity, looking at 13

15 

Conclusion 16 

Given the detrimental impact of diet-induced hypothalamic inflammation on health[10,18,36,107,117–119], 17 

understanding the mechanisms underlying this complex phenomenon is essential. Here, we demonstrate that 18 

microglial C3aR expression parallels diet-induced hypothalamic gliosis and a LAM phenotype, and that targeted 19 

conditional ablation or pharmacological antagonism provides a protective effect. The studies presented here 20 

highlight the potential for developing and utilizing selective antagonists of C3aR as a therapeutic strategy to 21 

preserve CNS function under conditions associated with hypothalamic gliosis and diet-induced 22 

neuroinflammation, reducing the risk and incidence for associated health complications and disease. 23
