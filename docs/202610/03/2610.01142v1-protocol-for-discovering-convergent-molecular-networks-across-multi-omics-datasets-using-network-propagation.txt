Summary 

Phenotypic outcomes arise from coordinated changes across molecular layers. Although multi-omics data capture complementary molecular signals, integrating them into coherent biological mechanisms remains challenging due to limited overlap at the gene level. Network propagation maps significant gene hits from each dataset onto a prior knowledge molecular interaction network and diffuses their signals to neighboring genes. Mathematical integration of propagated networks, followed by clustering and pathway enrichment, reveals shared molecular networks, pinpointing the core biology captured across orthogonal molecular layers. 

For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Olwal et al. 1,2. 2

Graphical abstract 

Before you begin 

Integration of data, particularly omics datasets, is critical for understanding mechanistic insights and discerning the interconnectedness of molecular networks underlying health and disease. For instance, an extracellular stimulus can activate a receptor, propagate intracellular signaling through phosphorylation, activate transcription factors, induce gene expression, and ultimately alter protein abundance. Integrating multi-omics measurements is therefore essential for reconstructing the chain of molecular and mechanistic events that links a biological perturbation to a downstream phenotype, particularly given the multilayered nature of cellular signaling pathways. To this end, several approaches have been developed to integrate multi-omics datasets (reviewed in 3). Owing to the high-dimensionality, heterogeneity, and sparsity of most 3

omics datasets, 4–6 their integration remains a challenge (reviewed in 4,5 ). Statistical and computational methods, such as machine learning models, have gained traction in the integration of omics datasets as they are adapted to handling heterogeneous, high dimensional and sparse data (reviewed in 3). However, some of these methods often require resource-intensive computational infrastructure and elaborate training and often do not integrate decades of prior knowledge on molecular interactions. 

A simple computational data integration approach called “network propagation” can integrate multi-omics datasets within the context of prior knowledge molecular interaction networks. Networks are composed of nodes, often representing individual genes in the field of biology, and edges (i.e., connections), representing physical, functional, or other types of associations between genes. We refer to the prior knowledge network as the “base network”. Network propagation is anchored on an assumption that genes implicated in a certain phenotype have a higher tendency to associate with one another. 7 Network propagation is an iterative process in which genes of interest are labeled within a network (hereafter referred to as “seed genes”) 

and this signal is subsequently propagated (i.e., spread) via the edges to nearby nodes. One can imagine this process as a heat diffusion process, in which heat accumulates in heavily interconnected network neighborhoods. The final propagated scores are converted to p-values by comparing them with scores obtained from randomly labeled seed sets and reflect the network proximity of each gene to the observed seeds, enabling identification of genes that are functionally or physically connected to the seed genes. 8

In this protocol, we detail the steps involved in performing the two most common network 

propagation processes: random walk with restart (RWR; also known as insulated heat diffusion 

or personalized PageRank) and diffusion kernel (DK; also referred to as heat kernel) 8,9 .

Interestingly, many widely used search engines, including Google, were built on PageRank-

based strategies, 10,11 highlighting the power of network propagation approaches to connect and 

prioritize information across interconnected systems. Both methods work by spreading signal 

outward from the seed genes across the network and scoring every gene by how much signal 

reaches it, so that genes sitting close to many seeds rank highly even if they were not members 

of the seed genes. The two differ in how the signal fades with distance. RWR models a walker 

that steps between connected genes but periodically jumps back to the seeds, with a restart 

probability α that sets how far it strays; the resulting signal spread falls off steadily with distance, 

with the greatest signal accumulating within the neighbors of the seeds. 

Network propagation assigns scores to genes by spreading signal from a set of seed genes 

through an interaction network. Let  𝐴  denote the adjacency matrix,  𝐷  the diagonal matrix of 

node degrees, and  𝑝 0 the vector encoding the initial seed signal. Both Random Walk with 

Restart (RWR) and the Diffusion Kernel (DK) can be expressed as  𝑝 = 𝑆𝑝 0, where the 

propagation operator  𝑆  determines how signal spreads and decays across the network. For 

RWR,  𝑊 = 𝐴𝐷 −1  is the normalized adjacency matrix with 

𝑆 = 𝛼[𝐼 − (1 − 𝛼)𝑊] −1 ,

where the restart probability  𝛼  controls how strongly propagation remains anchored to the 

seeds. DK instead models heat diffusing from the seeds for a time  𝑡 , using the graph Laplacian 

(L=D-A), with 

𝑆 = 𝑒 −𝑡𝐿 

Thus, while RWR emphasizes progressively longer network paths according to (1 − 𝛼) , DK 

controls the spatial scale of propagation through  𝑡 , with diffusion allowing signal to reach genes 

beyond the immediate neighborhood. Importantly, in both methods  𝑆  depends only on the 

network, not on the input seed genes, allowing it to be computed once and efficiently applied 4

to multiple datasets. Finally, propagation scores can be calibrated against scores from random 

seed sets of equal size to obtain gene-specific empirical  𝑝 -values, identifying genes that receive 

more signal than expected given their network position. 

After propagation of each dataset individually across the base network, our protocol outlines 

how to integrate each propagation and identify converging gene sets. We additionally review 

how to identify pathway layers, or subnetworks, within the larger converging network, using 

gene set overrepresentation analysis (GSOA). This framework is broadly applicable for 

identifying shared mechanisms across two or more gene sets, regardless of their experimental 

source or the technology used to generate them. 

Innovation 

The advent of high-throughput techniques has led to multi-modal datasets that are often complex, heterogeneous, and sparse. Integration of these datasets is often needed to gain mechanistic insights. Several approaches, including statistical and computational methods, have been adopted for integration of multi-modal data from single or multiple independent studies. However, many of these approaches are limited by high computational demands or the need for large amounts of data. 3 Here, we review methods for employing network propagation, a simple, robust, and computationally efficient approach for integrating diverse datasets. Network propagation can integrate two or more distinct gene lists regardless of their source, measurement technique, or sample size. By propagating each dataset across the same prior-knowledge network, heterogeneous datasets are projected into a common network space, providing a standardized representation that facilitates direct comparison and integration across molecular data types. The protocol is specific for human genes in HUGO gene nomenclature but can be easily adapted to any organism, provided that the base network and seed gene lists use a common gene nomenclature.

Expected outcomes 

Network propagation expands a set of seed genes through a molecular interaction network to identify genes in their network neighborhood and molecular pathways that may not be apparent from the original gene set alone. 

Integration of datasets using network propagation is particularly useful when two or more gene sets have limited overlap at the gene level (Figure 1A) but may converge at the molecular pathway level. Before launching network propagation, users should assess (i) the extent of gene-level overlap between the seed sets and (ii) whether the overlapping genes form a network suitable for clustering and functional enrichment. When gene-level overlap is limited, network propagation can identify additional genes that connect the seed sets within the interaction network. 

Network propagation can also be used when mechanistic insights cannot be obtained from a single gene set. In such cases, a gene set of interest can be integrated with relevant gene sets derived from external resources, such as cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics (https://www.cbioportal.org/ ), DisGeNET ( https://disgenet.com/ ), the Catalogue of Somatic 

Mutations in Cancer (COSMIC;  https://www.sanger.ac.uk/tool/cosmic/ ), or genome-wide 

association studies, to identify molecular pathways potentially shared between the datasets.

As demonstrated in this protocol, integration of distinct gene sets can generate propagated and integrated networks, clustered subnetworks, and functional enrichment results highlighting molecular pathways or biological processes shared by the datasets of interest (Figures 1 –3, Table S1 –S3). The propagation results also show the relative yield of significant seed genes and newly identified genes, providing an indication of the extent of network expansion (Figure 1B –C). The full and clustered networks are generated side by side (Figure 2), and the functional enrichment map/heatmap is generated for the clustered networks (Figure 3). The network edge list and node table are additionally saved in the Tables directory and can be exported for visualization in Cytoscape. Limitations 

Although the network propagation processes described in this protocol are robust and can give mechanistic insights, these processes possess some limitations. First, due to the extent to 18 

which the signal from the seed genes is spread across a base network, the molecular pathways or biological processes resulting from the two network propagation processes may vary. Due to the level of connections between nodes in different base networks, some variations may be seen between different base networks. Identifying terms that converge across network propagation processes and/or base networks could strengthen the findings. Secondly, network propagation analysis produces several significant molecular pathways or biological process terms making it challenging to select the most relevant term. Thus, the choice of the molecular pathway(s) subjected to follow up wet lab validation may be subjective. It may be useful to rank the terms and identify at least the top three for downstream validation. Ranking subnetworks using some quantitative information (e.g., mutation rates, protein abundance, gene expression etc.) from experimental studies or databases could minimize subjectivity, as done in our prior work. 1 Third, this approach is limited to measurement technologies whose outputs can be mapped onto the entities represented in the underlying network, most commonly genes or proteins. Thus, datasets that cannot be readily linked to these entities (e.g., lipids or metabolites, unless they are somehow connected to regulatory enzymes) may be more difficult to integrate using this framework. Lastly, the choice of a base network for a set of seed genes may be challenging. It is advisable to understand the properties of the base networks and the goal of the study to guide in selection of the base network. For example, a study that is more interested in physical or functional interactions should choose a base network that is well known to have the type of interactions of interest (Table 2). 

Troubleshooting 

Problem 1: Users encounter a sparse-to-dense coercion warning or error when calculating the inverse or Laplacian matrix. Related to Troubleshoot 1. 

Users may see the following warning or error during network propagation: 

Warning: asMethod(object) : sparse->dense coercion: allocating vector of size 3.0 GiB when calculating the inverse or Laplacian matrix. 

Error: asMethod(object) : sparse->dense coercion: allocating vector of size 3.0 GiB when calculating the inverse or Laplacian matrix. 

Potential solution: 

The warning indicates that R is attempting to convert a sparse matrix to a dense matrix and may require substantial memory. If the message appears as a warning and the analysis continues, no action is required. If the operation terminates with an error, increase the memory limit available to R and restart R before rerunning the analysis. For example: Sys.setenv(R_MAX_VSIZE = "100Gb") 

The value should be adjusted according to the available system memory. 

Problem 2: Users encounter an HTTP request or Ensembl connection error while mapping network identifiers. Related to Troubleshoot 2. 

Users may encounter errors such as: 

Error in `req_perform()`: 

! Failed to perform HTTP request. 

Caused by error in `curl::curl_fetch_memory()`: 

! Could not resolve hostname [www.ensembl.org] 

or: 

Error in httr2::req_perform(): 

! HTTP 405 Method Not Allowed. 19 

Potential solution: 

These errors generally occur when the connection to the Ensembl server is unavailable or unstable. Ensure that the computer has a stable internet connection and rerun the affected step. If the error persists, retry the analysis later. 

Problem 3: Users encounter timeout or incomplete-download errors when retrieving Ensembl or STRING network files. Related to Troubleshoot 3. 

Users may encounter errors such as: 

Error in `httr2::req_perform()`: 

! Failed to perform HTTP request. 

Caused by error in `curl::curl_fetch_memory()`: 

! Timeout was reached 

or: 

Warning in download.file(...): 

downloaded length ... != reported length ... 

Error in download.file(...): 

download ... failed 

Potential solution: 

These errors may occur when downloading large network files over a slow or unstable internet connection. Increase the R download timeout before rerunning the affected Analysis.R: 

options(timeout = 600) 

The timeout can be increased further if necessary. 

Problem 4: Figure generation fails or figures are not displayed/saved. Related to Troubleshoot 4. 

Potential solution: 

Check that the required plotting packages are installed, that save_figures and preview_figures are set as intended, and that the results directory is writable. If a figure is too large or crowded, increase the corresponding width and height parameters in generate_figures() and rerun the figure-generation step. 

Problem 5. User encounter an error related the Figures. Related to Troubleshoot 4. 

Error in grDevices::pdf(file.path(fig_dir, filename), width = width, height = height, : cannot open file 'results/Figures/RWR/RWR_PathwayCommons_Figure_xx.pdf' Called from: grDevices::pdf(file.path(fig_dir, filename), width = width, height = height, useDingbats = FALSE) 

Potential solution: 

This error may occur when one or more PDF figure files are open while Analysis.R is being run. Close any open PDF figure files and rerun the affected Analysis.R chunks.
