# From Image Latent Space to Fuzzy Rules: Interpretable Analysis of Gastrointestinal Foundation Model 

Michael D. Vasilakakis 1[0000-0003-0739-0040] and Dimitris K. Iakovidis 1[0000-0002-5027-5323]  

> 1

Department of Computer Science and Biomedical Informatics, University of Thessaly, Lamia, Greece vasilaka@uth.gr, diakovidis@uth.gr 

Abstract. Foundation models pretrained on large-scale datasets demonstrate strong transferability to medical imaging tasks. However, understanding how their latent representations encode clinically relevant information remains an open challenge in safety-critical domains. This study proposes a prototype-based fuzzy-rule framework that interprets the patch-level features produced by the in-ner layers of pretrained foundation models, without any fine-tuning. Class-spe-cific prototypes are learned by clustering in the feature space, yielding compact visual patterns. Patch features are then expressed as prototype similarities and classified by fuzzy rules with linguistic IF-THEN conditions that are human read-able. The framework is applied across the final two blocks of ViT-S/16 back-bones pretrained on ImageNet-1K and GastroNet-5M, and benchmarked against k-nearest neighbours, kernel SVM, and linear probing under identical frozen fea-tures, on wireless capsule endoscopy classification, gastrointestinal endoscopy classification, and colonic polyp segmentation. The experimental analysis shows that the proposed method, without backbone fine-tuning, reaches accuracy com-parable to these black-box classifiers, and that domain-specific pretraining yields features that are both discriminative and symbolically compressible. Because the resulting rules are extracted from real data and expressed in interpretable terms, they are further used as an instrument to investigate synthetic medical images, providing a human-readable account of which real prototypes and rules a gener-ator reproduces or fails to reproduce, localising where a synthetic image departs from real tissue rather than summarising it with a single score. The framework thus offers a transparent, depth-resolved view of how foundation models organise clinically relevant structure, together with a practical downstream use of the ex-tracted rules.

# 1 Introduction 

Foundation models have significantly advanced medical image analysis through large-scale pretraining and improved transferability across downstream tasks. In gastrointes-tinal (GI) endoscopy, models are commonly pretrained on natural image datasets such as ImageNet-1K [1], whose visual statistics differ substantially from endoscopic im-agery. Recent studies demonstrate that domain-specific pretraining on large medical datasets, such as GastroNet-5M, substantially improves downstream performance, es-pecially when combined with self-supervised learning strategies[1–3]. Despite these advances, understanding how pretrained models internally organize clinically relevant structures within their features, and how that organisation changes across network depth, remains an open challenge in safety-critical domains where transparent decision-making is essential. Deep architectures such as Vision Transformers (ViT) [4, 5] produce high-dimen-sional latent embeddings whose discriminative organization and class separability are difficult to interpret. Post hoc explanation techniques, including saliency maps and Grad-CAM[6, 7], provide localized visualizations but often fail to characterize the structural geometry of the learned representation space. Moreover, saliency-based ex-planations can be misleading, reflecting model biases rather than causal relationships between inputs and outputs [8]. Inherently interpretable approaches analyze model be-havior through structured reasoning. Prototype-based models explain predictions through similarity to representative examples, revealing class-dependent structure in latent space [8, 9]. Complementarily, fuzzy rule-based systems express classification outcomes using linguistic if-then conditions that align naturally with human reason-ing[10–12], offering a transparent alternative to black-box decision mechanisms. This study proposes a prototype-based fuzzy-rule framework that operates on the patch-level features extracted from the inner layers of pretrained foundation models, without any fine-tuning. Class-specific prototypes are learned by clustering in the fea-ture space, giving compact visual patterns. Patch features are then transformed into prototype-similarity features and classified by sparse fuzzy rules with linguistic IF-THEN conditions designed for human inspection. The aim is not to design an interpret-able classifier for accuracy alone, but to characterise what interpretable structure is al-ready present in the features produced by a given pretrained backbone, and how that structure varies across inner layers and pretraining domain. To this end the framework is swept across the final two blocks of ViT-S/16 under both ImageNet-1K and Gas-troNet-5M pretraining, and its rule-based readout is compared against standard classi-fiers, namely k-nearest neighbours, kernel SVM, and linear probing, evaluated on the same features extracted using the pre-trained models under identical conditions. The experimental analysis serves first to establish that this interpretability has no significant cost in performance. On wireless capsule endoscopy classification (KID) [13], GI endoscopy classification (Kvasir-v2)[14], and colonic polyp segmentation (Kvasir-SEG) [15], the proposed method, without backbone fine-tuning, reaches accu-racy comparable to the black-box classifiers while, unlike them, yielding an explicit and compact set of human-readable rules. Classification is examined both for each ab-normality against normal tissue and in a pooled normal-versus-abnormal setting that From Image Latent Space to Fuzzy Rules: Interpretable Analysis of Gastrointestinal Foundation Model 3

reflects a first-line screening decision. Across depths and pretraining sources, domain-specific pretraining tends to produce latent spaces that are both discriminative and sym-bolically compressible, so that fewer and shorter rules describe the same structure. Because the extracted rules are derived from real data and expressed in interpretable terms, they can be repurposed beyond classification. This study uses them as an instru-ment to investigate synthetic medical images: fitted only on real data, the rule base reads a synthetic image through the vocabulary of real tissue and reports, in human-readable terms, which real prototypes and rules a generator over-activates or fails to activate (fidelity). It names the specific prototypes and rules where a generator diverges from real tissue, an explanation that aggregate generative metrics do not provide. This investigation shows that fidelity and utility need not coincide, and it names the proto-types and rules responsible for the gap. The contributions of this study are as follows. First, it presents an interpretable pro-totype-based fuzzy-rule framework that reads the inner layers of frozen pretrained foun-dation models and turns their patch-level features into compact, human-inspectable IF-THEN rules. Second, through a depth-resolved and pretraining-resolved analysis it shows that this readout, obtained without backbone fine-tuning, is comparable in accu-racy to black-box classifiers while remaining interpretable, and that domain-specific pretraining yields representations that are simultaneously discriminative and symboli-cally compressible. Third, it illustrates a downstream use of the extracted rules as an interpretable fidelity auditor of synthetic medical images, naming in clinically mean-ingful terms the prototypes and rules a generator fails to reproduce. 

# 2 Related Work 

Prototype-based interpretable classification . Case-based prototype networks make predictions by comparing an image to learned prototypical parts. ProtoPNet [16] intro-duced the "this looks like that" paradigm; TesNet[17] structured the prototype space on a Grassmann manifold; PIP-Net[18] added self-supervised prototype alignment and a non-negative, additive head; ProtoTree [19] organized prototypes in a decision tree. These methods are typically trained end-to-end, coupling prototype quality to task-specific backbone adaptation. These methods jointly optimize the backbone alongside the prototype layer, so prototype quality and backbone adaptation cannot be disentan-gled. This study instead operates on non-fine-tuned features, so the analysis reflects the representation itself rather than a co-adapted head, and it examines how the interpreta-ble structure changes across inner layers and pretraining domain rather than at a single depth. Because a single centroid is a poor summary of a visually diverse class, several prototypes per class are retained rather than one, which matters in particular when ab-normalities are pooled into a single, heterogeneous abnormal class. For the same reason a numerical comparison against end-to-end prototype networks is not made, since it would conflate feature quality with the effect of backbone adaptation; the benchmark is instead against classifiers that share the same frozen features. 4 M. Vasilakakis and D. Iakovidis 

Post-hoc prototype interpretability . Closer to our regime, recent work extracts prototype-based explanations from already-trained models without backbone retrain-ing; In [20] the authors decompose a trained classification head into part-prototypes post-hoc while preserving the original accuracy, and interpretable-by-design ap-proaches over trained foundation features have been proposed for general representa-tions [8, 9]. In particular, the framework [8] formalizes interpretable classification over frozen foundation-model feature spaces, the setting adopted here. This study shares the same gradient-free premise but differs in explanation modality as characterizing simi-larity through linguistic fuzzy sets rather than visual heatmaps, and in scope, by sys-tematically comparing feature quality across trained layers rather than at a single depth. 

Fuzzy rule-based interpretability . Fuzzy rule systems express decisions as linguis-tic IF–THEN conditions aligned with human reasoning, and their interpretability is well studied, including cognitive bounds on rule length[21]. Such systems have primarily characterized structured/tabular attributes[10, 12]; recent work quantifies prototype similarity for vision[18]. In particular, the rule-construction mechanism used here adapts the framework[12], which was developed for structured/tabular attributes, to frozen ViT patch features. Building on this, the fuzzy rules used here operate on the graded magnitude of prototype similarity through linguistic membership functions, re-taining a notion of partial match, and are derived directly from features across trained layers rather than from a trained architecture. This graded, partial-match view also makes the rules suitable for the downstream analysis of synthetic data, where a tolerant description is preferable to a hard decision. 

Evaluating synthetic medical images . Since part of this study repurposes the ex-tracted rules to examine synthetic images, it also relates to the evaluation of generative models. Synthetic-data quality is commonly summarised by distributional distances and embedding-based precision and recall, by the separability of a real-versus-synthetic discriminator, and by downstream train-on-synthetic, test-on-real protocols, interpreta-ble set-level utility measures have recently been introduced for the medical domain[22]. These provide a single score and treat the feature space as a black box. The rule base used here is complementary, giving a per-prototype and per-rule account of where syn-thetic and real data diverge while remaining human-readable.

# 4 Experiments and results 

4.1 Experimental setup 

Three gastrointestinal benchmarks are used. Wireless capsule endoscopy classification uses Dataset 2 of the KID database, which consists of normal images together with inflammatory, vascular, and polypoid lesions, reflecting clinical class imbalance. The gastrointestinal endoscopy classification uses Kvasir-v2. The colonic polyp segmenta-tion uses Kvasir-SEG, the only task with pixel-level ground truth and therefore real 8 M. Vasilakakis and D. Iakovidis 

patch labels. Patch features are read from a ViT-S/16 encoder trained with DINO self-supervision and used without any fine-tuning, under two pretraining sources, Gas-troNet-5M (GN-5M) and ImageNet-1K (IN-1K), and at two depths, the final block (block 11) and the preceding block (block 10). Each image yields 196 patch tokens of dimension 384 on a 14×14 grid. Data are split at the image level with a stratified 40 percent test partition and results are averaged over ten random repeats. For the classifi-cation tasks the image-level label is broadcast to all patches during vocabulary fitting, whereas Kvasir-SEG uses mask-derived patch labels. Per-patch decisions are produced by the fuzzy rule of Eq. (2) and aggregated into an image label by majority vote over the 𝐺 = 196 patches, an image being labelled ab-normal when at least half of its patches are predicted abnormal. This count-based ag-gregation of patch predictions into an image-level decision is the standard weakly-su-pervised, multiple-instance setting[23]. The proposed fuzzy framework (PF) is com-pared against three baselines that share the same pretrained ViT-S/16 features under this identical aggregation harness: a kernel SVM, a linear probe and a prototype k-nearest-neighbour classifier (kNN). Proposed Framework (PF) is evaluated over proto-types, with the number of prototypes per class swept over {5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15}. The fuzzy vocabulary uses per-class k-means prototypes with triangular membership func-tions and euclidean similarity; its number of linguistic terms L is swept over {3, 5, 7, 9} and selected by grid search on a held-out validation split, with L=3 (terms Low, Med, High) and the reduced phrase chosen for the image-level classification tasks. Classification is reported with AUC[24] and segmentation with per-patch Dice[3] at the 14x14 patch resolution.

4.3 Interpretability Analysis 

Only the fuzzy framework yields an explicit rule representation. From the real KID training split (GN-5M, block 10) the framework extracts a compact rule base of 35 rules (17 predicting Normal, 18 predicting Abnormal), several with only two conditions in their IF-part and none exceeding six, so the whole base sits within the 7±2 comprehen-sion bound. Because each prototype is displayed as its nearest real training patch, a condition such as Abnormal:High points to concrete tissue rather than an abstract index. As a soundness check, scoring the extracted rules reproduces the reported classifier decisions exactly, so the rules explain the reported numbers rather than defining a sep-arate model. Fig. 3 shows a representative rule pair from the Kvasir-SEG vocabulary, anticipated in Section 3.3, for a single patch 𝑓 &. The IF-part of each rule references prototype crops with their linguistic similarity terms, so the decision can be read both visually and in words. For the Abnormal rule, 𝑓 & is highly similar to the abnormal prototype p6 (High) and weakly similar to the normal prototype p1 (Low), yielding a positive relative cer-tainty of 0.4701 that assigns the patch to the Abnormal class; the symmetric Normal rule produces the counterpart −0.4701, so the two rules agree. 4.4 Synthetic-image audit via fuzzy rule fidelity 

The rule base extracted from the real training split, obtained without any optimiza-tion, is reused unchanged as an instrument to read synthetic images. Three generators are audited, StyleGAN[25], TiDE[26], and TiDE II[27]. For each synthetic image the same pipeline is applied as for real images, the same reading illustrated in Fig. 3, map-ping its patches to prototype similarities, then to dominant linguistic terms, and then to the IF-THEN rules, and the per-patch rule-firing and prototype-activation frequencies are averaged over a set of sampled synthetic images and compared against those of a real reference set of the same class. Each table reports two such frequencies: rule-firing frequency, the fraction of an image's 196 patches on which a given abnormal rule fires, and prototype-activation frequency, the fraction of patches whose nearest lesion-class prototype is a given prototype. # 5 Conclusion 

This study presented an interpretable prototype-rule framework that reads the inner lay-ers of a pretrained foundation model and turns their patch-level features into compact, human-inspectable IF-THEN rules, without any fine-tuning of the backbone. Proto-types are learned by clustering real patch features, their similarities are described by linguistic terms, and the resulting rules classify each patch while remaining short enough to fall within the 7±2 comprehension budget, with each condition grounded in a concrete real-tissue crop. Across wireless capsule endoscopy classification, gastrointestinal endoscopy classi-fication, and polyp segmentation, evaluated on ViT-S/16 features under both ImageNet-1K and GastroNet-5M pretraining and at two depths, the experimental analysis showed that this interpretability is achieved with no significant cost in the classification accu-racy. The rule-based readout, obtained without backbone fine-tuning, is comparable to the trained SVM and linear probes, trailing them by a few AUC points on classification and by about two Dice points on the domain backbone for segmentation, and it is ahead of the k-nearest-neighbour. Two consistent patterns emerged from the depth- and pre-training-resolved study: domain-specific pretraining produces latent spaces that are both discriminative and amenable to prototype-based reasoning, and the penultimate block matches or slightly exceeds the final block, indicating that clinically relevant structure is not confined to the deepest representation. Because the reduced phrase fixes the rule length independently of the number of prototypes, accuracy can be improved by adding prototypes without lengthening the explanation. Beyond classification, the study illustrated that the same rule base, fit only on real data, can be turned on synthetic images to give a rule-level, human-readable account of how a generator's images depart from real tissue, naming the specific prototypes and rules that a synthetic image fails to reproduce. The worked examples showed the in-strument pointing to a concrete, localised failure in one case and to close agreement in another, which suggests that interpretable rules can serve not only to explain a classifier but also to inspect generated data in clinically meaningful terms. Future work will extend the framework along several directions. A natural next step is to evaluate the extracted rules with clinicians, assessing how readily the prototype and rule explanations are understood and used in practice. The synthetic-image audit can be broadened from the illustrative single-image cases shown here to an aggregate study across many generated images and additional generators, turning the rule-level reading into a systematic fidelity instrument.
