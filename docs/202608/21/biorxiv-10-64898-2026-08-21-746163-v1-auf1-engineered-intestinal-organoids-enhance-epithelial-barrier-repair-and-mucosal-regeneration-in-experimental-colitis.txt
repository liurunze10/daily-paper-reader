Abstract 12 

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) often involves disrupted intestinal epithelial barrier, but therapies 13 

specifically targeting this barrier are limited. We found that downregulated AUF1 (HNRNPD) 14 

contributes to defective barrier integrity in ulcerative colitis (UC). Compared to controls, its 15 

expression level was decreased and inversely correlated with clinical severity. Knocking down AUF1 16 

in human and mouse colonic organoids led to impaired barrier function, with reduced Occludin and 17 

upregulated Claudin-2, mimicking characteristic IBD-associated mucosal alterations. Distinct RNA-18 

binding activity of AUF1 protein isoforms contributed to these changes: p37 stabilized Occludin 19 

mRNA and blocked microRNA-122/Ago2-mediated repression, whereas p40 promoted Claudin-2 20 

mRNA degradation via ubiquitin-proteasome pathway. Restoring AUF1 expression in organoids 21 

enhanced epithelial properties and, when transplanted into mice with established colitis, accelerated 22 

mucosal healing and epithelial regeneration in recipient mice and decreased fibrosis. Our study 23 

unravelled a post-transcriptional mechanism important for intestinal homeostasis and demonstrated a 24 

concept of using engineered organoids for treating IBD .25

Introduction 27 

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), encompassing Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, is 28 

characterized by chronic relapsing inflammation that progressively erodes epithelial barrier integrity 29 

and compromises mucosal homeostasis [1]. Although many therapies are currently targeting immune 30 

processes, a significant number of patients cannot achieve long-lasting remission, and there is a great 31 

need to develop approaches that directly restore epithelial integrity. Indeed, mucosal healing, which 32 

refers to the complete recovery of the epithelium, is recognized as a key player in long-term outcome 33 

in IBD [2]. 34

Organoids represent a promising regenerative medicine approach for the treatment of IBD. This 35 

therapy is based on the fact that intestinal organoids can be isolated from stem cells, expanded in 36 

vitro, and then used for transplantation. Although there is preliminary evidence that the transplantation 37 

of organoids can induce epithelial repair, organoids often fail to demonstrate long-lasting 38 

efficacy, and it is unclear how their function can be optimized to achieve durable restoration of the 39 

epithelium. 40 

The fact is that the intestinal epithelium itself regulates mucosal immunity and integrity, which means 41 

promoting its regeneration could have a significant impact on the outcome of IBD [3]. Tight junctions 42 

(TJ) are protein complexes located at the apical surface of intestinal epithelial cells that regulate 43 

the paracellular permeability barrier [4]. Epithelial cell death, TJ disruption, 44 

and subsequent loss of barrier integrity are recognized as critical events in the pathogenesis of IBD 45 

[5]. Specifically, pro-inflammatory mediators, which are increased in IBD, can cause TJ 46 

instability, epithelial cell death, and even ulceration [6]. 47 

A variety of transmembrane proteins constitute tight junctions (TJs) together with occludin, tricellulin, 48 

different claudins, and junctional adhesion molecules (JAMs) [7]. As an essential component of the 49 

tight junction strand, occludin plays a significant role in regulating the permeability of epithelia and 50 

endothelia [8]. Being a part of TJs, the protein helps maintain barrier functions, contributing to the 51 

regulation of paracellular permeability. What is more, Claudin-2 is reported to be responsible for the 52 

formation of cation-selective and large-conductance aquaporin water channels in “leaky” epithelia, 53 

which are characteristic of the kidney, intestine, and other body parts [9]. The decrease in occludin 54 

with the concomitant increase in Claudin-2 expression was found to be associated with the onset and 55 

progression of IBD [10] ; [11]. 56 

A substantial cohort of TJ proteins are encoded by mRNAs harboring AU-rich elements (AREs) 57 

within their 3 ′ untranslated regions (3’ UTR). These motifs, via interplay with ARE-binding proteins, 58 

determines both the stability and translational fate of their transcripts [12]. One such effector is 59 

heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein D (HNRNPD), more commonly known as AU-rich 60 

element/poly(U)-binding degradation factor 1 (AUF1). AUF1 is important in modulating ARE-mRNA 61 

stability [12]. Through alternative splicing, the AUF1 transcript yields four isoforms—p37, p40, p42, 62 

and p45—each endowed with RNA-binding property, albeit with divergent affinities [12]. 63 

Historically, AUF1 has been canonized as a destabilizer of mRNAs through the assembly of 64 

ribonucleoprotein complexes [12]. Intriguingly, emerging investigations delineate a stabilizing 65 

capacity of AUF1 toward select transcripts [13], underscoring its dualistic and enigmatic 66 

comportment, which remains largely unrevealed. 67 

Our prior inquiries revealed that ablation of AUF1 causes colitis in murine models, accentuating its 68 

physiological gravitas. Nonetheless, the precise molecular underpinnings by which AUF1 dictates 69 

epithelial barrier function remain elusive. This lacuna impels the pressing interrogation of whether 70 

AUF1 downregulation directly engenders barrier dysfunction within the intestinal epithelium. 71

In this study, we uncover a crucial regenerative function of AUF1 in safeguarding epithelial 72 

homeostasis and mucosal integrity. Silencing AUF1 using cell-penetrating morpholinos disrupted 73 

epithelial barrier function in both murine and human colonic organoids, closely mirroring the 74 

pathological features of colitis. Mechanistically, AUF1 binds to the mRNAs of the tight junction 75 

proteins Occludin and Claudin-2, stabilizing the former while facilitating the degradation of the latter, 76 

thereby fine-tuning epithelial permeability. Remarkably, transplantation of AUF1-expressing 77 

organoids into colitic mice not only restored epithelial structure and barrier function but also 78 

conferred long-lasting protection against mucosal inflammation. In contrast, transplantation of 79 

unmodified organoids offered only transient relief. These findings underscore the therapeutic 80 

superiority of AUF1-reconstituted organoid transplantation as a regenerative intervention capable of 81 

durable epithelial restoration and sustained remission in inflammatory bowel disease. 82

Results 84 

AUF1 suppression correlates with compromised intestinal epithelial barrier integrity in 85 

inflammatory bowel disease 86 

To identify key drivers of epithelial barrier dysfunction in inflammatory bowel disease, we first 87 

examined the expression of tight junction (TJ) genes in ulcerative colitis (UC) patients and healthy 88 

controls. Transcriptomic analysis revealed the most pronounced changes in two TJ components, 89 

OCLN and CLDN2 : compared with healthy controls (n = 4), UC patients (n = 5) exhibited reduced 90 

OCLN and increased CLDN2 expression ( Fig. 1A ). These findings were independently validated by 91 

quantitative qRT-PCR in a larger cohort (10 UC patients and 8 healthy controls; Fig. 1B, C ). 92 

Consistent with the transcriptional changes, immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis showed reduced 93 

Occludin and increased Claudin-2 protein levels in the epithelial layer of colonic biopsies of control 94 

and IBD patients ( Fig. 1D, E ). 95 

Most TJ transcripts, including Occludin and claudin 2 , contain AU-rich elements (AREs) within their 96 

3′ untranslated regions, which serve as binding sites for RNA-binding proteins that regulate transcript 97 

stability and turnover. We therefore examined the expression of ARE-binding proteins and found that 98 

the most significantly downregulated gene in UC was HNRNPD , which encodes the RNA-binding 99 

protein AUF1. Reduced AUF1 expression was validated at the mRNA level in an independent cohort 100 

of 21 UC patients and 9 healthy controls (p < 0.0001; Fig. 1F ). Importantly, AUF1 expression 101 

exhibited a strong inverse correlation with disease severity (Spearman’s ρ = −0.8892, P = 0.0182), 102 

indicating that progressive loss of AUF1 is closely associated with epithelial barrier disruption during 103 

intestinal inflammation ( Fig. 1G ). Together, these findings associate with reduced and enhanced 104 

expression of the TJ protein, Occludin and claudin 2 respectively and downregulation of the post-105 

transcriptional regulator AUF1, suggesting a potential link between altered RNA stability and TJ 106 

disruption in UC. 107

Dysfunction in patient derived organoid 109 

To determine whether AUF1 contributes to increased epithelial permeability in the colon, we 110 

established colonic organoids from colorectal biopsies obtained from controls and IBD patients. 111 

Biopsy-derived tissues were propagated for 10 days to allow organoid formation. Organoids derived 112 

from controls were subsequently treated for 48 h with either a cell-penetrating morpholino targeting 113 

AUF1 (AUF1-MO) or a scrambled morpholino (S-MO). Treatment with 25 nM (0.025 μM) AUF1-114 

MO did not affect organoid viability ( Fig. S1A, B ) but resulted in efficient AUF1 knockdown ( Fig. 115 

S1C ). 116 

We next assessed AUF1 protein expression by immunofluorescence in S-MO-treated control 117 

organoids (S-MO organoid), AUF1-MO-treated organoids (AUF1-MO organoid), and organoids 118 

derived from IBD biopsies (IBD-organoid). AUF1 expression was markedly reduced in IBD-119 

organoids compared with S-MO- organoids. As expected, AUF1-MO organoid showed reduced 120 

AUF1 expression relative to S-MO organoid indicating optimal knockdown ( Fig. 2A, B ). To assess 121 

epithelial barrier integrity, organoids were exposed to FITC-dextran (4 kDa; FITC-D4) 72 h after 122 

morpholino treatment. In S-MO- organoids, FITC-D4 fluorescence was predominantly restricted to 123 

the extraluminal compartment, resulting in a low intraluminal-to-extraluminal (IN/OUT) fluorescence 124 

ratio. In contrast, both AUF1-MO-organoids and IBD- organoids showed increased accumulation of 125 

FITC-D4 within the lumen, reflected by a significantly higher IN/OUT fluorescence ratio ( Fig. 2C, 126 

D). 127

Polysome profiling and translational status of Occludin and Claudin 2 mRNA 159 

To interrogate the translational regulation of gene expression, polysome profiling was employed to 160 

evaluate the ribosomal engagement of Occludin and Claudin-2 mRNAs. Ribosome-associated 161 

transcripts from HT-29 cells were separated on a 0-50% continuous sucrose density gradient and 162 

analyzed by monitoring the gradient absorbance at 254 nm. This allowed to distinguish between 163 

polysomes, monosomes (80S or 40S/60S) and free RNA species. Thus, we found that in AUF1 164 

silenced cells (AUF1-MO) Occludin mRNA was enriched in lighter fractions containing mainly 165 

monosomes (80S, 40S and 60S), whereas in S-MO cells Occludin mRNA was mostly localized in 166 

polysomes indicating its active translation. In contrast to Occludin, Claudin-2 mRNA in AUF1-MO 167 

cells was enriched in heavy polysome fractions, whereas in control cells it was distributed mainly in 168 

light fractions. As an internal control, GAPDH mRNA consistently localized to polysomal fractions 169 

irrespective of AUF1 status ( Fig. S2A-E ), thereby substantiating that AUF1 specifically orchestrates 170 

the translational regulation of Occludin and Claudin-2. 171

Proteasome is involved in the decay of claudin-2 mRNA 259

isoforms [18]. To assess whether such a mechanism underlies Claudin-2 decay, we overexpressed 262 

AUF1p37 or AUF1p40 in HT-29 cells and subjected them to treatment with or without the pan-263 

proteasome inhibitor MG132. As anticipated, Claudin-2 expression was markedly reduced by 264

Generation and characterization of AUF1-engineered colonic organoids for regenerative 290 

therapy 291 

Having established the role of AUF1 in maintaining epithelial barrier integrity by fine-tuning 292 

Occludin and Claudin-2, we asked whether restoring AUF1 expression could be beneficial in terms of 293 

epithelial repair. To this end, we generated colonic organoids from control and DSS-treated mice and 294 

engineered them to express EGFP-tagged AUF1p37 and p40. Plasmids encoding EGFP-AUF1p37 and 295 

EGFP-AUF1p40 protein were transfected into the organoids, and the stable expression of EGFP-296 

AUF1 was validated 20 days after transfection. We first evaluated the phenotype of AUF1-engineered 297 

organoids derived from healthy control mice. These organoids exhibited inherent AUF1 isoforms at 298 

baseline, and the introduction of EGFP-AUF1p37 and EGFP-AUF1p40 led to a significant elevation 299 

in AUF1 levels. Notably, AUF1 overexpression in control-derived organoids was accompanied by 300 

increased Ki67 staining, indicative of enhanced proliferative activity (Fig S4) . Because excessive 301 

proliferation could represent an undesirable property for a cell-based therapeutic product, we next 302 

evaluated AUF1-engineered organoids generated from DSS-injured colonic tissue. 303 

Organoids derived from DSS-injured mice similarly showed robust expression of EGFP-AUF1p37 304 

and EGFP-AUF1p40 following transfection, but did not exhibit the increase in Ki67-positive cells 305 

observed in AUF1-engineered control-derived organoids (Fig. S4) . Thus, although AUF1 expression 306 

was effectively increased in both organoid populations, organoids derived from DSS-injured tissue 307 

displayed a more favourable functional phenotype for transplantation, with restoration of epithelial 308 

characteristics without evidence of excessive proliferation. We therefore selected AUF1-engineered 309 

organoids derived from DSS-injured tissue for subsequent regenerative transplantation experiments. 310

We next evaluated whether transplantation of AUF1-engineered organoids could enhance tissue repair 313 

in vivo. AUF1 p37- and p40-expressing organoids derived from DSS-injured colons were transplanted 314 

by rectal delivery into mice with DSS-induced colitis (Fig. 6A). This approach was designed to 315 

determine whether restoring the AUF1-dependent epithelial program within transplanted cells could 316 

improve the regenerative capacity of organoid therapy in an inflamed and structurally damaged colon. 317 

Following transplantation, in contrast to control EGFP-expressing organoids, AUF1-engineered 318 

organoids were associated with more extensive restoration of colonic architecture. Mice receiving 319 

AUF1-engineered organoids showed greater recovery of colon length, a macroscopic indicator of 320 

disease-associated tissue injury and recovery, compared with animals receiving control EGFP-321 

organoids (Fig. 6B, C) . Histological analysis provided further evidence of enhanced mucosal repair 322 

(Fig. 6D, E) . Consistent with these results, AUF1-engineered organoids engrafted within areas of 323 

epithelial injury and were detected within damaged colonic crypts. The transplanted organoids 324 

progressively expanded during the recovery phase, consistent with their persistence and integration 325 

into the regenerating epithelium ( Fig. 6F-H )326 

Additionally, AUF1-engineered organoid transplantation promoted restoration of crypt architecture 327 

and epithelial organization, with a corresponding increase in Ki67-positive epithelial cells, indicative 328 

of enhanced epithelial proliferative activity. Recovery of apical Ezrin expression further supported 329 

restoration of epithelial polarity and differentiation (Fig. 6I-K) .330 

Because persistent epithelial injury can promote pathological tissue remodelling, we next examined 331 

markers of fibrosis. AUF1-engineered organoid transplantation was associated with reduced 332 

accumulation of α-smooth muscle actin ( α-SMA) and collagen III within the injured colon (Fig. S5A– 333 

C). These changes suggest that restoration of AUF1-dependent epithelial function not only promotes 334 

epithelial regeneration but may also limit the pathological remodelling that accompanies unresolved 335 

mucosal injury. 336 

Together, these findings demonstrate that engineering intestinal organoids to restore AUF1 enhances 337 

their regenerative activity following transplantation into the injured colon. The improved epithelial 338 

architecture, proliferative response, epithelial polarity, and reduced fibrotic remodelling observed after 339 

AUF1-engineered organoid transplantation support the concept that restoring a disease-relevant 340 

epithelial regulatory program can improve the functional outcome of organoid-based therapy in 341 

experimental colitis. 342 

Discussion 343 

Our findings identify AUF1 as a post-transcriptional regulator of intestinal epithelial barrier integrity 344 

and provide a mechanistic rationale for restoring AUF1 activity as part of a regenerative approach to 345 

IBD. Across human tissue, primary colonic organoids, epithelial monolayers, and experimental colitis, 346

Claudin-2—and with impaired barrier function. AUF1 has previously been implicated in coordinated 348 

regulation of gene expression programs in multiple physiological contexts [12]; [19], and our findings 349

antagonizing miR-122–RISC engagement, whereas p40 promoted Claudin 2 mRNA decay through a 352

The translational value of these findings was validated by the concordance between human and model 354 

systems. Reduced epithelial AUF1 was observed in UC tissue and inversely correlated with the 355 

UCEIS. AUF1 reduction recapitulated the barrier dysfunction phenotype in human organoids similar 356 

to those derived from IBD patients. Our decision to focus on Occludin and Claudin-2 was based on 357 

their established and opposing roles in regulating epithelial permeability, with the former promoting 358 

barrier integrity [20] and the latter associated with enhanced cation selectivity and increased 359 

expression in inflamed intestine [9]; [21]. Consistent with previous observations in IBD, reduced 360 

Occludin and increased Claudin-2 were associated with disease activity [10]; [11]. These observations 361 

support a model in which loss of AUF1 contributes to, rather than merely reflects, epithelial 362 

dysfunction. Nevertheless, the human data are associative, and longitudinal studies will be required to 363 

determine whether AUF1 loss precedes disease activity or is predominantly induced by the 364 

inflammatory environment. 365 

Our data further establish that AUF1 is not a uniform regulator of ARE-containing transcripts. Instead, 366 

its effects depend on isoform, target RNA, and regulatory context. AUF1 exists as four alternatively 367 

spliced isoforms—p37, p40, p42, and p45—with distinct RNA-binding properties [12]; [22]. In our 368 

experiments, p37 preferentially associated with occludin mRNA and miR-122, limiting Ago2 369 

association and thereby preserving Occludin expression. This mechanism is consistent with previous 370 

evidence that RNA-binding proteins can compete with microRNAs for access to target transcripts 371 

[23]; [24]; [25] and with reported interactions between AUF1 and microRNA pathways [14]. RNA 372 

binding protein-dependent remodelling of RNA structure can alter accessibility of microRNA 373 

recognition sites, as described for other RNA-binding proteins [26]. 374 

In contrast, AUF1 p40 associated with Claudin 2 mRNA and promoted its turnover through a 375 

ubiquitin–proteasome–dependent pathway. AUF1 has been linked to the human exosome and to ARE-376 

mRNA decay [27], while earlier work demonstrated coupling of AUF1-dependent mRNA degradation 377 

to polyubiquitination and proteasomal activity [28]; [29]. Our findings extend this model by 378 

suggesting that AUF1 p40 can couple Claudin 2 mRNA to ubiquitin-dependent decay. The parallel 379 

effects of AUF1 depletion on Claudin 2 mRNA stability and protein abundance, together with the 380

additional TJ transcripts or other AUF1-associated microRNAs remains an important question. 388 

Our in vivo findings provide an initial test of the therapeutic implications of this mechanism. Previous 389 

work using AUF1-deficient mice showed that loss of AUF1 produces intestinal phenotypes that 390 

resemble inflammatory and metabolic perturbations [18], supporting a broader role for AUF1 in 391 

intestinal homeostasis. In our experimental colitis model, transplantation of AUF1-engineered 392 

organoids enhanced epithelial regeneration, improved crypt architecture, increased epithelial 393 

proliferation, restored apical Ezrin expression, and reduced fibrotic remodeling. These effects are 394 

consistent with the emerging concept that intestinal organoids can provide a regenerative source of 395 

epithelium and may complement conventional anti-inflammatory therapies. Recent preclinical and 396 

clinical efforts have highlighted both the potential and the limitations of autologous intestinal tissue or 397 

organoid transplantation [30]; [31]. 398 

The therapeutic effect of AUF1-engineered organoids is particularly relevant because organoid 399

barrier function. In our study, engineering the graft to restore AUF1 activity improved epithelial repair 401 

in experimental colitis, suggesting that functional modification of the transplanted epithelium may 402 

enhance the efficacy of regenerative cell therapy. Rectal delivery by enema also provides a route that 403 

is conceptually compatible with endoscopic administration approaches used in early organoid-404 

transplantation studies (Clinical Trial ID: jRCTb032190207). 405 

Several questions remain before translation to human therapy. The relative contribution of individual 406 

AUF1 isoforms to human intestinal regeneration requires further definition, as does the identity of the 407 

ubiquitin machinery coupling AUF1 p40 to claudin2 decay. The durability, safety, biodistribution, and 408 

engraftment of engineered organoids will also need to be evaluated in clinically relevant models. 409 

Because AUF1 participates in diverse cellular processes, including inflammation, metabolism, 410 

senescence, and oncogenic pathways [12], approaches that selectively restore its epithelial activity 411 

may be preferable to systemic manipulation. In addition, the present therapeutic experiments were 412 

performed in experimental colitis and therefore do not establish efficacy in human IBD or 413 

demonstrate that AUF1 restoration is sufficient to overcome the complex inflammatory and immune 414 

components of disease. 415

again reintroduced via drinking water for 7 days (DSS-relapse). After second phase of DSS 684 

administration, following euthanasia, the colon was carefully excised from the ileocecal junction to 685 

the anus and gently laid out without stretching. The colon length was measured using a ruler, and the 686 

measurement was recorded in centimetres (cm). Colon shortening was assessed as an indicator of 687 

DSS-induced intestinal injury. 688 

Rectal administration of AUF1-engineered organoids 689 

For organoid-based regenerative therapy, AUF1-engineered organoids were administered to mice by 690 

rectal enema as described [51] with minor modifications. Organoids were harvested from Matrigel 691 

using gentle cell dissociation reagent, washed thoroughly with sterile PBS to remove residual 692 

extracellular matrix and culture medium, and resuspended in 100 μL of sterile PBS. At Day-7 of DSS 693 

administration, DSS-treated mice were lightly anesthetized, and the organoid suspension containing 694 

approximately 10 6 organoid cells gently administered into the rectum using a flexible catheter. Control 695 

mice received an equivalent volume of PBS. After organoid transplantation, mice were subsequently 696 

monitored for disease progression and tissue regeneration. 697
